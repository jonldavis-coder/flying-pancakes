United States

Who settled the flavor

The United States does not have one national pancake. The stack on a diner plate is mostly a wheat batter raised with baking powder, and that technology is late. Under it are older corn cakes, immigrant griddles, and recipes people carried when they were forced to move. This essay follows specific cakes in the journal. It is not a claim that a region belongs to one people.

Dutch settlers in the Hudson Valley cooked buckwheat cakes. Buckwheat does well in a cool climate and a short season, which is why it also shows up in Brittany, Russia, and parts of the Northeast. The Hudson Valley buckwheat cakes are the New York stop on that trail.

German-speaking settlers in Pennsylvania, the people called Pennsylvania Dutch from Deutsch, brought potato pancakes and a fair-day funnel cake. Those are the Pennsylvania pages. The Dutch baby is a different story: an oven pancake whose American name is usually traced to Deutsch, and whose fame in the twentieth century is tied to restaurants in the West, not to a Lancaster farmhouse. The similar name is a trap.

English cooks brought the Shrove Tuesday pancake, a thin egg batter meant to clear the kitchen before Lent. American breakfast cakes grew thicker after chemical leaveners. Southern buttermilk pancakes are that later cake, soured with buttermilk. Rhode Island johnnycakes keep an older cornmeal idea. The word johnnycake is disputed, journey cake or Shawnee cake, and the page does not pick a winner.

Scots-Irish and English settlement in Appalachia lines up with buckwheat, sorghum, dried apple, and a cornmeal griddle. Appalachian buckwheat cakes and sorghum pancakes are the mountain stops. Sorghum, not maple, was the everyday sweetener in much of the upland South. German-speaking settlers also left pickling and sweet-sour habits nearby. The mountains were not an empty English kitchen. Enslaved people and later Black Appalachian cooks worked the same farms.

Corn was already the staple of Southeastern Native nations before European wheat arrived. Southeastern corn griddle cakes are a plain cornmeal cake in that family. The page does not pretend one recipe belongs to every nation, and it does not turn a ceremonial bread into a diner stack. Hopi piki is a separate food, a paper-thin corn sheet on a hot stone, and it is named as Hopi rather than folded into a generic Indian cake. Hoecakes are the Southern corn cake whose hoe-blade origin story is famous and unsettled.

Rice in the Carolina Lowcountry is tied to the skill of enslaved West Africans, who knew how to grow rice, and to the brutal plantation economy built on their labor. Lowcountry rice griddle cakes use that grain and say so. New Orleans calas are rice fritters sold for generations by Black vendors. The journal does not invent a single West African village recipe and rename it a Southern pancake. It names the labor and the dish that can actually be documented.

Scandinavian settlement in the Upper Midwest put Swedish pancakes, lefse, and aebleskiver on church-basement tables and in home kitchens. The Danish and Norwegian pages keep the European recipe and say where it landed in Minnesota, Wisconsin, Iowa, and the Dakotas. The cakes did not replace Indigenous food. They sat down beside it, sometimes on land taken by treaty or by force. The pages stay with the recipe and that history, and they do not dress it up as a harmless picnic.
