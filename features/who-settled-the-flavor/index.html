A United States route

Who settled the flavor

Regional pancakes in the United States are a settlement history you can taste. This essay stays with the cakes in the journal.

The United States does not have one pancake. It has a stack of arrivals. Corn was already a staple of Native nations from New England to the Southwest before any English griddle showed up. Wheat, buckwheat, rice, and the habit of a chemical leavener came later, with people.

In Rhode Island the johnnycake is cornmeal on a griddle. Narragansett and other Algonquian peoples of the region grew and prepared corn first. English colonists adopted cornmeal breads. The word johnnycake has more than one proposed origin, and this journal does not pretend the argument is settled. Thin cakes and thick cakes are both local, and they are different batters.

Hoe cakes are a Southern corn bread kept by enslaved African American cooks and by the Black Southern kitchens that followed. The story that they were cooked on a hoe blade is traditional. West African grain cakes cooked on a griddle are part of the wider history. The bread is documented. The single origin story is not a patent.

Appalachian buckwheat cakes belong to cool upland farms and to settlers from the British Isles and from German-speaking Pennsylvania. Sorghum, not maple, was often the syrup. Cherokee and other Native nations of those mountains grew corn long before those farms. This journal does not hand them the later settler recipes, and it does not invent a pancake to fill a gap.

Pennsylvania Dutch potato pancakes came with German-speaking immigrants. The name Dutch is Deutsch. Jewish latkes are a different community’s potato cake, tied to Hanukkah’s oil. Blintzes in New York are the filled crepe Ashkenazi immigrants brought from eastern Europe. Sharing a potato does not make the tables the same.

Acadian ployes, buckwheat cakes cooked on one side, belong to northern Maine and New Brunswick, a French-speaking people expelled from Nova Scotia in 1755. Louisiana’s calas, fried rice cakes sold by Black Creole women, are another food from that wider history of expulsion and of African rice cooking. They are not the same cake.

Swedish pancakes, lefse, and æbleskiver in the Upper Midwest are immigrant church-supper foods. Finnish pannukakku in Michigan’s Upper Peninsula is the oven pancake of mining and logging towns. The grains and the pans came with people. Wild rice in a Minnesota pancake is a modern use of manoomin, a traditional Anishinaabe food. The pancake is not claimed here as an Anishinaabe recipe.

The Dutch baby is a German oven pancake. Seattle restaurants, often Manca’s Cafe, get the credit for the American name. The Netherlands has pannenkoeken, which are a different food. Sourdough hotcakes in Alaska and California are the breakfast of a starter kept because fresh yeast was hard to hold.

Blue corn pancakes in New Mexico use a Southwestern Indigenous crop in a modern breakfast. Hopi piki, the paper-thin blue-corn bread made with culinary ash, is a different practice. This journal names it and does not imitate it. Navajo frybread is included with its history in the Long Walk and ration foods. It is not a pre-contact bread.

None of this is a ranking of who cooked first. It is a map of what the griddle was given: a Native crop, an enslaved cook’s cornmeal, a buckwheat field, a potato, a starter, a holiday rule about oil or butter. The taste changes because the people and the pantry changed. The essay is the route. The recipes are the stops.
