Alaska Sourdough Hotcakes

Mildly sour, sturdy cakes from a live starter.

20 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 cakes

History

Sourdough starters were how prospectors and settlers leavened bread in Alaska and the Yukon when fresh yeast was hard to keep. Hotcakes from that starter became a camp and roadhouse breakfast. “Sourdough” even became a nickname for an old-timer. The cake depends on a living starter. This is a home version for someone who already keeps one, not a story that every Alaska breakfast used the same recipe.

Cultural background

A spoon of baking soda is often added to the batter just before cooking. It reacts with the acid and lifts the cake faster than the starter alone would on a griddle.

Why it tastes this way

A starter tastes sour because lactic acid bacteria work alongside the yeast. That acid is the flavor people mean by sourdough, and it is also what the soda reacts with. In a cold place, a starter kept near the stove was more reliable than a cake of perishable yeast. The hotcake is a faster use of the same pot that made bread.

How it is made

Active sourdough starter is mixed with flour, water, egg, and salt, rested briefly, and finished with baking soda.

Authentic touch

Stir the soda in just before you cook. If it sits, the bubbles fade and the cakes are heavy. The griddle should be ready.

How it is used

Butter and syrup, or butter and jam. They are a trail breakfast brought indoors.

Field notes

  • You need a lively starter. A neglected jar in the back of the fridge will not lift these.
  • The soda is a griddle trick. It is not a sign the starter failed.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup active sourdough starter
  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 3/4 cup water
  • 1 large egg
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • Oil for the griddle

Steps

  1. Stir starter, flour, water, egg, sugar, and salt. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Heat the griddle.
  3. Stir in the baking soda.
  4. Cook immediately on an oiled medium griddle.
  5. Flip when the surface is set.
  6. Serve hot. The lift does not wait.

This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A purely yeast-risen batter with no soda is slower and more breadlike.
  • Milk instead of water is richer and adds dairy.

Pairings

  • butter
  • syrup
  • jam
