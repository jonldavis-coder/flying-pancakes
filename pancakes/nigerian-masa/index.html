Arriving in Kano

Nigerian masa

You come in by Plane. Nigeria · West Africa.

Pronounced MAH-sah

Mildly sour rice cakes with crisp rims and a soft, slightly sweet center.

30 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 4 · About 16 molded cakes

History

Masa is the Hausa fermented rice cake cooked in a dimpled pan. Waina is another name for it. Rice is soaked, ground, and left to sour, then spooned into oiled cups so each cake takes a round, puffed shape. In Kano and across Hausa kitchens it is breakfast and street food, eaten with sugar, honey, or a soup. This recipe is rice only, so it can be gluten-free.

Cultural background

The pan matters as much as the ferment. Traditional cups are set into a wide clay or metal pan. A home cook can use a heavy dimpled pan if the cups are well oiled. The cake is a snack you buy by the piece as well as a dish made for the house.

Why it tastes this way

Fermentation takes the raw starch edge off the rice and leaves a light sourness. A little sugar in the batter keeps it in breakfast territory. The oiled mold fries the rim while the covered center stays tender. It does not taste like a wheat pancake, because there is no wheat.

How it is made

Soak rice, grind it with water into a thick batter, and ferment it until slightly sour. Stir in salt, sugar, and a pinch of yeast if the kitchen is too cool for a wild ferment. Oil the dimples of a masa pan and cook spoonfuls, turning once, until both sides are golden.

Authentic touch

Use a dimpled pan and oil the cups. Waina and masa are the same family of cakes. Do not add wheat flour if you are keeping the gluten-free tag.

How it is used

Masa is breakfast and a street snack. Sugar or honey goes on top, or the cakes are eaten with miyan taushe, a pumpkin soup, or another Hausa soup. Honey and soup are not in the batter.

Field notes

  • Waina is another name for these Hausa rice cakes.
  • They are cooked in a dimpled pan, not poured as a wide flatbread.
  • A rice-only batter can be gluten-free if the yeast and the pan are not carrying wheat.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups raw rice (about 400 g), soaked 4 hours and drained
  • 1 1/2 cups water, for grinding
  • 1/2 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
  • Oil for the dimples

Steps

  1. Grind the drained rice with the water to a thick, slightly grainy batter. Stir in the yeast and sugar. Leave the salt out.
  2. Cover and rest the batter for 4 to 8 hours, until it looks bubbly and smells faintly sour. This is a fermented rice batter.
  3. Stir in the salt. The batter should drop from a spoon in a heavy ribbon. Thin it with a spoon of water if it stands up in peaks.
  4. Oil the cups of a dimpled masa pan and heat them until the oil shimmers. Fill each cup about two-thirds full.
  5. Cover and cook until the bottoms are golden, then turn each cake and cook the second side. Serve warm. Waina is the same cake under another name.

Gluten-free only if the rice, yeast, and pan have no wheat on them. Hot oil in the dimples can spit.

Refrigerate leftover masa and reheat in a lightly oiled pan. The rims soften in a covered box. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Waina is another name, not a different grain.
  • A wild ferment without added yeast takes longer and tastes a little sharper.
  • Wheat flour stirred in for softness removes the gluten-free claim.

Pairings

  • A sprinkle of sugar
  • Honey
  • Miyan taushe, pumpkin soup
