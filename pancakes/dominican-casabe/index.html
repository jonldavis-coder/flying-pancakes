Arriving in Santo Domingo

Dominican casabe

You come in by Boat. Dominican Republic · Caribbean.

Pronounced kah-SAH-beh

A thin, dry cassava flatbread, lightly salty, crisp at the edge and bland in a way that suits a stew.

15 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 flat rounds

History

Casabe comes from Taíno cooking on the island. People in the Dominican Republic still make it and buy it. What you find in a shop today is a processed flatbread: cassava that has already been turned into flour or into packaged rounds, then cooked until dry. This recipe uses commercial cassava flour. It does not reconstruct a pre-colonial kitchen, and it does not teach you to process raw bitter cassava at home. Wheat breads arrived later. Casabe is the older starch on this table.

Cultural background

Casabe is everyday bread next to beans, eggs, or a stew, and it keeps because it is dried on the griddle rather than left soft. Treat it as a plain cracker-bread, not as a symbol. The Indigenous origin is specific: Taíno. The modern product is a factory or home griddle food made from flour that is already safe to cook.

Why it tastes this way

Once the moisture is cooked out, cassava starch tastes mild, almost neutral, with a faint sweetness and a dry crunch. Salt is the seasoning. There is no fermentation, no wheat, and no fat required in the dough. It tastes quiet on purpose, so the beans or the stew on top can be the loud part.

How it is made

Mix commercial cassava flour, salt, and water into a stiff paste. Press very thin rounds and cook them on a dry skillet until crisp and dry.

Authentic touch

Spread the dough thinner than a tortilla and cook it longer, until it is dry and snaps. If you take it off while it is still flexible, you have made a different cake. Do not expect it to puff.

How it is used

Break a round and use it to scoop beans, avocado, or a sauced dish. Packaged casabe, already baked in large circles, is the same food if you are not cooking.

Field notes

  • Today's casabe is a processed flatbread. Naming the Taíno origin does not turn a bag of flour into a ceremony.
  • Raw bitter cassava can be poisonous. Use commercial cassava flour or packaged casabe.
  • Casabe should dry out on the griddle. A soft tortilla texture means it needs more time.

Ingredients

  • 250 g commercial cassava flour
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 180 ml water
  • Extra commercial cassava flour if the dough sticks to your hands

Steps

  1. Stir the commercial cassava flour, salt, and water into a stiff dough. Add a spoon of water only if it will not hold together.
  2. Divide into 6 pieces and press each into a very thin round between plastic sheets.
  3. Cook on a dry skillet over medium-low heat. Turn often.
  4. Keep cooking until the round is dry, spotted, and crisp at the edge. This takes longer than a wheat tortilla.
  5. Cool on a rack. Serve with beans or another savory plate, or store the dry rounds in a tin.

Raw bitter cassava can be poisonous. This recipe uses commercial cassava flour or packaged casabe and does not teach home processing of raw cassava.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A brush of oil after cooking is a modern table habit. The older shop casabe is usually dry.
  • Whole packaged rounds can be warmed in a dry pan instead of made from flour.
  • Leave the salt out only if the stew is very salty. The bread itself is plain.

Pairings

  • Habichuelas and rice
  • Avocado and a slice of cheese, which adds dairy
  • Scrambled eggs
