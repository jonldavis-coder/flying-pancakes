Japanese hotcakes

You come in by Plane. Japan · East Asia.

Pronounced JAP-uh-neez HOT-kakes

Thick, soft cafe hotcakes with a browned top, butter, and syrup, taller than a thin crepe and gentler than a souffle cake.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 3 · 6 thick hotcakes

History

Japanese hotcakes are a 20th century cafe style. Kissaten, the coffee shops that spread through Japanese cities in that century, served thick wheat cakes with butter and syrup alongside coffee. They are not an ancient dish. Baking powder lifts them. They are related to American breakfast pancakes and became their own cafe portion: tall, neat, and eaten with a fork. Souffle hotcakes, a later and much thicker trend, are a separate page.

Cultural background

A kissaten hotcake is cooked covered, in a ring or as a deep dollop, so the side stays pale and the top dries. Butter and maple syrup, or a local sugar syrup, are the usual finish. The cake is a breakfast and a dessert depending on the hour, not a savory okonomiyaki.

Why it tastes this way

Wheat, egg, and milk taste familiar and sweet, with browning only on the surfaces that touch the pan. Baking powder opens a soft crumb that syrup sinks into. Butter adds a dairy aroma the batter already started. Because the cake is thick, the center stays custardy if you pull it early, which is the cafe texture. There is no ferment and no cabbage. The flavor is a 20th century coffee-shop sweet.

How it is made

Mix a baking-powder batter with egg and milk, cook thick rounds covered on low heat, and serve them with butter and syrup.

Authentic touch

Cook on low heat under a lid. A hot pan browns the bottom before the center sets. Give a thick cake several quiet minutes.

How it is used

Serve two or three to a plate while hot, with butter melting on top. Coffee is the kissaten partner.

Field notes

  • These are 20th century kissaten hotcakes, not an ancient dish.
  • They are thick cafe pancakes. The very tall souffle kind is a later page.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose wheat flour
  • 2 teaspoons baking powder
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1 egg
  • 3/4 cup milk
  • 2 tablespoons melted butter, plus more to serve
  • A pinch of salt
  • Syrup to serve

Steps

  1. Whisk flour, baking powder, sugar, and salt. In another bowl mix egg, milk, and melted butter.
  2. Stir the wet ingredients into the dry until just combined. A few lumps are fine.
  3. Heat a pan on low. Butter it. Pour tall rounds, or pour into buttered rings.
  4. Cover and cook until the tops are full of bubbles and the bottoms are brown. Flip and cover again until the centers set.
  5. Serve with butter and syrup.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A ring mold for straight sides
  • A little lemon zest in the batter

Pairings

  • Butter
  • Maple or sugar syrup
  • Coffee
