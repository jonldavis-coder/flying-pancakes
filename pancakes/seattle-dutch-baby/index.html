A puffed oven pancake, crisp at the rim, eggy in the center.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · one 10-inch pancake

History

The Dutch baby is a German oven pancake. The “Dutch” in the name is generally explained as a muddle of Deutsch, not a recipe from the Netherlands. In Seattle, Manca’s Cafe is often credited with serving it in the early twentieth century and with the name that stuck in American restaurants. David Eyre’s pancake, a related skillet pancake published later, is a cousin. The Netherlands has its own pannenkoeken, which are a different food.

Cultural background

It is baked in a hot buttered skillet and brought to the table puffed, then collapses. Lemon and powdered sugar are the usual finish. Fruit is a fair variation.

Why it tastes this way

Steam from a hot pan and a wet, eggy batter inflates the rim before the center sets. That is why it looks like a popover the size of the skillet. Butter browns the bottom. Lemon is there because the cake is rich and needs acid. The Seattle story is immigration and restaurants, not a medieval Dutch guild.

How it is made

A blender batter of egg, milk, and flour is poured into a preheated buttered skillet and baked until risen.

Authentic touch

The skillet and the oven must be hot before the batter goes in. A cold pan makes a flat custard. Serve it as soon as it leaves the oven if you want anyone to see the puff.

How it is used

Lemon juice and powdered sugar, or fruit.

Field notes

  • Dutch here is widely understood as a twist on Deutsch. The cake is German in origin.
  • Manca’s Cafe in Seattle is the restaurant usually credited with the American name. Treat that credit as the common account.

Ingredients

  • 3 large eggs
  • 2/3 cup whole milk
  • 2/3 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 3 tablespoons butter
  • Lemon and powdered sugar, to serve

Steps

  1. Heat the oven to 425°F with a 10-inch ovenproof skillet inside.
  2. Blend eggs, milk, flour, sugar, and salt until smooth.
  3. Add the butter to the hot skillet and let it melt.
  4. Pour in the batter and bake 16 to 20 minutes, until puffed and browned.
  5. Do not open the door early.
  6. Dust with sugar and lemon and serve at once.

This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A savory baby skips the sugar and is finished with herbs and cheese.
  • David Eyre’s version is the same family, often in a smaller skillet.

Pairings

  • lemon
  • powdered sugar
  • berries
