Chilean sopaipillas

You come in by Train. Chile · South America.

Pronounced soh-pai-PEE-yahs

Fried pumpkin rounds, crisp and puffed, eaten with pebre or with chancaca syrup.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · 12 sopaipillas

History

Chilean sopaipillas are pumpkin fried flat cakes, the food people make in the central valley when it rains. The dough is mashed zapallo, winter squash, and wheat flour, rolled thin and fried. They are fried. A crepe filled with manjar is a different page. Sopaipillas are eaten two ways on the same day: with pebre, the herb and chile relish, or with chancaca syrup, unrefined cane sugar simmered with orange peel and cinnamon. This recipe includes both finishes. The pebre has a mild chile, so the heat is mild.

Cultural background

Rain in Talca, Santiago, and the towns of the central valley is the usual excuse to fry a batch. They are street food in winter and home food the same afternoon. The pumpkin keeps the dough soft. The fry makes them puff.

Why it tastes this way

Pumpkin sweetens the wheat from the inside, and frying browns that sugar at the edge. The center stays a little bready. Pebre is sharp: cilantro, onion, tomato, vinegar, and a small mild chile. Chancaca syrup is dark and spicy from cinnamon and orange, not from chile. One batch of fritters can go either direction. The cake itself has no dairy and no egg.

How it is made

Mix mashed pumpkin with wheat flour, salt, and a little oil. Roll thin rounds and fry them. Serve with pebre, with chancaca syrup, or with both.

Authentic touch

Mash the pumpkin until it is dryish, not watery, or the dough will drink oil. Roll the rounds thin. Fry in enough oil that they bob and puff. Drain them before the syrup, or the chancaca cannot cling.

How it is used

Eat them hot, the day they are fried, especially when the weather is wet. Pebre makes them a snack with a drink. Chancaca makes them dessert. Both are traditional.

Field notes

  • Sopaipillas are fried. Rainy afternoons in central Chile are when many kitchens make them.
  • Pebre is the savory relish. Chancaca syrup is the sweet one. The fritter can take either.
  • Zapallo, winter squash, is the vegetable in the dough, not a potato.

Ingredients

  • 250 g cooked, well-drained pumpkin puree
  • 250 g wheat flour, plus more for rolling
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil in the dough, plus oil for frying
  • 1 small mild chile, chopped, for the pebre
  • 1/2 cup chopped cilantro, 1/4 onion, 1 tomato, 1 tablespoon vinegar, and a pinch of salt for the pebre
  • 150 g chancaca or panela, 200 ml water, 1 cinnamon stick, and a strip of orange peel for the syrup

Steps

  1. Stir the pumpkin, flour, salt, and 1 tablespoon oil into a soft dough. Rest 10 minutes. Add flour only if it sticks badly.
  2. Chop the chile, cilantro, onion, and tomato. Stir in the vinegar and a pinch of salt. That is the pebre.
  3. Simmer the chancaca, water, cinnamon, and orange peel until the sugar dissolves and the syrup thickens slightly.
  4. Roll the dough thin and cut 12 rounds. Fry in hot oil, turning once, until they puff and turn deep gold.
  5. Drain and serve with pebre, with warm chancaca syrup, or with both.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Some cooks add a pinch of baking powder so the rounds puff higher. This dough relies on moisture and a hot fry.
  • Lard in the dough or the fryer adds meat and is not vegetarian. This recipe uses oil.
  • Skip the pebre and the plate has no chile heat. The written recipe includes it, so the heat stays mild.

Pairings

  • A bowl of pebre
  • Warm chancaca syrup
  • Tea on a rainy afternoon
