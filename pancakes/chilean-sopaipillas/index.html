History

Chilean sopaipillas are fried rounds of a dough that includes pumpkin or squash, eaten in winter rains as street and home food. They can be savory, with pebre, a Chilean salsa, or sweet, with a chancaca syrup. They are not the same as New Mexican sopapillas, which are a puffed wheat bread. The pumpkin is the Chilean clue.

Cultural background

The dough is rolled and fried. Rainy-day vendors are part of the food’s reputation. This recipe is the fried bread. Pebre is a side you can make from onion, cilantro, and chile.

Why it tastes this way

Pumpkin sweetens and colors the dough and keeps it moist, which is why a sopaipilla tastes different from a plain fry bread. Winter squash was the vegetable on hand. The fry puffs the rounds. Savory pebre or sweet syrup are both real finishes, a split personality worth reporting. The New Mexican bread shares a name and not a recipe.

How it is made

Mashed squash is mixed with flour and fried as thin rounds.

Authentic touch

Cook the squash dry before it goes into the dough. Watery squash makes a sticky dough that drinks oil. Fry hot so they puff.

How it is used

Hot, with pebre or a sprinkle of sugar. A sweet syrup is the other traditional finish.

Field notes

  • Chilean sopaipillas contain pumpkin. New Mexican sopapillas do not.
  • They are a rainy-day fried bread.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup mashed cooked pumpkin or winter squash, dry
  • 2 cups flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • Oil for frying

Steps

  1. Mix squash, flour, baking powder, and salt into a soft dough. Add a spoon of water only if needed.
  2. Roll 1/4 inch thick and cut rounds.
  3. Fry in hot oil until puffed and golden.
  4. Drain.
  5. Serve with pebre or a little sugar.
  6. The squash must be dry.

Fry over steady heat.

This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Pass-through New Mexican sopapillas are a different wheat puff and are not this recipe.
  • A sweet chancaca syrup is the traditional sweet plate.
