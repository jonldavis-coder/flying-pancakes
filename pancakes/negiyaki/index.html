Negiyaki

You come in by Plane. Japan · East Asia.

Pronounced NEH-gee-yah-kee

A scallion-heavy savory pancake, sweeter and greener than cabbage okonomiyaki, with a soft wheat crumb.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 2 pancakes

History

Negiyaki is a scallion pancake of the Kansai, associated with Osaka and Kobe scallion cakes that predate the modern cabbage-heavy okonomiyaki. Negi means scallion. This page does not give the dish an invention year. The batter is wheat and egg, crowded with scallion, and cooked thicker than Korean pajeon. A kombu and shiitake stock keeps this version vegetarian. It is an older relative in the savory batter family, not a cabbage okonomiyaki with a new sauce.

Cultural background

Kobe and Osaka both still serve negiyaki, sometimes under names that vary by shop. The pancake is softer and more onion-sweet than a Korean jeon, and it lacks the tall cabbage of later okonomiyaki. Sauce and mayonnaise are optional. The scallion should be obvious in the crumb.

Why it tastes this way

A large amount of scallion steams inside the batter and turns sweet, with a green bite at the tips. Wheat and egg make a cushioned crumb rather than a thin cracker. Kombu and dried shiitake add a quiet savor without fish. Because cabbage is not the bulk, the cake tastes cleaner and more onion-like than Osaka okonomiyaki.

How it is made

Fold a large quantity of scallion into a wheat batter made with kombu-shiitake stock, then griddle it slowly.

Authentic touch

Cut the scallions fairly fine and fold them in until the batter looks green. A few strips on top will not make negiyaki.

How it is used

Cut and eat it hot, with sauce if you want it, or with only salt. It is a savory street cake.

Field notes

  • Negiyaki is tied to Kansai scallion cakes that came before cabbage-heavy okonomiyaki.
  • This page does not state an invention year for it.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose wheat flour
  • 1 egg
  • 3/4 cup kombu and shiitake stock
  • 6 scallions, finely chopped
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Neutral oil

Steps

  1. Simmer a piece of kombu and two dried shiitakes in 2 cups of water for 10 minutes. Measure 3/4 cup and cool it. Save the rest for another pot.
  2. Whisk flour, egg, stock, and salt. Fold in the scallions.
  3. Oil a pan over medium-low. Spread half the batter into a thick round.
  4. Cover and cook until the underside browns and the center sets. Flip and finish the other side.
  5. Repeat. Slice and serve.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A pinch of tenkasu, tempura bits, which are wheat
  • Bonito flakes, which would add fish

Pairings

  • Okonomiyaki sauce
  • Mayonnaise
  • Aonori
