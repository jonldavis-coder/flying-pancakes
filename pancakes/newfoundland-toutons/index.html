Arriving in Saint John's

Newfoundland toutons

You come in by Boat. Canada · North America.

Pronounced TOO-tuns

Fried bread dough, crisp at the rim, with salt pork and a side of molasses.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 toutons

History

Toutons are pieces of bread dough fried for breakfast in Newfoundland, often in salt pork fat and almost always with molasses at the table. Scrunchions, the crisp bits of salt pork, are part of that plate. Saint John's is the map pin for an island-wide habit. The dough here is a simple yeast dough, and the rise happens in the steps rather than in a multi-day prep time.

Cultural background

Toutons use the same kind of dough as a plain white loaf. Frying is what makes them toutons. Molasses on the hot bread is the usual sweetness. This written recipe fries them in rendered salt pork, so it contains meat.

Why it tastes this way

Pork fat browns the dough and adds salt. The inside stays soft because the pieces are fried only long enough to color and heat through. Molasses is bitter-sweet and runs into the fried face. A dough fried in a dry pan without fat will toughen instead of crisp.

How it is made

Yeast dough pinched into pieces and fried in rendered salt pork fat.

Authentic touch

Fry the pieces in rendered salt pork fat and serve molasses on the side. Butter is a home substitute and would make a different allergen list.

How it is used

Hot, with dark molasses.

Field notes

  • Toutons are fried bread dough, a Newfoundland breakfast, commonly served with molasses.
  • Salt pork fat is the usual frying medium, and the crisp bits are called scrunchions.

Ingredients

  • 2 1/2 cups all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup warm water
  • 4 ounces salt pork, diced
  • 1/2 cup molasses, to serve

Steps

  1. Stir the flour, yeast, sugar, and salt. Add the warm water and mix to a soft dough. Knead a few times in the bowl.
  2. Cover and let the dough rise until puffy, about 1 hour. That rise is unattended time, not extra active prep.
  3. While the dough rises, cook the diced salt pork in a skillet over medium-low heat until the fat renders and the bits are crisp. Scoop the bits out and leave about 3 tablespoons of fat in the pan.
  4. Pinch off pieces of dough and flatten them into small rounds.
  5. Fry in the pork fat over medium heat until both sides are golden and the center is cooked, about 2 to 3 minutes per side.
  6. Serve hot with molasses and the crisp pork bits.

Render the pork slowly and pour off excess fat before it smokes. Do not leave the pan.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Leftover risen bread dough is the practical Newfoundland start when you already have a loaf going.
  • Frying in butter instead of salt pork removes the meat and adds dairy.

Pairings

  • Molasses
  • A cup of tea
  • The crisp salt-pork bits from the pan
