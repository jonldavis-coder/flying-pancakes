History

Pain perdu, lost bread, is the French kitchen’s way of frying stale bread that has been soaked in milk and egg. The name says the bread would have been wasted. It is the ancestor of American French toast and a relative of New Orleans pain perdu, which is richer and often orange-scented. The French home version is simpler: stale bread, custard, butter, and sugar.

Cultural background

It is breakfast or a last-minute dessert. Brioche makes it cake-like. A lean country loaf makes it more bread-like. Both are fair if the bread is dry.

Why it tastes this way

Stale bread drinks custard because it has lost moisture. Fresh bread turns to paste. Butter browns the milk sugars on the surface. The thrift is the cultural fact: a bakery culture had leftover loaves, and the pancake journal includes this because it is the fried, egg-soaked cousin of the crêpe, not because it is poured batter.

How it is made

Stale bread is dipped in sweetened milk and egg and fried in butter.

Authentic touch

Dry the slices overnight if they are fresh. Soak them until heavy, then let them drip. Fry in butter over medium so the center custard sets.

How it is used

Sugar, honey, or fruit.

Field notes

  • The name means lost bread, bread that would have been thrown out.
  • Dry bread is the requirement. Fresh bread will not do.

Ingredients

  • 4 slices stale bread
  • 1 cup milk
  • 1 large egg
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon vanilla
  • Pinch of salt
  • 2 tablespoons butter

Steps

  1. Whisk milk, egg, sugar, vanilla, and salt.
  2. Soak the bread until the centers soften.
  3. Fry in butter over medium until deep golden on both sides.
  4. The center should be set.
  5. Sugar them lightly.
  6. Serve at once.

This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • The New Orleans page is the Creole version with orange zest.
  • Brioche needs less soaking than a tight crumb.
