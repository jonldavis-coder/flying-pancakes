Mexican Torrijas

Lenten fried bread in a cinnamon syrup.

20 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 8 · 8 slices

History

Torrijas in Mexico are a Lent and Easter sweet in many households: bread soaked in milk or a cinnamon syrup, dipped in egg, and fried. They belong to the Spanish torrija tradition and to local Lenten cooking. They are a holiday bread, not hotcakes.

Cultural background

A bolillo or a white loaf can be used if it is dry. Piloncillo or sugar and cinnamon scent the syrup. They are eaten cool or warm.

Why it tastes this way

Cinnamon and piloncillo, unrefined cane sugar, are the Mexican sweetness, deeper than white sugar. Lent is why a bread dessert shows up when meat is set aside in some observant kitchens. The fry and the syrup make stale bread a sweet. The Spanish root is real. The piloncillo is the local taste.

How it is made

Dry bread is dipped in milk, then egg, fried, and soaked briefly in cinnamon syrup.

Authentic touch

Dry bread, a short soak, and a syrup that is sticky but not a candy. Fry only until the egg sets and the outside is brown.

How it is used

As a Lenten sweet, warm or cold.

Field notes

  • Mexican torrijas are a Lent and Easter sweet in many houses.
  • Piloncillo or brown sugar is the Mexican sweetness.

Ingredients

  • 8 slices stale bread
  • 1 cup milk
  • 1 cinnamon stick
  • 2 tablespoons piloncillo or brown sugar
  • 2 eggs
  • Oil for frying
  • 1/4 cup sugar
  • 1 teaspoon cinnamon

Steps

  1. Heat milk with the cinnamon and piloncillo. Cool and strain.
  2. Dip the bread, then the eggs.
  3. Fry until golden.
  4. Mix sugar and cinnamon and coat the slices.
  5. Serve warm or cool.
  6. Use dry bread.

This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Spanish torrijas are the peninsula cousin.
  • Cuban torrejas lean Christmas rather than Lent.

Pairings
