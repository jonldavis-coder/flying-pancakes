Brazilian beiju

You come in by Boat. Brazil · South America.

Pronounced bay-ZHOO

A plain tapioca flatbread, soft or crisp depending on how long it stays on the griddle, with almost no seasoning.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 flatbreads

History

Beiju is an Indigenous tapioca flatbread. Tupi-Guarani peoples use beiju. That name covers a language family spread over a wide region, not one village and not a secret recipe this page can honestly claim. The street tapioca crepe filled with cheese or coconut is a later urban relative that uses the same starch. Today's home beiju starts from commercial tapioca starch, which is already processed. It is a plain round cooked on a griddle. It is not a lesson in grating and pressing bitter cassava.

Cultural background

In Paraíba, Pernambuco, and other Northeastern kitchens, beiju is breakfast and a snack, sometimes soft, sometimes dried until it snaps. People eat it with butter, coconut, or nothing at all. This recipe leaves the cheese off so the flatbread itself is clear. Butter would add dairy. It is not in the ingredient list.

Why it tastes this way

Commercial tapioca starch is mild, almost neutral, with a faint sweetness when it toasts. Salt is optional and small. A short cook keeps the round flexible and chewy. A longer cook dries it toward a cracker. There is no cheese to salt it and no wheat to brown like bread. The flavor is the starch and the pan.

How it is made

Moisten dry commercial tapioca starch if needed, salt it lightly, sieve it into a dry skillet, and cook until the round holds together. Leave it longer if you want it crisp.

Authentic touch

Sieve the moist starch into an even layer and leave it alone until it coheres. Stirring breaks the sheet. For a crisp beiju, keep it on the heat longer after it sets, until the surface looks dry. Use commercial starch only.

How it is used

Eat it warm as a plain bread, next to coffee, or with coconut and salt if you want the Northeastern snack. Cheese belongs on the tapioca-crepe page, not in this dough.

Field notes

  • Tupi-Guarani is a language family. This page does not invent a single tribe's secret recipe.
  • Use commercial tapioca starch. Raw bitter cassava can be poisonous, and home processing is not taught here.
  • The filled street tapioca is a later relative. Beiju on this page is the plain flatbread.

Ingredients

  • 200 g commercial tapioca starch
  • Up to 80 ml water, only if the starch is dry
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon neutral oil, only if you need to wipe a sticky pan

Steps

  1. If the commercial starch is dry, moisten it with just enough water to feel like damp sand. Toss with the salt.
  2. Heat a dry nonstick skillet over medium heat.
  3. Sieve a thin, even layer of starch into the pan. Do not stir.
  4. Cook until the round holds together and the underside spots. For a crisp beiju, leave it longer, until the surface looks dry.
  5. Slide it out and repeat. Serve warm or let the crisp ones cool.

Use commercial tapioca starch. Raw bitter cassava can be poisonous. This recipe does not teach home processing of raw cassava.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Beiju de tapioca with coconut is a common Northeast snack. Coconut does not add wheat.
  • The filled cheese crepe is the other tapioca page in this journal.
  • Drying the round completely makes a cracker that keeps. A shorter cook makes a flexible bread.

Pairings

  • Black coffee
  • Grated coconut and a pinch of salt
  • Butter, which adds dairy and means the bread is no longer vegan
