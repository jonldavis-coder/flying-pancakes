Arriving in Dunedin

New Zealand pikelets

You come in by Boat. New Zealand · Oceania.

Pronounced PIKE-lets

Small tea-table pikelets with a golden edge, finished with butter and golden syrup.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · About 14 pikelets

History

New Zealand pikelets come from the same British drop-scone cooking that settlers carried south. On this tea table they are a batch for morning or afternoon tea, the small thick cakes home cooks still stir up without a special occasion. The pin is Dunedin, a city with a strong Scottish settlement history, and the cakes are not a Dunedin invention. They are a national home recipe. Golden syrup and butter finish this batch. The Australian pikelet page is a different write-up, with jam, and it is not copied here.

Cultural background

Pikelets belong to the kitchen, the school fair, and the visit. The batter is mixed in one bowl. Golden syrup is a common New Zealand pantry sweetener, poured in a thin ribbon. The cakes are eaten warm if possible, and cold if the batch was large.

Why it tastes this way

A spoon of golden syrup in the batter tastes of cooked cane sugar, deeper than white sugar alone. Baking powder makes a soft, small rise. The griddle edge goes a shade darker, which keeps the cake from eating like plain bread. Butter on top melts into that surface. The result is tea food, not a lemon dessert pancake.

How it is made

Rub or stir flour, baking powder, and salt with an egg, milk, melted butter, and a spoon of golden syrup to a thick batter. Cook spoonfuls on a moderate griddle until both sides are golden. Serve with more butter and a thread of golden syrup.

Authentic touch

Keep them small, for the tea table, and offer butter and golden syrup. Do not scale the spoonfuls up into dinner plates. The British source is shared with Australian pikelets. The finish here is syrup, and the batter has syrup in it.

How it is used

New Zealand pikelets are a snack for tea, at home or at a hall table. They are not a savory meal. Extra syrup is the condiment. Jam is the other spread and is not the one this page builds around.

Field notes

  • New Zealand pikelets share a British drop-scone source with Australian pikelets.
  • This batch is finished with butter and golden syrup.
  • They are small tea-table cakes, not large dinner pancakes.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup wheat flour (about 125 g)
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/4 teaspoon fine salt
  • 1 egg
  • 1/2 cup milk
  • 1 tablespoon melted unsalted butter, plus more for the pan and the table
  • 1 tablespoon golden syrup, plus more to serve

Steps

  1. Stir the flour, baking powder, and salt. Whisk in the egg, milk, melted butter, and golden syrup until you have a thick batter.
  2. Heat a griddle to medium and butter it lightly.
  3. Drop tablespoons of batter so the cakes stay small. Leave room. They are tea pikelets, not wide pancakes.
  4. Cook until the tops dull and the bottoms are golden, then turn once and cook the second side.
  5. Serve warm with butter and a thin pour of golden syrup.

The batter contains wheat, egg, and dairy. Golden syrup is a concentrated sugar. It is not honey.

Keep leftover pikelets covered and eat them the same day if you can. Refrigerate overnight and warm them in a covered pan. Add syrup when you serve them. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Jam instead of syrup is the other tea-table finish.
  • A mashed ripe banana in the batter is a moist home variant and a different flavor.
  • Lemon juice in the batter pushes the cakes toward a sharper pancake and away from this syrup batch.

Pairings

  • Butter
  • Golden syrup
  • Tea
