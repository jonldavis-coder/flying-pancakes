Midlands Pikelets

Thick, soft, lightly sweet griddle cakes.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 12 pikelets

History

Across much of the Midlands, a pikelet is a small thick pancake, close to a Scotch pancake or drop scone, not the holey Yorkshire kind. The shared name confuses visitors and is worth keeping straight. These are teatime cakes, buttered, sometimes with jam or syrup.

Cultural background

They are made from a thick egg batter and cooked in spoonfuls. They do not need yeast or rings.

Why it tastes this way

Egg and baking powder lift a soft crumb, and a spoon of sugar makes them tea cakes rather than bread. They taste milder and sweeter than a crumpet because they are not a salt sponge full of holes. The regional word is the cultural fact. The batter is the technical one.

How it is made

A thick sweetened batter is dropped onto a griddle and flipped once.

Authentic touch

Flip when the bubbles pop and stay open. They dry out if you cook them as long as a large pancake.

How it is used

Butter, jam, or a little golden syrup.

Field notes

  • Same word as the Yorkshire pikelet, different cake. The split is real.
  • They are the English drop-scone family.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1 large egg
  • 1/2 cup milk
  • 1 tablespoon melted butter

Steps

  1. Stir the dry ingredients together, then stir in the wet ingredients until the batter is combined.
  2. The batter should drop from the spoon and hold a shape.
  3. Heat a griddle or heavy skillet over medium heat and film it with butter.
  4. Spoon on the batter. Cook until the underside is browned and the top looks set.
  5. Flip once and cook the second side through.
  6. Cook small rounds and serve warm with butter.

This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Yorkshire pikelets are the holey ones.
  • Currants in the batter are a home variation.

Pairings

  • butter
  • jam
  • golden syrup
