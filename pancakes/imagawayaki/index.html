Imagawayaki

You come in by Plane. Japan · East Asia.

Pronounced ee-mah-gah-wah-YAH-kee

A round filled cake cooked in a hinged iron, with sweet red bean paste sealed inside a soft wheat shell.

15 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 8 · 8 filled cakes

History

Imagawayaki are round filled cakes cooked in a molded iron. Batter is poured, sweet red bean paste is set in the center, and a second skin of batter closes the cake before the iron is turned. The names vary by city. Imagawayaki is one name, and obanyaki is another, heard often in Kansai. This page does not pick a founding shop or a year. The method is the mold, which makes it a different sweet from dorayaki's two separate pancakes.

Cultural background

Street stalls still use heavy hinged pans over a gas flame. The cake should be brown, not pale, and the paste should reach neither the hinge nor the open air. A leak of anko welds the cake to the iron. Home cooks can use a small filled-cake pan or an electric mold with the same batter.

Why it tastes this way

The iron browns the batter on both sides at once, so the crust tastes more toasted than a dorayaki cooked in an open pan. Egg and milk keep the interior tender. Anko is sweet and beany, a filling rather than a sandwich layer you can see. Because the paste steams inside the mold, it stays moist against the crumb.

How it is made

Cook a wheat batter in a round mold, bury a spoon of anko, cover it with more batter, and turn the iron until both faces brown.

Authentic touch

Add the bean paste only after the first batter has skinned over. If you drop the paste into raw batter, it sinks and breaks the seal.

How it is used

Cool them until the center is no longer scorching. Eat them as a street snack with tea. They are a dessert, not a meal.

Field notes

  • Imagawayaki and obanyaki are two city names for this molded filled cake.
  • The iron cooks both sides. Dorayaki is two pancakes cooked in the open and then joined.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose wheat flour
  • 1 egg
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/2 cup milk
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1 cup sweet red bean paste
  • Neutral oil for the molds

Steps

  1. Whisk flour, egg, sugar, milk, and baking powder into a thick batter. Rest it 10 minutes.
  2. Oil a hot imagawayaki iron or a round filled-cake pan.
  3. Pour a little batter into each mold, add a spoon of anko, and cover with more batter so the paste is sealed.
  4. Cook, turning the iron or flipping the cakes, until both sides are deep brown and the batter is set.
  5. Cool briefly and serve.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Custard in place of anko
  • Obanyaki, the same mold cake under another city's name

Pairings

  • Green tea
  • Roasted barley tea
  • Nothing sweet beside them
