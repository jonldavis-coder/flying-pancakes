History

Piaya is a flatbread from Bacolod in Negros, filled with muscovado sugar and cooked on a griddle until the sugar melts. Negros is sugar country, which is why the filling is unrefined cane sugar. It is a pasalubong, a gift food, and a snack. The dough is simple. The sugar is the point.

Cultural background

The finished piaya is flat, browned in spots, and chewy. It keeps for a few days, which is why it travels as a gift. Sesame on the outside is common. This recipe includes sesame and lists it.

Why it tastes this way

Muscovado tastes of molasses, not of white sugar, because it keeps the cane’s minerals. A thin dough on a griddle melts that sugar into a sheet. Negros grew the sugar. The snack is the local use of it. Sesame on the crust adds a toasted note. It is a griddled bread, not a poured pancake, and it belongs with the other filled griddle breads.

How it is made

A simple dough is filled with muscovado, rolled thin, and cooked on a griddle.

Authentic touch

Use moist muscovado. Roll the sealed disk thin. Cook on medium until both sides spot and the sugar is molten. If the disk is thick, the center stays doughy.

How it is used

Warm or cool, as a snack. It firms as it cools and is still good.

Field notes

  • Piaya is Bacolod’s muscovado flatbread.
  • Negros sugar is the reason the filling tastes of molasses.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups flour
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 1 cup muscovado sugar
  • 1 tablespoon sesame seeds

Steps

  1. Mix a dough from flour, salt, oil, and water. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Fill disks with muscovado, seal, and roll thin. Sprinkle with sesame.
  3. Cook on a medium griddle until spotted and the sugar has melted.
  4. Cool slightly.
  5. They firm as they cool.
  6. Serve as a snack.

This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Without sesame it is plainer and free of that allergen.
  • A very dark muscovado tastes more molasses-like.

Pairings
