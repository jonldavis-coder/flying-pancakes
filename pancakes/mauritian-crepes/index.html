Arriving in Port Louis

Mauritian crepes

You come in by Boat. Mauritius · East Africa.

Pronounced KREPS

Thin French Creole crepes with a vanilla batter, folded over brown sugar.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · About 8 thin crepes

History

Mauritian crepes are the thin pancakes of French Creole cooking on the island, made at home and sold on the street in Port Louis and at fairs. French colonial kitchens left the crepe. Mauritian cooks kept it beside a table that is also Indian, African, and Chinese. This page is only the crepe: a thin wheat batter with vanilla, cooked in a small pan and folded with brown sugar. It is not a Breton galette and not a dholl puri. Mauritius is filed under East Africa on this map.

Cultural background

A street crepe is cooked to order and handed over in paper. At home the same batter waits in the refrigerator and is cooked for a snack. Brown sugar is the local sweet finish in this recipe. The island grows sugar, and the crepe is one of the foods that use it plainly.

Why it tastes this way

A thin batter of milk, egg, and flour browns in spots and tastes of toasted butter and vanilla. Brown sugar on the hot crepe melts into a molasses-tasting pool, darker than white sugar. There is no buckwheat and no salty filling. The flavor is simple because the cake is thin. The first crepe of the pan is the test piece.

How it is made

Blend or whisk flour, egg, milk, water, sugar, salt, and vanilla into a thin batter and rest it. Cook small crepes in a buttered pan, flipping once. Sprinkle brown sugar on the hot crepe, fold it, and serve it as a street snack or a home one.

Authentic touch

Cook the batter thin enough to see the color of the pan through it, and fold it with brown sugar. A buckwheat galette with ham would be a different country's crepe. This is the Mauritian street and home version.

How it is used

These crepes are street food, a snack, and a simple dessert. They are eaten hot. Lemon is another finish. A savory filling is a different order and may add ingredients this batter does not contain.

Field notes

  • Mauritian crepes are French Creole street and home food, thin and sweet.
  • The island's wider table is also Indian, African, and Chinese. This page is only the crepe.
  • Brown sugar is the finish in this recipe, melted on the hot cake.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup wheat flour (about 125 g)
  • 1 egg
  • 1 cup milk
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon fine salt
  • 1 teaspoon vanilla extract
  • Butter for the pan
  • Brown sugar, to finish

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, egg, milk, water, sugar, salt, and vanilla into a thin batter. Rest it for 20 minutes.
  2. Heat a small pan and melt a little butter. Pour a thin layer of batter and swirl it. This is a crepe, not a thick pikelet.
  3. Cook until the edge lifts and the underside spots, then flip once and cook briefly.
  4. Sprinkle brown sugar over the hot crepe and fold it into quarters.
  5. Repeat with the remaining batter. Serve the crepes as they are cooked, the way a street pan does.

The batter contains wheat, egg, and dairy. Vanilla extract may contain alcohol. Brown sugar is still sugar.

Cooked crepes can be refrigerated, stacked with paper between them, and reheated in a pan. Add the brown sugar after reheating. Do not leave the egg batter out. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A spoon of white rum in the batter is a grown-up home variant and not in this recipe.
  • Coconut flakes on the sugar are a coastal finish and are not required.
  • Sweetened condensed milk, a common island pantry item, adds more dairy on a crepe that already contains milk.

Pairings

  • Brown sugar, already in the fold
  • Lime or lemon
  • Coffee
