Pronounced KOLE-bool-ar

A thick pork-flecked pancake cooked outdoors in the old style, here in a skillet.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · one large cake

History

Kolbullar were the food of Swedish charcoal burners and timber workers: a thick pancake of flour, water, salt pork, and sometimes egg, cooked in a pan over an open fire. The name is tied to coal, the charcoal camps. They are a historical work food more than a modern café item, and some local festivals still cook them. This is a home skillet version of that camp cake.

Cultural background

Pork is diced and cooked in the batter. It is a meal. Lingonberry is a welcome side if you have it. The original pan sat on coals, which a kitchen cannot honestly copy. A heavy skillet is the adaptation, stated as such.

Why it tastes this way

Salt pork and a simple flour batter made a high-calorie meal where workers had a sack of flour and cured meat and no kitchen. The cake tastes salty, smoky if you use smoked pork, and plain. That plainness is the history. It was fuel for cold outdoor work, not a spice-trade dessert.

How it is made

Diced salt pork is mixed into a thick flour batter and fried as one or two sturdy cakes.

Authentic touch

Cut the pork small so it cooks in the time the batter sets. Cook covered, on lower heat than a crepe. The center is thick.

How it is used

Hot, in wedges, with lingonberry if you want the tart side.

Field notes

  • Kolbullar are associated with charcoal-burner camps, which is the origin of the name.
  • A home skillet is an adaptation of a fire pan, not a claim that the coals are in your kitchen.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup flour
  • 1 1/4 cups water
  • 1 large egg
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 4 ounces salt pork or bacon, diced small

Steps

  1. Whisk flour, water, egg, and salt into a thick batter. Stir in the pork.
  2. Heat a skillet and cook the batter as one cake over medium-low, covered.
  3. Flip when the bottom is brown and the pork on the underside looks cooked.
  4. Cook until the center is set and the pork is done.
  5. Rest 2 minutes.
  6. Cut into wedges.

This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Bacon can stand in for salt pork.
  • A meatless batter is a different modern cake and should not be called kolbullar.

Pairings
