Arriving in Gondar

Ethiopian kita

You come in by Plane. Ethiopia · East Africa.

Pronounced KEE-tah

Mild, soft teff flatbread with a light toasted face and no sour ferment.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · About 8 small rounds

History

Kita is a quick unfermented flatbread, cooked the day it is mixed. It is not injera. Injera needs days of souring and is cooked on one side. Kita is mixed, rested only long enough for the flour to drink the water, and cooked on a skillet. Torn kita becomes chechebsa, tossed with spiced butter and berbere, or kita firfir, soaked in a spiced sauce. This recipe uses teff and stays unfermented.

Cultural background

Kita is a breakfast and a way to use a hot pan when there is no sour batter waiting. In home kitchens it is torn by hand. The bread itself is plain. The chile and the spiced butter arrive when it is turned into chechebsa or firfir.

Why it tastes this way

Because the batter is unfermented, kita tastes of toasted teff rather than of lactic sourness. There are no ayen. The crumb is closer to a soft griddled flatbread than to a sponge. If it sits for days it will start to become something else, which is why this method cooks it the same morning.

How it is made

Stir teff flour, water, and salt into a thick batter and rest it for about ten minutes so the flour hydrates. Do not ferment it. Cook small rounds on a moderate skillet, flipping once, until both faces are set and lightly spotted. Tear the bread while it is warm if you are making chechebsa or kita firfir.

Authentic touch

Say it plainly while you cook: this dough is unfermented. Tear the finished bread for chechebsa or kita firfir instead of leaving it as a neat stack. A fermented sour loaf is injera, and it does not belong on this page.

How it is used

Plain kita is breakfast with tea. Torn and mixed with niter kibbeh, the spiced clarified butter, and berbere, it becomes chechebsa. Torn into a berbere sauce, it becomes kita firfir. Those finishes add dairy and chile. They are not in the bread.

Field notes

  • Kita is unfermented. Leaving the batter for days would turn the method into a different bread.
  • Chechebsa is torn kita tossed with spiced butter and berbere.
  • Kita firfir is the torn bread soaked in a spiced sauce rather than folded.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups teff flour (about 210 g)
  • 1 1/2 cups water
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
  • 1 teaspoon neutral oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Stir the teff flour, water, and salt into a thick batter with no dry pockets. This dough is unfermented. Do not add yeast or sourdough.
  2. Rest it for 10 minutes so the flour hydrates. Do not leave it overnight.
  3. Heat a skillet over medium heat and wipe it with the oil. Spread a small ladle of batter into a round about the width of a saucer.
  4. Cook until the underside is set and spotted, then flip once and cook the second side. Kita is cooked on both sides, unlike injera.
  5. Stack the rounds under a cloth. Tear them for chechebsa or kita firfir, or eat them plain while warm.

The bread is gluten-free only if the teff flour is free of wheat. Berbere used later for chechebsa is a chile blend and can be very hot.

Eat kita the day it is cooked. Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Do not ferment leftover batter and call it this recipe. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Wheat kita is common and must be tagged as wheat. It is not gluten-free. This written recipe does not use it.
  • Chechebsa uses spiced butter, so that version is not vegan or dairy-free.
  • A drier dough makes a firmer bread for tearing. A looser batter makes a softer round.

Pairings

  • Tea
  • Niter kibbeh and berbere, for chechebsa
  • A berbere sauce, for kita firfir
