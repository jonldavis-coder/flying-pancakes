Bolivian buñuelos

You come in by Train. Bolivia · South America.

Pronounced boo-NYWEH-lohs

Puffed fried dough scented with anise, served beside a cup of api, not cooked in it.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 8 · 16 fritters

History

Bolivian buñuelos are fried dough, often eaten with api at Todos Santos and also at breakfast in the highlands. Api is a hot purple-corn drink, spiced with cinnamon and sweetened, served in a cup beside the fritters. It is not part of this dough. The buñuelos themselves are wheat flour, egg, milk, and anise, spooned into oil and fried until they puff. They are fried. Mexican Christmas buñuelos are a different fritter, rolled thin and soaked in piloncillo syrup. These are the highland spoon-drop kind.

Cultural background

Around the first days of November, Todos Santos, families in La Paz and other highland cities fry buñuelos and pour api. The same pair shows up at ordinary breakfasts when the weather is cold. The drink and the fritter share a table. They do not share a bowl.

Why it tastes this way

Anise is the perfume in the dough, and frying turns the outside brown and crisp while baking powder and steam open the center. Milk and egg make the crumb tender rather than cracker-dry. There is only a little sugar in the dough, so the fritter is mildly sweet and the cup of api, if you serve it, carries more of the sugar. Purple corn in the drink tastes fruity and earthy. None of that corn is in the fritter.

How it is made

Mix wheat flour, egg, milk, sugar, anise, baking powder, and salt into a soft dough. Fry spoons of it until puffed and golden. Offer api, the purple-corn drink, on the side if you are making the full Todos Santos plate.

Authentic touch

Fry spoons of soft dough in oil deep enough to float them, and do not crowd the pan. They should puff and turn gold. A flat, dark fritter was fried too hot or from a dough that was too stiff. Serve api in cups next to the plate. Do not pour it into the dough.

How it is used

Serve the buñuelos hot, at breakfast or for Todos Santos, with api in a separate cup. The drink is a pairing. The recipe below is only the fried dough.

Field notes

  • Api is a purple-corn drink served beside the buñuelos. It is not mixed into the dough.
  • These buñuelos are fried and puffed, a highland breakfast and a Todos Santos food.
  • The Mexican buñuelo in this journal is a thin fried sheet with piloncillo syrup, a different recipe.

Ingredients

  • 200 g wheat flour
  • 1 egg
  • 100 ml milk
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1 teaspoon anise seed
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • Neutral oil for frying

Steps

  1. Stir the flour, sugar, anise, baking powder, and salt. Beat in the egg and milk until a soft, sticky dough forms.
  2. Heat 3 centimeters of oil until a pinch of dough sizzles and rises.
  3. Fry spoons of dough, turning once, until they puff and turn gold, about 2 to 3 minutes. Do not crowd them.
  4. Drain on a rack.
  5. Serve hot. If you make api, pour that purple-corn drink into cups beside the plate. Leave it out of the dough.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Buñuelos de queso add cheese to the dough and are still fried. They still contain dairy.
  • A cinnamon syrup poured over them is closer to the Mexican Christmas fritter and is not required here.
  • Api can be made from commercial purple-corn flour and spices. Keep it in the cup.

Pairings

  • Api, a purple-corn drink served beside them
  • A cup of coffee
  • A dust of sugar for people who want them sweeter
