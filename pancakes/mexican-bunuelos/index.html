Arriving in Puebla

Mexican buñuelos

You come in by Plane. Mexico · Mexico & Central America.

Pronounced boo-NYWEH-lohs

Thin, crisp fried dough with anise, broken into shards and soaked with piloncillo and cinnamon syrup.

30 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 8 · 8 buñuelos

History

These are the Christmas buñuelos of Mexican plazas and posadas: a wheat dough rolled thin, fried until blistered, and finished with piloncillo syrup. They are fried. They are not baked, and they are not griddled masa. Wheat sweets of this kind sit beside much older corn foods. A buñuelo is a feast fritter, tied to December, not a breakfast hotcake.

Cultural background

Around Christmas, stands fry buñuelos and break them into bowls, then pour hot syrup over the shards. Puebla and Mexico City both keep the custom, and so do smaller towns. The dough is an everyday flour-and-egg mix. The occasion is what makes it a feast.

Why it tastes this way

Frying drives water out of a thin sheet of dough, so the buñuelo shatters instead of chewing like bread. Anise and orange peel scent the syrup. Piloncillo, unrefined cane sugar, tastes of molasses rather than of white sugar alone. Cinnamon rounds it out. The contrast is crisp dough and a syrup that softens it a minute after it is poured.

How it is made

Make a simple wheat dough with egg and anise, rest it, roll thin rounds, and fry them until golden and blistered. Simmer piloncillo with cinnamon and orange peel and pour the syrup over the hot fritters.

Authentic touch

Roll the dough as thin as a flour tortilla. Thick pieces stay doughy in the center. Fry one at a time in enough oil to float it, and drain it before the syrup goes on.

How it is used

Break the buñuelos into a bowl, pour on warm syrup, and eat them at a Christmas gathering or a December street stand. They go soft if they sit in the syrup, which is how many people like them.

Field notes

  • These buñuelos are fried, not baked on a comal and not cooked like a pancake batter.
  • Piloncillo is a cone of unrefined cane sugar. Grate it or let it dissolve in the syrup.
  • Christmas plazas often serve the fritter already broken, with syrup ladled on top.

Ingredients

  • 250 g wheat flour, plus more for rolling
  • 1 egg
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon anise seed, crushed
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 80 ml water
  • Neutral oil for frying
  • 200 g piloncillo, 250 ml water, 1 cinnamon stick, and a strip of orange peel for the syrup

Steps

  1. Stir the flour, sugar, anise, and salt. Beat in the egg and water until a firm dough forms. Knead briefly, cover, and rest 20 minutes.
  2. Simmer the piloncillo, 250 ml water, cinnamon, and orange peel until the sugar dissolves and the syrup thickens slightly. Keep it warm.
  3. Divide the dough into 8 pieces and roll each very thin.
  4. Fry one round at a time in shimmering oil, turning once, until blistered and golden. Drain on a rack.
  5. Break into pieces, pour on the warm syrup, and serve.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Buñuelos de viento are small and puffy rather than wide and thin. They are also fried.
  • White sugar can stand in for piloncillo, with a less molasses-like syrup.
  • Some cooks add a little sugar to the dough. This one keeps the sweetness in the syrup.

Pairings

  • Café de olla
  • Hot chocolate beaten with water or milk
  • A second pour of syrup for people who want them softer
