A wheat arepa from the Andes, softer and breadier than the corn cake.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 arepas

History

In the Venezuelan Andes, arepas are often made with wheat flour rather than maize, a regional difference that surprises people who know only the corn arepa. They are cooked on a griddle and eaten at breakfast. This page keeps them separate so the corn arepa is not asked to stand for every Andean kitchen.

Cultural background

They are thicker than a tortilla and milder than masarepa cakes. Butter or cheese is a common partner. The dough here is vegan. Butter at the table adds dairy.

Why it tastes this way

Wheat grows better than a lowland maize habit in some highland kitchens, which is why the bread changed grain and kept the name. The flavor is toasted wheat, not nixtamal. A griddle still makes the crust. The regional fact is the point of a separate page.

How it is made

A soft wheat dough is shaped into thick disks and cooked on a griddle.

Authentic touch

Do not roll them as thin as tortillas. Cook them covered on medium-low so the center bakes through.

How it is used

Split or whole, with butter or cheese.

Field notes

  • In the Venezuelan Andes, arepas are often wheat, not corn.
  • They are still called arepas.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 3/4 cup water

Steps

  1. Mix a soft dough and rest 10 minutes.
  2. Shape thick disks.
  3. Cook covered on a medium-low griddle, turning once, until the center is set.
  4. Serve warm.
  5. They are wheat, not corn.
  6. Keep them thicker than tortillas.

This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Corn arepas are the lowland daily bread.
  • A spoon of anise is used in some Andean doughs.

Pairings

  • butter
  • cheese
