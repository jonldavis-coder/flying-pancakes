Injera

You come in by Plane. Ethiopia · East Africa.

Pronounced in-JEER-uh

Sour, spongy teff bread with an earthy crumb and open holes on top.

25 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 6 · About 8 large rounds

History

Teff, Eragrostis tef, was domesticated in the Horn of Africa, and injera is the fermented flatbread made from it. A teff batter is left to sour, then cooked on one side on a mitad, the clay griddle. The bread covers a platter and is torn to pinch stews. This recipe is 100 percent teff. Wheat blends used by some bakeries are a different loaf and are not in this method.

Cultural background

Injera is both the starch and the utensil at a shared Ethiopian meal. Guests tear pieces and use them to pick up wat. The sourness is expected. A fresh injera should bend and hold a stew without cracking into dust.

Why it tastes this way

Two to three days of fermentation feed lactic acid bacteria and wild yeast, so the bread tastes sour rather than sweet. Teff itself is earthy and slightly nutty. On the hot mitad the bubbles set into ayen, the eyes. The top stays soft because the bread is never flipped, and steam under a cloth finishes it so it can soak up sauce.

How it is made

Whisk teff flour and water into a batter like heavy cream and ferment it, covered, for 2 to 3 days until sour and bubbly. Salt it only after that rest. Pour it in a spiral on a hot mitad or a wide nonstick skillet, cover, and cook on one side until the ayen are open and the top is matte. Do not flip. Stack the rounds in a cloth so they steam and soften.

Authentic touch

Cook on one side only and leave the ayen open. Do not flip. If the eyes close under a second side of heat, the surface toughens and stops drinking the stew.

How it is used

Injera lines a platter under meats and vegetable stews, or under a fasting spread of lentils and greens. It is the accompaniment to the meal, not a side dish in a separate basket. Leftover bread is torn into firfir the next day.

Field notes

  • The holes on the surface are called ayen, the Amharic word for eyes.
  • A mitad is the round clay griddle used to cook injera on one side.
  • One hundred percent teff injera is gluten-free only if the flour was not milled with wheat.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups teff flour, ivory or brown (about 280 g)
  • 3 cups water, plus more for thinning
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
  • A few drops of neutral oil, only if the pan sticks

Steps

  1. Whisk the teff flour and water into a smooth batter about as thick as heavy cream. There is no wheat in this recipe. Cover the bowl.
  2. Leave the batter at room temperature for 2 to 3 days, until it smells sour and looks bubbly. Stir it once a day. This is the ferment. Do not add baking powder.
  3. Stir in the salt only after the ferment. Add a spoon of water if the batter is thicker than pourable cream.
  4. Heat a mitad or a wide nonstick skillet over medium heat. Wipe it with a few drops of oil only if the surface sticks. Pour the batter in a spiral and tilt the pan into a large thin round.
  5. Cover and cook on one side until the top is set and the ayen, the eyes, are open. Do not flip. Move the bread to a clean cloth and let the steam soften it while you cook the rest.

This loaf is gluten-free only when the teff flour has not been milled or stored with wheat. A shared griddle can transfer wheat flour.

Wrap cooked injera in a clean cloth for the day, then refrigerate and reheat in a covered pan. Keep sour batter refrigerated if you are not cooking it the same day. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Ivory teff makes a paler injera with the same ferment.
  • A spoon of saved batter from a previous batch can start the next one.
  • Adding wheat flour is a bakery shortcut. It is not this recipe, and it removes the gluten-free diet tag.

Pairings

  • Red lentil stew
  • Collard greens
  • Spiced chicken stew, for cooks who eat meat
