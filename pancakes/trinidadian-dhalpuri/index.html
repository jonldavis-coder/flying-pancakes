Trinidadian dhalpuri roti

You come in by Boat. Trinidad and Tobago · Caribbean.

Pronounced dahl-POO-ree ROH-tee

A soft wheat roti with a layer of seasoned ground split peas that flakes when the bread is clapped.

40 min prep · 35 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 rotis

History

After emancipation in the British Caribbean, planters recruited indentured workers from India to keep the cane fields going. Indenture was a fixed contract, often five years, and it was not free passage. The first ships from India reached Trinidad in 1845. Guyana received workers on the same system. Dhalpuri roti, a wheat flatbread stuffed with seasoned ground split peas, comes from that Indo-Caribbean kitchen. It is a shop and home staple in Trinidad and Tobago and in Guyana. This is the stuffed roti, not a stand-in for every Indian bread.

Cultural background

Dhalpuri is the wrap around curries at roti shops in Port of Spain and in Guyanese lunchrooms. The filling is called dhal, but it is ground and dried, not a soup. Families also cook it for Sunday lunch. The recipe uses oil, not ghee, so it stays vegan.

Why it tastes this way

The split-pea filling tastes of cumin, garlic, and black pepper, toasted by the griddle through the wheat skin. Because the peas are ground and dry, they flake inside the bread instead of steaming it into paste. The roti itself is plain wheat and salt, a little rich from the oil you wipe on while it cooks. Clapping the hot bread breaks the pea layer into a loose crumble.

How it is made

Boil yellow split peas, drain them very well, and grind them with garlic, cumin, salt, and pepper. Seal a spoonful inside a wheat dough, roll it out, and cook it on a hot tawa with a little oil.

Authentic touch

The pea paste has to be dry and fully cooled before it goes into the dough. Wet filling bursts and soaks the roti. Roll gently so the peas spread in a thin sheet without tearing the surface.

How it is used

Use the roti to wrap a curry, or eat it beside a stew. In a shop it is often the edible plate under goat, chicken, or a potato and chickpea curry. Those fillings are separate from this bread.

Field notes

  • The first indenture ships from India arrived in Trinidad in 1845, after emancipation.
  • Dhalpuri is also a Guyanese staple. The same labor system brought the kitchen there.
  • The split peas are ground and dried, not left as a poured lentil soup.

Ingredients

  • 200 g dried yellow split peas
  • 2 garlic cloves
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1 teaspoon salt, divided
  • 1/4 teaspoon black pepper
  • 300 g wheat flour, plus more for rolling
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil, plus more for the tawa
  • 160 ml water

Steps

  1. Boil the split peas in unsalted water until they are very soft. Drain them and spread them out so the steam can leave. They must be dry.
  2. Grind the peas with the garlic, cumin, 1/2 teaspoon salt, and the pepper into a fine, dry paste. Cool it completely.
  3. Mix the flour, the remaining salt, 1 tablespoon oil, and the water into a soft dough. Rest it, covered, for 20 minutes.
  4. Divide into 6 pieces. Flatten one, put a spoon of pea paste in the center, seal, and roll carefully into a thin round.
  5. Cook on a hot tawa, turning and brushing with oil, until both sides are spotted. Clap the hot roti between clean hands, or fold it in a towel and press, so the pea layer crumbles. Serve warm.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A small minced hot pepper in the peas is common. It raises the heat above none.
  • Ghee on the tawa adds dairy, so the roti is no longer vegan or dairy-free.
  • Guyanese shop roti uses the same stuffed idea, with local differences in spice and size.

Pairings

  • Potato and chickpea curry
  • Mango kuchela on the side
  • A meat curry, which makes the whole plate not vegetarian
