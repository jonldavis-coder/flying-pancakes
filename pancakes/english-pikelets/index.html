English pikelets

You come in by Train. United Kingdom · Western Europe.

Pronounced PIKE-lets

Small freeform yeast cakes with a holey top, toasted and buttered.

1 hr 30 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 pikelets

  • No chili heat

History

In England a pikelet is a small yeast griddle cake, close to a crumpet but cooked without a ring. The batter sits in a free puddle, so the cake stays flatter and wider than a ring-bound crumpet. The name is used differently in Australia, and that cake has its own page. This one is the Midlands and northern English pikelet.

Cultural background

Pikelets belong to tea tables and breakfasts. They are toasted, then buttered, so the butter runs into the holes. Some home recipes now use baking powder. This one keeps the yeast.

Why it tastes this way

Yeast and a loose batter make an open, damp top. The bottom browns against the griddle. Toasting dries the surface just enough for butter to melt in. The taste is mildly sour from the yeast, not sweet unless you add jam.

How it is made

A yeast batter of flour, milk, and water rises, then a pinch of baking soda is stirred in. Spoonfuls cook on a greased griddle until the top is set and honeycombed.

Authentic touch

Do not use crumpet rings for this page. Spoon the batter freely. If the top is not full of holes, the batter is too thick or the pan is too hot.

How it is used

Toasted and buttered, with jam or honey if you want them sweet. Honey adds a honey allergen and means the plate is not vegan, which this batter was not anyway.

Field notes

  • English pikelets are cooked without rings. Crumpets use rings.
  • Australian pikelets are a different cake and a different page.
  • The holes are for melted butter, which is why they are toasted.

Ingredients

  • 225 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tsp instant yeast
  • 1 tsp sugar
  • 250 ml warm milk
  • 100 ml warm water
  • 1 large egg
  • 1/2 tsp fine salt
  • 1/2 tsp baking soda
  • Butter, for the griddle and for serving

Steps

  1. Stir the flour, yeast, sugar, warm milk, water, egg, and salt into a batter like thick cream. Cover and leave until bubbly, about 1 hour.
  2. Dissolve the baking soda in a spoon of water and stir it in. The batter should look busy with bubbles. Rest 10 minutes.
  3. Grease a griddle over medium heat. Spoon on small rounds and cook until the top is full of holes and no longer liquid.
  4. Turn and brown the other side briefly. Cool on a cloth. Toast later and butter them while hot.

The batter should smell yeasty. If it smells rotten, discard it.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A baking-powder pikelet, faster, with a different flavor
  • Cheese melted on a toasted pikelet

Pairings

  • Cold butter
  • Jam
  • Strong tea
