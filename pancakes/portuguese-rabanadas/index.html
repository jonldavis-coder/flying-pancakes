Wine-or-milk soaked bread, fried, with sugar and cinnamon.

History

Rabanadas are Portuguese fried sliced bread, a Christmas dessert related to pain perdu and to Spanish torrijas. Stale bread is dipped in milk or wine, then egg, fried, and coated with sugar and cinnamon. They are holiday food, not a weekday pancake stack. The cinnamon-sugar crust is the Portuguese signature.

Cultural background

A sweet loaf or a dry country loaf both appear in recipes. The bread must be stale. Families fry them ahead of Christmas Eve and keep them for a day or two.

Why it tastes this way

Cinnamon and sugar on hot fried bread taste like a holiday because that is when the dish is made, when eggs and sugar were spent on a feast. Milk softens the crumb. A splash of port or sweet wine, if you use it, perfumes the soak. This version uses milk so the recipe has a clear non-alcoholic path. The fry gives a crust that plain French toast, cooked in a thin film of butter, does not.

How it is made

Stale bread is dipped in sweetened milk, then egg, fried, and tossed in cinnamon sugar.

Authentic touch

Use bread that is at least a day old, and let the sugar-cinnamon coat the slices while they are still hot and slightly oily so it sticks.

How it is used

Warm or at room temperature, as a Christmas sweet.

Field notes

  • Rabanadas are a Christmas bread fritter in Portugal.
  • Stale bread is required, as with other lost-bread dishes.

Ingredients

  • 8 slices stale bread
  • 1 cup milk
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1 teaspoon vanilla
  • 2 large eggs, beaten
  • Oil for frying
  • 1/4 cup sugar
  • 1 teaspoon cinnamon

Steps

  1. Stir milk, 2 tablespoons sugar, and vanilla. Dip the bread and let it soften briefly.
  2. Dip in egg.
  3. Fry in a shallow layer of oil over medium until golden.
  4. Mix the remaining sugar and cinnamon and coat the hot slices.
  5. Drain on a rack.
  6. Serve warm or the same day.

Shallow-fry over steady heat.

This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A little port in the milk is traditional. It is alcoholic.
  • Spanish torrijas are the close cousin, often associated with Lent as well as other feasts.

Pairings

  • coffee
