Arriving in Mexico City

Mexican hotcakes

You come in by Plane. Mexico · Mexico & Central America.

Pronounced HOT-kayks

Soft wheat cakes with browned butter, finished with cajeta, honey, or condensed milk.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 hotcakes

  • Wheat batter
  • No chili heat
  • Vegetarian

History

Wheat hotcakes spread through Mexican breakfasts in the twentieth century, when baking powder, packaged flour, and diner griddles showed up on fonda menus. A morning cook still turns them out beside coffee and eggs. Corn tortillas and other masa cakes are much older in Mexico. These hotcakes are a later wheat batter. They are not an ancient recipe, and they are not a corn cake under a new name.

Cultural background

In a fonda, hotcakes are ordinary city breakfast, not a festival dish. The plate is familiar in Mexico City and in towns across the country, usually sweetened at the table rather than in the bowl.

Why it tastes this way

The crumb tastes of wheat and browned butter, and baking powder keeps it soft if you stop stirring once the flour disappears. Cajeta, goat-milk caramel, is tangy and dark. Sweetened condensed milk is plain and very sweet. Honey stays floral. The topping does as much work as the batter, which is why a stack without one of them tastes unfinished.

How it is made

Whisk milk, egg, and melted butter. Stir them into the dry ingredients only until the flour is wet. Cook small rounds in a buttered skillet until the bubbles stay open, then flip once.

Authentic touch

Rest the batter for five minutes, then leave the lumps alone. A smooth, overworked batter makes a tough cake. The griddle is ready when a drop of water skitters.

How it is used

Serve straight from the pan at breakfast, with cajeta, honey, or condensed milk, and coffee. This is a fonda plate, not a street antojito.

Field notes

  • Baking powder is a nineteenth-century tool, and this Mexican breakfast cake follows that later wheat style.
  • Cajeta is goat milk cooked with sugar. It is not the same jar as cow-milk dulce de leche.
  • Corn cakes were already the daily griddle food of Mexico long before these hotcakes arrived.

Ingredients

  • 150 g wheat flour
  • 2 teaspoons baking powder
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 240 ml milk
  • 1 egg
  • 2 tablespoons melted butter, plus more for the skillet
  • Cajeta, for serving

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, baking powder, sugar, and salt in a bowl.
  2. Whisk the milk, egg, and melted butter in a second bowl, then pour them into the flour and stir only until the dry streaks disappear.
  3. Rest the batter for 5 minutes. Heat a skillet over medium heat and film it with butter.
  4. Pour about 60 ml of batter for each cake. Cook until the bubbles stay open and the underside is browned, then flip and cook the second side.
  5. Serve at once with cajeta. Honey or sweetened condensed milk can go on at the table instead.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Honey or sweetened condensed milk can replace the cajeta.
  • A spoon of orange zest in the batter is a home variation.
  • Whole wheat flour makes a heartier cake and still contains wheat.

Pairings

  • Café de olla or black coffee
  • Eggs on the same breakfast plate
  • Sliced fruit beside the stack
