Australian pikelets

You come in by Hot air balloon. Australia · Oceania.

Pronounced PIKE-lets

Small, thick drop pancakes with a soft crumb, eaten with butter and jam at tea.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · About 16 small pikelets

History

Australian pikelets are small thick pancakes dropped by the spoon, a British inheritance on the tea table. Drop scones and pikelets traveled with British settlers and stayed as afternoon-tea food, the kind made for a visit or a church hall. They are not the large thin pancake. That cake has its own page, with a looser batter, a wider pan, and lemon and sugar. Pikelets are the small ones, buttered and jammed.

Cultural background

A pikelet pan is an ordinary frying pan and a tablespoon. The batter is stiff enough that the spoonful keeps a small round and rises thick. Jam and butter are the spread. They are home food more than restaurant food, and they are not an Indigenous Australian dish.

Why it tastes this way

Baking powder lifts a thick batter fast, so the crumb is soft and cakey. Egg and milk taste rich. Sugar in the batter is light, and the jam supplies the fruit. Because the cakes are small, the browned face is a big part of each bite. They do not taste of lemon, and they are not rolled.

How it is made

Stir flour, baking powder, sugar, and salt, then fold in egg, milk, and melted butter to a thick batter. Drop tablespoons onto a moderate griddle and cook until bubbles hold and the underside is brown. Flip once. Serve with butter and jam.

Authentic touch

Drop them small and keep the batter thick. If the cakes spread to the edge of a wide pan, you have left pikelet territory and started the larger pancake. Butter and jam belong on the tea plate.

How it is used

Pikelets are a tea-table snack, not a plated dinner dessert and not a savory meal. Butter and jam are the usual spreads. Cream is a richer extra and still dairy, which the batter already contains.

Field notes

  • Pikelets are small and thick. They are not the large Australian pancake.
  • They come from British drop-scone cooking and sit on the tea table.
  • Butter and jam are the usual spread.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup wheat flour (about 125 g)
  • 2 teaspoons baking powder
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon fine salt
  • 1 egg
  • 3/4 cup milk
  • 1 tablespoon melted unsalted butter, plus more for the pan

Steps

  1. Stir the flour, baking powder, sugar, and salt in a bowl.
  2. Add the egg, milk, and melted butter. Stir to a thick batter with a few small lumps left. Do not thin it into a crepe.
  3. Heat a griddle over medium heat and butter it lightly. Drop tablespoons of batter. They should stay small and thick.
  4. Cook until bubbles sit on the surface and the bottoms are brown, then flip once and cook the second side.
  5. Serve warm with butter and jam. These are the tea pikelets, not the larger lemon-and-sugar pancakes.

The batter contains wheat, egg, and dairy. Do not confuse these with a gluten-free cake.

Pikelets are best the day they are cooked. Wrap leftovers and reheat them briefly in a covered pan. Refrigerate if you are keeping them overnight. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A spoon of golden syrup in the batter makes a deeper sweetness. This batch uses sugar.
  • Whole-wheat flour makes a heartier pikelet and still contains wheat.
  • Lemon and sugar is the finish for the larger Australian pancake, not for these.

Pairings

  • Butter
  • Jam
  • Tea
