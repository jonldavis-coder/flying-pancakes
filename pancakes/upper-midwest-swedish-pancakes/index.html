Arriving in Minneapolis

Upper Midwest Swedish pancakes

You come in by Train. United States · North America.

Thin, eggy, and lightly sweet, rolled around butter and jam.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 10 thin pancakes

History

These are the thin egg pancakes of Swedish-American kitchens in Minneapolis and the Upper Midwest. The batter is close to a crepe and is usually rolled or folded with butter and jam, often lingonberry. This is the United States page, the cake at church breakfasts and home tables. It is not the page for the Swedish original.

Cultural background

Scandinavian settlement put this breakfast on Upper Midwest tables. The cakes did not replace Indigenous food in the region. They are one immigrant griddle among others. Lingonberry jam is the familiar preserve, and raspberry jam is the common stand-in.

Why it tastes this way

More egg and milk than a diner pancake makes the cake thin and custardy. A resting batter spreads evenly and tastes less floury. Butter in the pan is the browning you taste at the edge of the roll.

How it is made

Thin egg batter swirled in a buttered skillet and rolled.

Authentic touch

Cook them thin enough to roll. A thick, baking-powder pancake is the diner stack, not this page. Lingonberry jam is the preserve most tied to the Swedish-American table.

How it is used

Rolled or folded, with butter and lingonberry or raspberry jam.

Field notes

  • This is the Swedish-American pancake of Minneapolis and the Upper Midwest, not the Swedish original.
  • Church-basement breakfasts helped keep the thin pancakes on the regional table.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 3 large eggs
  • 1 1/2 cups whole milk
  • 2 tablespoons unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the pan

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, sugar, salt, eggs, milk, and melted butter into a thin batter. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Heat an 8-inch skillet over medium heat and coat it with butter.
  3. Pour about 1/4 cup batter and swirl the pan so the batter covers the bottom.
  4. Cook until the top looks dry and the underside is golden in spots. Flip if you can, or slide the cake out if it is already set.
  5. Roll each cake with a little butter and jam, or fold them on a plate.
  6. Keep finished cakes warm in a low oven while you cook the rest.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A squeeze of lemon and a dusting of sugar is a plainer finish.
  • Whipped cream is a festive table addition and is still dairy.

Pairings

  • Lingonberry jam
  • Butter
  • Raspberry jam
