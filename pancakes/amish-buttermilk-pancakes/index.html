History

Amish and Mennonite communities in Pennsylvania, Ohio, and Indiana kept farmhouse baking into the present: buttermilk, eggs, and flour, cooked on a griddle for a large household. Their pancakes are not a secret recipe. They are a careful version of the American buttermilk pancake, often served in big breakfasts with fried potatoes, eggs, and fruit preserves. This page describes that table without turning a living religious community into a costume.

Cultural background

The cooking is practical. Batters are big. Sweetness is usually at the table, as syrup, sorghum, or fruit butter, because the same breakfast feeds people who will work.

Why it tastes this way

Buttermilk and soda make a high cake that still tastes a little sour, which is what keeps a plain pancake from tasting like sweet bread. A farm that had a cow had buttermilk. The lack of vanilla and spice is a choice: the cake is fuel and comfort, and the preserves do the perfuming.

How it is made

Flour, buttermilk, egg, and leavener are stirred briefly and cooked as a generous stack.

Authentic touch

Cook them on a steady medium griddle. A smoking pan makes a bitter crust and a raw middle, and a big batch punishes a cook who rushes.

How it is used

Butter and fruit preserves, sorghum, or maple syrup.

Field notes

  • This is a farmhouse buttermilk pancake associated with Amish and Mennonite breakfasts, not a claim of a single secret recipe.
  • Preserves and sorghum often matter more than anything mixed into the batter.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups all-purpose flour
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1 1/2 teaspoons baking powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 1/2 cups buttermilk
  • 1 large egg
  • 3 tablespoons melted butter

Steps

  1. Stir the dry ingredients together, then stir in the wet ingredients until the batter is combined.
  2. Stir until the flour is just wet. Rest 5 minutes.
  3. Heat a griddle or heavy skillet over medium heat and film it with butter.
  4. Spoon on the batter. Cook until the underside is browned and the top looks set.
  5. Flip once and cook the second side through.
  6. Keep the cooked cakes warm while you finish the batch, and serve them together.

This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A cup of cornmeal in a double batch leans toward a corn cake.
  • Vanilla is a diner addition. It is not needed.

Pairings

  • fruit preserves
  • butter
  • sorghum
