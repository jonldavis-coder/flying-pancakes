A plain cake whose point is real maple syrup.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 pancakes

History

Maple syrup is made by boiling the sap of maple trees, a practice of the Native nations of the northeastern woodlands long before Vermont’s sugarhouses. European-descended farmers turned sugaring into a late-winter farm season. The wheat pancake under the syrup is a later New England breakfast. Vermont’s reputation is the syrup, which is graded and sold as an agricultural product, not a flavoring.

Cultural background

Sugar-on-snow and sugarhouse breakfasts are the seasonal frame. At home, the cake is simple so the syrup can be tasted.

Why it tastes this way

Maple tastes like the tree and the boil: caramel, wood, and acid, stronger in darker grades. A very sweet batter would hide that. The climate of a cold late winter is why the sap runs at all, when nights freeze and days thaw. The pancake is only the warm bread underneath.

How it is made

A lightly sweetened buttermilk pancake is cooked plain and served with maple syrup.

Authentic touch

Warm the syrup. Cold syrup sits on a hot cake and tastes less aromatic. Use syrup you trust is maple, not pancake syrup made from corn syrup and flavoring.

How it is used

Butter and maple syrup. Sausage is common and is a separate meat dish.

Field notes

  • Pancake syrup and maple syrup are different products. This plate wants maple.
  • Indigenous sugaring in the Northeast is older than the Vermont wheat pancake.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup buttermilk
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons melted butter
  • Maple syrup, to serve

Steps

  1. Stir the dry ingredients together, then stir in the wet ingredients until the batter is combined.
  2. Rest the batter 5 minutes. Do not sweeten it like cake.
  3. Heat a griddle or heavy skillet over medium heat and film it with butter.
  4. Spoon on the batter. Cook until the underside is browned and the top looks set.
  5. Flip once and cook the second side through.
  6. Serve with warm maple syrup.

This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A teaspoon of maple syrup in the batter is enough if you want the flavor inside. Do not replace the table syrup with it.
  • Buckwheat cakes with maple are an older northern pairing.

Pairings

  • butter
