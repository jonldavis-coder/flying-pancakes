Lacy cornbread

You come in by Train. United States · North America.

Crisp, salty corn with a brittle lace of holes and a tender spot in the middle.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 skillet cakes

History

Lacy cornbread is a very thin cornmeal batter cooked in a hot skillet until the surface opens into crisp holes. It belongs to Appalachian corn cooking, especially in West Virginia and southwestern Virginia. It is a griddle bread, closer to a crisp cake than to a deep pan of cornbread. The batter is meal, water, and salt.

Cultural background

Mountain cooks poured this batter into a hot greased skillet when they wanted cornbread in a hurry. The lace is what the heat does to a thin batter, not a separate ingredient.

Why it tastes this way

Extra water lets the batter spider across the pan instead of sitting in a mound. Hot fat fries the strands as they spread, so the cake tastes toasted and crisp. Coarse meal keeps a corn flavor that wheat cornbread would soften.

How it is made

Poured thin into a very hot oiled skillet so the batter laces.

Authentic touch

The batter should look thin, almost like cream. If it sits in a thick puddle, add water. The lace is the test that you got it right.

How it is used

Whole from the skillet, broken at the table, often with beans or greens.

Field notes

  • The holes are the batter frying as it spreads, which is why cooks call it lacy.
  • It is closer to a crisp griddle cake than to a square of baked cornbread.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup cornmeal
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 3/4 cups water
  • 3 tablespoons neutral oil, divided

Steps

  1. Whisk the cornmeal, salt, water, and 1 tablespoon of the oil into a thin batter. It should run off the spoon.
  2. Rest the batter 5 minutes and whisk again.
  3. Heat a small skillet over medium-high heat. Add 1 1/2 teaspoons of oil and let it get hot.
  4. Pour in about 1/3 cup batter and tilt the pan. The batter should spread and break into holes.
  5. Cook until the top looks dry and the underside is brown and crisp, then flip carefully and brown the other side.
  6. Repeat with the remaining oil and batter. Serve hot.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Bacon fat in place of oil is common in kitchens that cook pork.
  • A spoon of wheat flour stops the lace and makes a different bread.

Pairings

  • Pot beans
  • Cooked greens
  • Butter, if you eat dairy
