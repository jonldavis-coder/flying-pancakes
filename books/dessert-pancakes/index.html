Æbleskiver

Spherical pancakes, browned in a special pan, dusted with sugar.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · about 28 spheres

History

Æbleskiver are Danish round pancakes cooked in a cast-iron pan with hemispherical wells. The name means apple slices, and some recipes tuck in a bit of apple, though many modern æbleskiver have no apple inside. They are a Christmas and winter treat in Denmark and at Danish American church events. The pan is essential.

Cultural background

Powdered sugar and jam, especially raspberry, are the finish. They are turned with a knitting needle or a skewer so the sphere browns all over.

Why it tastes this way

The well traps steam and batter so the cake becomes a ball instead of a disk. A little cardamom is a common Danish perfume. Sugar on the outside clings to the crisp shell. The apple in the name records an older filling. Leaving the apple out is common now and should be admitted, not hidden.

How it is made

A thick batter is cooked in an æbleskiver pan, turned until round, and sugared.

Authentic touch. Fill the wells two-thirds full. Turn the cakes before the batter on top is completely dry, so the seam seals into a ball. Too late, and you get a hemisphere.

How it is used

Hot, with powdered sugar and jam.

Field notes

  • The name refers to apples, but many æbleskiver today have none inside.
  • Danish American festivals still cook them as heritage food.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1/4 teaspoon cardamom
  • 1 large egg, separated
  • 1 cup milk
  • 2 tablespoons melted butter
  • Powdered sugar, to serve

Steps

  1. Mix the batter with the yolk. Whip the white and fold it in.
  2. Butter an æbleskiver pan over medium heat.
  3. Fill the wells two-thirds full.
  4. Turn the cakes with a skewer as they set, until they are round and cooked through.
  5. Dust with powdered sugar.
  6. Serve with jam.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A small piece of cooked apple in the center is the older idea.
  • Dutch poffertjes use a similar pan and a different batter.

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Holiday table
  • Breakfast
  • powdered sugar
  • raspberry jam
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Åland Pancake

A baked rice and cardamom pancake from the Åland Islands.

30 min prep · 45 min cook · Serves 8 · one 8-inch dish

History

Åland pancake is a baked rice porridge pancake from the Åland Islands, which are Swedish-speaking and autonomous within Finland. Cardamom and rice porridge are the base. It is a local dessert and café food, served with jam, often prune jam, and whipped cream. It is distinct from a thin lettu and from Gotland’s saffron pancake, though both are baked rice cakes of the Baltic.

Cultural background

The porridge is cooked, cooled a little, enriched, and baked until the top browns. Prune jam is the usual partner. Another dark jam is a fair substitute if you name it honestly.

Why it tastes this way

Rice cooked in milk tastes sweet and substantial, and cardamom perfumes it the way it perfumes Nordic buns. Åland’s position between Sweden and Finland shows in the language and in this cake, which sits beside both countries’ oven pancakes and keeps its own name. Prune jam is tart and dark, a preserved fruit that stands up to cream.

How it is made

Rice porridge is mixed with egg, sugar, and cardamom and baked in a buttered dish.

Authentic touch. Cook the rice fully before baking, or the finished cake has hard grains. The bake is for setting and browning, not for cooking raw rice.

How it is used

With prune jam or another dark jam, and whipped cream.

Field notes

  • Åland pancake is a named island dish, not a generic baked custard.
  • Prune jam is the traditional fruit partner.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup short-grain rice
  • 2 cups milk
  • 2 large eggs
  • 1/4 cup sugar
  • 1 teaspoon cardamom
  • 1 tablespoon flour
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • Butter for the dish

Steps

  1. Simmer rice and milk until the rice is soft and the porridge is thick. Cool 10 minutes.
  2. Stir in eggs, sugar, cardamom, flour, and salt.
  3. Pour into a buttered dish.
  4. Bake at 375°F about 40 minutes until set and brown.
  5. Rest 10 minutes.
  6. Serve with prune jam and cream.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Gotland’s pancake uses saffron. This one uses cardamom. Do not treat them as one recipe.
  • A gluten-free version can skip the spoon of flour. The rice is the body.

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Breakfast
  • prune jam
  • whipped cream
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Appelpannenkoek

A plate-sized pancake with tender apple cooked into it.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 2 · 2 large pancakes

History

An appelpannenkoek is a Dutch pancake with apple, either sliced into the batter as it cooks or sautéed and laid on the cake. It is a standard sweet pannenkoek, served as a meal or a hearty dessert. Cinnamon is common and not universal.

Cultural background

Apple varieties that hold their shape are the right fruit. The cake is large, like other pannenkoeken, and is eaten with syrup or sugar.

Why it tastes this way

Apple sweetens the cake and adds water, so the center stays soft while the edge crisps. Dutch cooking puts fruit in the pancake rather than only beside it, which is the difference from a plain pannenkoek with applesauce. A cool climate apple, tart rather than a dessert banana, keeps the richness in check.

How it is made

A pannenkoek batter is cooked with thin apple slices set into it, then flipped.

Authentic touch. Slice the apple thin and press the slices into the batter at the start of cooking. Thick chunks stay raw when the cake is brown.

How it is used

Powdered sugar or stroop, and a little cinnamon if you like.

Field notes

  • The apple goes in the cake, not only on the side.
  • Keep the slices thin so they cook with the batter.

Ingredients

  • 3/4 cup flour
  • 1 large egg
  • 3/4 cup milk
  • Pinch of salt
  • 1 apple, thinly sliced
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • Butter for the pan

Steps

  1. Whisk flour, egg, milk, and salt.
  2. Butter a skillet and lay in the apple slices. Sprinkle with sugar.
  3. Pour the batter over the apples and cook covered on medium until set.
  4. Flip and brown the apple side.
  5. The fruit should be tender.
  6. Serve with sugar or stroop.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pear works the same way and should be called a pear pancake.
  • Raisins with the apple are a home addition.

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Supper
  • powdered sugar
  • stroop
  • cinnamon
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Argentine Panqueques

Thin crepes filled with dulce de leche.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 crepes

History

Panqueques in Argentina are thin crepes, a standard dessert when filled with dulce de leche. Dulce de leche is milk cooked with sugar until it is caramel, a Río de la Plata sweet shared with Uruguay. The crepe is the wrapper. The filling is the national flavor.

Cultural background

They are rolled and sometimes flambéed in restaurants. This recipe does not flame them. It warms the dulce de leche and rolls the crepes.

Why it tastes this way

Dulce de leche tastes of cooked milk sugar, richer and less tangy than goat’s-milk cajeta. A thin egg crepe is mild enough to carry it. The dessert is a café and home sweet of the twentieth century, French technique and a local caramel. Lemon and sugar would be a different country’s crepe.

How it is made

Thin crepes are spread with dulce de leche and rolled.

Authentic touch. Warm the dulce de leche so it spreads. Cold caramel tears the crepe. Do not boil it.

How it is used

Warm, as dessert.

Field notes

  • Dulce de leche is the filling that makes the crepe Argentine.
  • This recipe warms the caramel and does not flame it.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup flour
  • 2 eggs
  • 1 1/4 cups milk
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • Pinch of salt
  • 1 tablespoon melted butter
  • 3/4 cup dulce de leche

Steps

  1. Cook thin crepes.
  2. Warm the dulce de leche until it spreads.
  3. Fill and roll.
  4. Serve warm.
  5. Do not boil the caramel.
  6. A thin ribbon of filling is enough.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A sprinkle of coconut is a home finish.
  • Chilean panqueques are a close relative.

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • dulce de leche
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Asturian Frixuelos

Thin carnival pancakes from Asturias, finished with sugar or honey.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 10 frixuelos

History

Frixuelos are the thin pancakes of Asturias, especially at carnival. The batter is flour, egg, and milk, close to Galician filloas and to other Spanish carnival crepes. Honey or sugar is the finish. They are a regional name worth keeping beside filloas rather than folded into one generic Spanish pancake.

Cultural background

Families cook a stack. The cake is thin. Anís appears in some batters and is left out here so the recipe is alcohol-free.

Why it tastes this way

Egg and milk are the richness before Lent, the carnival pattern. Asturias’s dairy is why the batter tastes of milk rather than of water. Sugar or honey is enough. The mountain and coastal kitchens of the region both claim them. The method is a crepe. The name is local.

How it is made

A thin batter is cooked as crepes and finished with sugar or honey.

Authentic touch. A small pan and a thin pour. Rest the batter. Butter, not a deep fry, is the fat.

How it is used

Stacked, warm.

Field notes

  • Frixuelos are Asturias’s carnival crepes.
  • This batter has no alcohol.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup flour
  • 2 eggs
  • 1 1/4 cups milk
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • Pinch of salt
  • Butter for the pan
  • Honey, to serve

Steps

  1. Whisk and rest the batter 15 minutes.
  2. Cook thin pancakes in butter.
  3. Serve stacked with honey or sugar.
  4. Do not make them thick.
  5. Eat them the day they are made.
  6. A small pan is easier.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A splash of anise liqueur is a flavored variation and is alcoholic.
  • Filloas are the Galician kin.

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Holiday table
  • sugar
  • honey
  • Vegetarian

Austrian Palatschinken

Thin crepes filled with apricot jam.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 crepes

History

Palatschinken are Austria’s thin pancakes, close to Hungarian palacsinta and to other Central European crepes. Apricot jam is the filling most often met in Vienna. They are rolled or folded and dusted with sugar. Kaiserschmarrn is the torn cousin, not the same technique.

Cultural background

They are a home dessert and a café dessert. The batter is simple. The jam should be good because there is nowhere to hide.

Why it tastes this way

Apricot orchards and apricot jam are an Austrian flavor, sharper than strawberry. The crepe is mild and eggy, a wrapper. Powdered sugar on the outside adds a dry sweetness that jam alone does not. The shared Habsburg geography explains why the name sits so close to palacsinta.

How it is made

Thin pancakes are rolled around apricot jam and sugared.

Authentic touch. Warm the jam so it spreads. Cold jam tears a thin crepe.

How it is used

Warm, with a little more sugar.

Field notes

  • Apricot jam is the classic Viennese filling.
  • The name is a cousin of Hungarian palacsinta, and the dishes are still each their own.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup flour
  • 2 large eggs
  • 1 cup milk
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • Pinch of salt
  • 1 tablespoon melted butter
  • 1/2 cup apricot jam

Steps

  1. Whisk the batter and rest 10 minutes.
  2. Cook thin crepes.
  3. Spread with warm jam and roll.
  4. Dust with sugar.
  5. Serve warm.
  6. Keep the pan lightly buttered.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Chocolate sauce is a café flourish.
  • Kaiserschmarrn is the torn pancake on the previous page.

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Stuffed or filled
  • apricot jam
  • powdered sugar

Bosnian Palačinke

Thin crepes filled with jam or a walnut-sugar mix.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 crepes

History

Palačinke are a standard dessert in Bosnia and Herzegovina, filled with jam or with ground walnuts and sugar, and sold in simple restaurants as well as made at home. This recipe uses jam. The walnut filling is noted as a variation because it is a tree nut.

Cultural background

They are often folded in quarters. A glass of yogurt or a coffee finishes the order.

Why it tastes this way

The crepe is light and the filling is sweet, a cheap and loved dessert. Walnut fillings taste of local nuts and sugar. Jam is the filling that keeps the cake accessible to someone who does not eat nuts. Mineral water in some Bosnian batters lightens the texture, the same trick used next door.

How it is made

Thin crepes are cooked and filled with jam, then folded.

Authentic touch. Fold in quarters rather than a tight roll if the crepe is delicate. The shape is a matter of the shop, not of authenticity police.

How it is used

Warm, with coffee or yogurt.

Field notes

  • Palačinke are an everyday Bosnian dessert, not only a holiday dish.
  • Jam is written here. Walnuts are the nut variation.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup flour
  • 2 large eggs
  • 1 1/4 cups milk
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • Pinch of salt
  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • Jam, to serve

Steps

  1. Whisk the batter and rest 10 minutes.
  2. Cook thin crepes.
  3. Fill with jam and fold into quarters.
  4. Serve warm.
  5. Yogurt on the side is optional.
  6. Do not overcook the second side.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Ground walnuts cooked briefly with sugar and a little milk are a classic nut filling.
  • Sparkling water can replace part of the milk.

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Stuffed or filled
  • jam
  • yogurt
  • Vegetarian

Bulgarian Palachinki

Thin pancakes with jam, honey, or white cheese.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 crepes

History

Palachinki are Bulgarian crepes, part of the Balkan crepe family. They are eaten with jam, honey, walnuts, or a savory white cheese. This recipe uses honey and jam so the written cake stays nut-free. Sirene, the brined white cheese, is the savory option.

Cultural background

They are a home breakfast and a dessert. The batter is thin. Honey is a natural partner in a country with a strong beekeeping tradition.

Why it tastes this way

Honey tastes floral and less jammy than apricot, so a plain crepe with honey is a different plate from the Hungarian jam roll. White cheese makes the same batter savory and salty. Both uses are Bulgarian. The crepe technique is the regional one. The sweetener is the local choice.

How it is made

Thin crepes are folded and finished with honey or jam.

Authentic touch. If you use honey, drizzle it on the hot crepe so it thins. Cold honey sits in a lump.

How it is used

Warm, with honey or jam. Sirene is the savory alternative.

Field notes

  • Palachinki are the Bulgarian crepe.
  • Honey is a classic finish and is not the same plate as jam.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup flour
  • 2 large eggs
  • 1 cup milk
  • 1/4 cup water
  • Pinch of salt
  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • Honey, to serve

Steps

  1. Whisk a thin batter.
  2. Cook crepes in a lightly oiled pan.
  3. Fold and drizzle with honey.
  4. Serve warm.
  5. Jam can replace honey.
  6. Do not make them thick.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Walnuts and honey are a traditional combination and add tree nuts.
  • Sirene inside the fold makes them savory and saltier.

Pairings and uses

History

Chilean panqueques are thin dessert crepes, filled with manjar, the local name for dulce de leche. They are a home dessert, close to the Argentine plate and worth a separate page because the name manjar and the Chilean table are not identical to Buenos Aires. The batter is a standard crepe batter.

Cultural background

They are rolled. A dust of sugar finishes them. Orange zest in the batter is a home variation and is optional here.

Why it tastes this way

Manjar is sweet, thick, and milky, so the crepe stays thin and not very sweet. The dessert is the same Atlantic idea as Argentine panqueques with a Pacific name. Butter in the pan is the browned note. The filling does the rest.

How it is made

Thin crepes are filled with manjar and rolled.

Authentic touch. Spread a thin ribbon of manjar. A heavy hand makes the roll slide apart.

How it is used

Warm.

Field notes

  • In Chile the caramel is often called manjar.
  • The crepe is the wrapper. The manjar is the flavor.

Ingredients

  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • Pinch of salt
  • 1 tablespoon melted butter
  • 3/4 cup manjar

Steps

  1. Cook thin crepes.
  2. Spread with manjar and roll.
  3. Dust with a little sugar if you like.
  4. Serve warm.
  5. Do not overfill.
  6. Eat them the day they are made.

Mexico · Mexico & Central America

Crepas de Cajeta

Thin crepes in goat’s-milk caramel.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 crepes

History

Sweet crepes with cajeta are a Mexican dessert-café standard. Cajeta is a caramel of goat’s milk, famously associated with Celaya. The crepe is the French technique. The sauce is Mexican. Together they are a real menu item, not a home invention of this guide.

Cultural background

The crepes are thin. The cajeta is warmed and thinned with a spoon of milk so it can coat. Chopped nuts are optional and are a tree nut if you add them.

Why it tastes this way

Goat’s milk caramel tastes tangier than cow’s-milk dulce de leche, which is the point of cajeta. A thin crepe folds into the sauce and tastes of browned butter and caramel. The pairing is a café dessert of the twentieth century, French technique and Mexican dairy sweet on one plate.

How it is made

Thin crepes are folded with warmed cajeta.

Authentic touch. Warm the cajeta with a splash of milk until it flows. Thick cajeta tears a crepe. Fill and fold, then give them a moment in the pan.

How it is used

Warm, as dessert.

Field notes

  • Cajeta is goat’s-milk caramel, tangier than cow’s-milk dulce de leche.
  • This dessert does not ask you to flame the sauce.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup flour
  • 2 eggs
  • 1 1/4 cups milk
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • Pinch of salt
  • 1 tablespoon melted butter
  • 3/4 cup cajeta
  • 2 tablespoons milk, to thin the cajeta

Steps

  1. Make thin crepes and stack them.
  2. Warm the cajeta with the milk until it flows.
  3. Spread and fold the crepes.
  4. Warm them briefly in the pan.
  5. Serve immediately.
  6. Do not boil the cajeta.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pecans on top are common and add tree nuts.
  • Cajeta from a jar is the practical ingredient. A from-scratch caramel is a longer project.

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • cajeta
  • Vegetarian

France · Western Europe

Crêpes Suzette

Wheat crêpes in orange butter, with a citrus sauce.

40 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 crêpes

History

Crêpes Suzette are thin crêpes in an orange butter sauce, a restaurant dessert rather than a Breton farm cake. The origin stories, including who Suzette was, are disputed and are left out here. What is solid is the dish: crêpes, butter, sugar, and orange, flambéed in some dining rooms. This home version skips the tableside flame and reduces the orange sauce instead, which is safer and still the flavor.

Cultural background

It is a dessert course, made from the sweet crêpe batter. Grand Marnier or another orange liqueur is traditional in the sauce. The alcohol cooks off if you simmer it. You can leave it out.

Why it tastes this way

Orange zest’s oil is the perfume, and butter carries it. Sugar and a reduction make a sauce that clings to a thin crêpe instead of soaking it into porridge. The dish tastes like a restaurant because it is one: a plain crêpe dressed after the fact. Skipping the flame does not remove the orange. It removes a fire hazard.

How it is made

Sweet crêpes are folded into a butter and orange sauce and warmed through.

Authentic touch. Zest the orange before you juice it. Reduce the sauce until it coats a spoon, then add the crêpes and turn them. If the sauce is watery, the dessert tastes like juice.

How it is used

Immediately, as dessert, with the sauce spooned over.

Field notes

  • The dedication stories are disputed, so this page does not repeat them as fact.
  • This home recipe does not ask you to set alcohol on fire.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 2 large eggs
  • 1 1/4 cups milk
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • Pinch of salt
  • 1 tablespoon melted butter
  • 4 tablespoons butter, for the sauce
  • 3 tablespoons sugar, for the sauce
  • Zest and juice of 1 orange

Steps

  1. Make the crêpe batter, rest 30 minutes, and cook thin crêpes.
  2. In a wide pan, melt 4 tablespoons butter with 3 tablespoons sugar, the zest, and the juice.
  3. Simmer until the sauce looks syrupy.
  4. Add the folded crêpes and turn them in the sauce for a minute.
  5. Serve at once.
  6. Do not flame the sauce for this version.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A tablespoon of orange liqueur in the sauce is traditional. Simmer it.
  • Lemon is a sharper, less Suzette finish.

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • orange

History

Swedish immigrants in Minnesota, Wisconsin, and neighboring states kept the thin pancake, pannkaka, and the even smaller plättar. The scale is American community cooking.

Cultural background

A special pan with round wells makes plättar. A ordinary skillet makes larger thin pancakes that are rolled. Both appear at the same suppers.

Why it tastes this way

More egg and a thinner batter than a fluffy American pancake give a crepe-like bite that rolls without breaking. Lingonberries are the tart northern fruit Swedes preserved, and imported preserves kept that taste on the prairie when the berry itself did not grow in the church kitchen. Butter and sugar, the other topping, is the plain home version.

How it is made

A thin egg batter is cooked like crepes, then rolled or stacked with preserves.

How it is used

Lingonberry preserves, or butter and sugar. Whipped cream is a festive addition.

History

Plättar are small Swedish pancakes cooked in a plättpanna, a pan with several round wells. They are the miniature form of the Swedish pancake and a cousin of the larger pannkakor. Lingonberry jam is the classic partner. The Upper Midwest church-supper pancake is the emigrant version of this batter.

Cultural background

They are a weeknight dessert or a weekend breakfast. Without the special pan, a spoon of batter in a small skillet makes one cake at a time and is less charming and still the recipe.

Why it tastes this way

A thin, eggy batter cooks into a tender coin because the well is small and hot. Lingonberries are the tart forest fruit preserved as jam, and their acid is why a rich little cake does not taste cloying. Butter in the wells browns the edges. The pan, not a different flour, is what separates plättar from a plate-sized pancake.

How it is made

A thin egg batter is cooked in a plättpanna or æbleskiver-style pan and served with jam.

Authentic touch. Butter each well, not just the first one. The cakes stick the moment the fat is gone. Flip them with a skewer when the tops look dry.

How it is used

Lingonberry jam, or butter and sugar.

Sweet Crêpes

A thin wheat pancake with a tender, lightly sweet crumb.

40 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 10 crêpes

History

Crêpes are thin pancakes cooked across France. In Brittany they are a regional pride, and February 2, Chandeleur, is the candle-day associated with eating them. That date is Candlemas, not Shrove Tuesday, though both belong to the late-winter Christian calendar. Sweet crêpes are usually wheat. Savory buckwheat galettes are the other Breton cake and have their own page.

Cultural background

Street stands and home kitchens both cook them. Sugar, jam, chocolate, or a squeeze of lemon are ordinary finishes. Crêpes Suzette is a restaurant dish, not the everyday cake.

Why it tastes this way

A rested batter of flour, egg, milk, and butter spreads paper-thin and tastes of browned butter rather than of baking powder. Wheat, not buckwheat, is what makes a sweet crêpe pale and mild enough for sugar. Chandeleur explains a spike in home cooking. The rest of the year, the cake is simply a fast dessert.

How it is made

A thin wheat batter is swirled in a hot, lightly buttered pan and cooked briefly on both sides.

Authentic touch. Rest the batter at least 30 minutes, or the gluten snaps back and the crêpe tears. The first one seasons the pan and is rarely the prettiest.

How it is used

Sugar, jam, lemon, or a chocolate spread. Fold or roll.

Field notes

  • Chandeleur, February 2, is the French crêpe day most often named. It is not the same day as Shrove Tuesday.
  • Sweet crêpes are wheat. Galettes are buckwheat.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/4 cups milk
  • 2 tablespoons melted butter, plus more for the pan
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • Pinch of salt

Steps

  2. Heat a crêpe pan and film it with butter.
  3. Swirl a thin layer and cook until the edge browns.
  4. Flip for a few seconds.
  5. Fill or sugar, and fold.
  6. Keep finished crêpes warm under a plate.

Syrniki

Pan-fried farmer-cheese cakes, lightly sweet.

15 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 cakes

History

Syrniki are fried cakes of tvorog, the farmer cheese of Russia and Ukraine, bound with egg and a little flour. The name comes from syr, for the cheese. They are a breakfast across the region, served with sour cream, jam, or honey. They are not blintzes. The cheese is the cake, not a filling.

Cultural background

Tvorog is drier than many supermarket ricottas. If you use ricotta, drain it overnight or the cakes will not hold. That is a substitute, and it should be drained.

Why it tastes this way

Tvorog tastes mildly sour and milky, which is the flavor under the browned crust. A little sugar makes them breakfast-sweet without turning them into dessert pastries. Flour is only the binder. The cheese is there because dairy regions kept tvorog as a way to use milk. Sour cream on top doubles the dairy and softens the crust.

How it is made

Farmer cheese, egg, sugar, and a little flour are shaped into thick patties and fried.

Authentic touch. Drain the cheese until it is crumbly, not wet. Flour your hands. Fry on medium-low so the center heats before the crust blackens.

How it is used

Sour cream and jam or honey.

Field notes

  • Syrniki are cheese cakes, in the literal sense, not a New York cheesecake.
  • Wet ricotta must be drained or the patties collapse.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup well-drained tvorog or farmer cheese
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/4 cup flour, plus more for shaping
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • Oil or butter for frying

Steps

  1. Mash the cheese with egg, sugar, salt, and flour.
  2. Shape thick patties with floured hands.
  3. Fry over medium-low until deep golden on both sides and hot in the center.
  4. Serve with sour cream.
  5. They should not ooze liquid. If they do, the cheese was too wet.
  6. A few raisins can be mixed in.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Raisins in the cheese are common.
  • Ukrainian and Russian breakfasts both claim syrniki. The cake is shared.

Tiganites

History

Tiganites are Greek fried batter cakes, known by that name in medieval Greek sources and still made at home. They are closer to a fritter than to a flipped pancake, served with honey, nuts, or cinnamon. Honey and sesame or walnut are common. This version keeps the nuts on the side so the cake itself can be nut-free, and it says so.

Cultural background

They are a sweet snack, sometimes associated with holidays and sometimes simply with a jar of honey. Olive oil is a traditional frying fat. Neutral oil also works.

Why it tastes this way

Honey is the historic Greek sweetener, which is why the cake tastes floral and sticky rather than like powdered sugar. A simple flour-and-egg batter fries into something airy if the oil is hot enough. Cinnamon is a later trade spice that stuck. The fry, rather than a dry griddle, is the method the name points at: tigani is the frying pan.

How it is made

A thick batter is fried in spoonfuls and drizzled with honey and cinnamon.

Authentic touch. Fry spoonfuls in enough oil to come halfway up the cakes. A dry pan makes tough disks. Honey should go on while they are hot so it soaks the crust.

How it is used

Hot, with honey. Walnuts are traditional and are a tree nut if you add them.

  • Tiganites are an old Greek batter cake, fried rather than flipped dry.

Welsh Cakes

A currant girdle cake, crisp at the edge and soft like a scone.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 12 · about 12 cakes

History

Welsh cakes, picau ar y maen, are small rounds of a currant dough cooked on a bakestone or griddle. They are a tea cake, not a poured pancake, and they are one of the best-known Welsh griddle foods. The bakestone was a flat iron used on the hearth. Currants and a little spice are typical.

Cultural background

They are eaten plain or with butter, warm or cold, and they keep better than a crepe. Bake sales and family tins both rely on that.

Why it tastes this way

Fat rubbed into flour makes a short dough, and currants add a dark sweetness that traveled into Welsh cupboards as a dried fruit. The bakestone gives a spotted crust an oven does not. A pinch of spice, often mixed spice or nutmeg, is enough. Too much and they taste like a cookie rather than a cake from the griddle.

How it is made

A short dough with currants is rolled, cut into rounds, and cooked on a griddle.

Authentic touch. Cook them slower than pancakes. The dough is thick, and the currants burn if you rush. They are done when the sides look set and a central one, sacrificed to the cook, is not raw.

How it is used

Warm, with or without butter, with tea.

Field notes

  • Picau ar y maen means cakes on the stone, the bakestone.
  • They are a dough cooked on a griddle, which is why they sit with pancakes and are not pancakes.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1/4 teaspoon nutmeg
  • 1/4 cup sugar
  • 1/4 cup cold butter
  • 1/3 cup dried currants
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons milk

Steps

  1. Stir flour, baking powder, salt, nutmeg, and sugar. Rub in the butter.
  2. Add currants, egg, and milk to a soft dough.
  3. Roll 1/4 inch thick and cut rounds.
  4. Cook on a medium-low griddle, a few minutes a side, until browned and cooked through.
  5. Cool on a rack.
  6. Dust with a little sugar if you like.
