Aebleskiver

Round Danish pancakes from a molded pan, cardamom, jam, and powdered sugar.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · about 28 aebleskiver

History

Aebleskiver are round pancakes cooked in a special pan with hemispherical cups. The name means apple slices. Older versions tuck in a bit of apple. Many Danish kitchens now cook them plain, with cardamom, and serve jam and powdered sugar. They are especially common in December, and they are also eaten at other times. Church breakfasts in the American Upper Midwest are a later home of the pan, not the origin of the cake. This recipe is the Danish one.

Cultural background

The pan is the recipe's tool, as with Dutch poffertjes, and the batters are not the same. Aebleskiver are turned with a knitting needle or a skewer so a ball forms. They are Advent and Christmas food in many families now.

Why it tastes this way

Whipped egg whites and baking powder make them light. Cardamom perfumes the crumb. The crust browns in butter while the inside stays like a small popover. Powdered sugar and a tart jam are the contrast. Apple, if you add it, steams inside and tastes like the name.

How it is made

A batter of flour, buttermilk, cardamom, and separated eggs is cooked in a buttered aebleskiver pan, turned once into rounds, and served with jam and powdered sugar.

Authentic touch. Turn each cake before the center is dry, so the unset batter runs into the cup and makes a ball. A flat pan cannot do this. A piece of apple in the cup is the older filling. Leaving it out is the common modern cake.

How it is used

Hot, with jam and powdered sugar, at breakfast, as a snack, or through December. The American church breakfast is a later table, not the source.

Field notes

  • The name means apple slices, and many current recipes leave the apple out.
  • Upper Midwest church breakfasts are a later home, not the origin.
  • The ball shape comes from turning the cake in a molded pan.

Ingredients

  • 180 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tsp baking powder
  • 1/2 tsp ground cardamom
  • 2 tbsp sugar
  • 1/4 tsp fine salt
  • 2 large eggs, separated
  • 250 ml buttermilk
  • 40 g unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the pan
  • Jam and powdered sugar, for serving

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, baking powder, cardamom, sugar, and salt. Stir in the yolks, buttermilk, and melted butter.
  2. Beat the whites to soft peaks and fold them in.
  3. Heat an aebleskiver pan, butter the cups, and fill them almost full. Cook until the bottoms are brown.
  4. Turn each cake with a skewer and cook until the ball is set and browned. Serve with jam and powdered sugar.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A cube of apple in each cup
  • A yeasted batter, which is an older style than baking powder

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Dessert
  • Feast or fast day
  • Snack
  • Jam
  • Powdered sugar
  • A small piece of apple hidden in the batter
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Aegean akıtma

Thin Aegean semolina pancakes with a tangy crumb, finished with walnuts and syrup.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · About 8 thin pancakes

History

Akıtma is a semolina pancake from the Aegean coast of Turkey. The name sits next to the verb for pouring, and the batter is meant to be poured thin. Around Ayvalık, Izmir, and the nearby towns it is breakfast or a sweet snack, often with crushed walnuts or a light syrup. Semolina is wheat. Walnuts make this version a tree-nut dish. It is not gözleme, which is a stuffed flatbread, and it is not a layered katmer.

Cultural background

Aegean kitchens keep semolina, yogurt, and olive oil close at hand. Akıtma uses that pantry. Some houses eat it with cheese instead of walnuts. This page chooses the walnut and syrup finish and says so, because the allergen is different.

Why it tastes this way

Semolina gives a fine grit and a mild sweetness. Yogurt makes the batter tangy, and yeast lifts it without a long sour ferment. The pancake is thin, so the griddle browns a larger share of the crumb. Walnuts add bitterness and fat, and the syrup makes those pieces glossy. The Aegean habit is this contrast, not a stack of thick cakes.

How it is made

Whisk fine semolina, a little flour, yeast, yogurt, water, and salt into a pourable batter. Rest it until foamy. Cook thin rounds on an oiled griddle. Top with crushed walnuts and a warm sugar syrup.

Authentic touch. Pour the batter thin, in the Aegean way, and finish with walnuts. If you leave the nuts off, say so, because this written recipe is not nut-free. Cheese instead of walnuts is a real alternative and drops the tree-nut allergen only when the nuts are actually absent.

How it is used

Akıtma is an Aegean breakfast or a sweet snack. Tea goes with it. It can be dessert when the syrup is generous. It is not a street stuffed bread.

Field notes

  • Akıtma is an Aegean Turkish semolina pancake, poured thin.
  • This version includes walnuts, so it is not nut-free.
  • The place on the map is Ayvalık, on the Aegean coast.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup fine semolina (about 160 g)
  • 1/2 cup wheat flour
  • 1 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1/2 cup yogurt
  • 1 cup warm water
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
  • 1/2 cup chopped walnuts
  • 1/2 cup sugar
  • 1/4 cup water, for the syrup
  • Oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Stir the semolina, flour, yeast, yogurt, warm water, and salt into a thin batter. Cover it for 30 to 40 minutes, until foamy.
  2. Simmer the sugar and the quarter-cup of water for 5 minutes to make a light syrup. Keep it warm.
  3. Oil a griddle over medium heat and pour a thin round. This is a pourable Aegean batter, not a stuffed flatbread.
  4. Cook until the top sets and the underside spots, then flip once and cook briefly.
  5. Top each pancake with walnuts and a spoon of syrup. The walnuts are a tree nut.

Semolina and flour are wheat. Yogurt is dairy. Walnuts are a tree nut. This plate is not nut-free.

Eat akıtma the day it is cooked. Refrigerate plain pancakes separately from the syrup and nuts, and finish them when you reheat them. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Cheese instead of walnuts removes the tree nut and keeps the dairy.
  • More yogurt makes a tangier batter.
  • A savory herb finish is a home choice and not this sweet plate.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Dessert
  • Snack
  • Black tea
  • Extra crushed walnuts
  • A salty white cheese, if you want a savory contrast
  • Vegetarian

Åland pancake

A baked porridge pancake from Åland, cardamom-scented, with prune jam.

25 min prep · 45 min cook · Serves 8 · 1 baking dish, 8 squares

History

The Åland pancake is a baked pancake made from a cooked porridge, usually semolina or rice, with eggs, milk, sugar, and cardamom. This recipe uses semolina. A rice-porridge version is a real variation. It is the signature sweet of the Åland Islands, which are an autonomous part of Finland, so the country pin is Finland. Prune jam and whipped cream are the serving. It is not a pan-fried Finnish lettu.

Cultural background

It is dessert and celebration food, cut in squares from a baking dish. Cardamom is the spice of the batter. The texture is closer to a firm porridge pudding with a browned top than to a crepe.

Why it tastes this way

Semolina makes it creamy and slightly resilient. Cardamom is floral and piney. Sugar and milk round it. The oven browns the top so there is a crust over a soft interior. Prune jam is dark and tart against the mild cake. Cream carries the cardamom.

How it is made

Semolina is cooked in milk with cardamom into a porridge, enriched with eggs and sugar, baked until set and brown, and served with prune jam.

Authentic touch. Cook the porridge fully before the eggs go in, and cool it a little so the eggs do not scramble. Serve prune jam. Cardamom belongs in the batter.

How it is used

Warm or cold, in squares, with prune jam and whipped cream, as a dessert.

Field notes

  • Åland pancake is baked from porridge. It is not a fried lettu.
  • Semolina and rice versions both exist. This page uses semolina.
  • Prune jam and whipped cream are the usual plate.

Ingredients

  • 1 liter whole milk
  • 100 g semolina
  • 1 tsp ground cardamom
  • 1/2 tsp fine salt
  • 3 tbsp sugar
  • 3 large eggs
  • 20 g unsalted butter, for the dish
  • Prune jam and whipped cream, for serving

Steps

  1. Heat the milk with the cardamom and salt. Rain in the semolina, stirring, and cook until you have a thick porridge. Stir in the sugar. Cool for 10 minutes.
  2. Beat the eggs and stir them into the porridge.
  3. Spread the mixture in a buttered baking dish. Bake at 200°C until set and browned on top, about 40 minutes.
  4. Cut in squares and serve with prune jam and whipped cream.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Rice porridge instead of semolina, which changes the grain
  • Finnish lettu, the thin pan pancake, which is a different page

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Feast or fast day
  • Prune jam
  • Whipped cream
  • A little extra ground cardamom
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Apam balik

A turnover pancake folded over crushed peanuts, sugar, and a little butter.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 turnovers

History

Apam balik is a Malaysian turnover pancake. Batter is cooked on a griddle, covered with peanuts and sugar, and folded. George Town stalls sell both a crisp thin kind and a thicker sweet kind. Singapore eats the same family of pancake, so it is listed here too. Peanuts are the usual filling, which is why this page carries a peanut allergen. It is street food, sweet, and distinct from Indonesian martabak manis even though the two are often mentioned together.

Cultural background

The name means a turnover. The fold is the point. Corn and extra butter show up in some batters. This recipe keeps the filling as peanuts and sugar so the cake matches its reputation. A gluten-free claim would be wrong: the batter is wheat.

Why it tastes this way

Peanuts and sugar melt against a hot pancake and taste toasted and sandy. Butter or margarine adds richness. The batter, with egg and condensed milk, browns and tastes like a sweet griddle cake rather than a crepe. Because the peanuts are crushed, they stick to the crumb instead of rolling out. The flavor is a street sweet built on sugar and roasted peanut, not on a fruit filling.

How it is made

Cook a sweet wheat batter, cover it with crushed peanuts and sugar, and fold it in half.

Authentic touch. Fold while the top is still moist enough to glue shut, and crush the peanuts fine. Whole nuts tear the pancake and fall out of the turnover.

How it is used

Cut the turnover into strips and eat it warm. It is a snack and a dessert, in Malaysia and in Singapore.

Field notes

  • Apam balik is folded over peanuts and sugar. The peanuts are not optional in this recipe.
  • Singapore sells the same pancake, so it is listed with Malaysia.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose wheat flour
  • 1 egg
  • 3/4 cup water
  • 2 tablespoons sweetened condensed milk
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking powder
  • A pinch of salt
  • 1/2 cup roasted peanuts, crushed
  • 3 tablespoons sugar
  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • Neutral oil

Steps

  1. Whisk flour, baking powder, salt, egg, water, and condensed milk into a smooth batter.
  2. Mix the crushed peanuts with the sugar.
  3. Oil a pan over medium heat. Pour a ladle and cook until the top is bubbly and the underside is brown.
  4. Scatter peanut sugar and a few dots of butter over the pancake. Fold it in half and press.
  5. Cook another minute, slide it out, and cut. Repeat.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Canned corn and extra butter, a common stall addition
  • A thinner, crisper batter if you add more water

Pairings and uses

  • Street food
  • Snack
  • Dessert
  • Hot tea
  • A few extra crushed peanuts
  • Sweet corn stirred into the batter
  • Vegetarian

Argentine panqueques

Thin milk crepes rolled around dulce de leche, with a browned-butter edge.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 crepes

History

Panqueques in Buenos Aires are thin wheat crepes, a dessert and a Sunday treat, filled most often with dulce de leche. The batter is flour, egg, and milk, the crepe family that spread through the Río de la Plata in the twentieth century along with other European kitchen habits. They are not corn cakes. Corn cakes elsewhere in South America are older than wheat hotcakes, and these are frankly a later wheat dessert. Dulce de leche, milk and sugar cooked until tan, is the filling people expect.

Cultural background

Home cooks in Buenos Aires make a stack of panqueques for dessert or for a late breakfast, then roll them while they are warm. Bakeries sell them already filled. The crepe is a vehicle. The jar of dulce de leche is the flavor most of the table is waiting for.

Why it tastes this way

A Buenos Aires panqueque is a thin egg batter, and the sweetness stays in the filling. Dulce de leche tastes of caramel and cooked cream, thick enough to stay in a ribbon when the crepe is rolled warm. The batter itself should taste lightly of milk and browned butter, not of sugar. A pinch of salt keeps the milk from tasting flat.

How it is made

Whisk flour, egg, milk, melted butter, and salt. Rest the batter, cook thin crepes in a buttered pan, and roll them around dulce de leche.

Authentic touch. Cook the crepes thin enough to see the pan through the batter, and fill them while they are still flexible. Cold crepes crack. Warm the dulce de leche if it is too stiff to spread, without boiling it.

How it is used

Serve them warm, two to a plate, as dessert or a sweet breakfast. A dust of sugar is optional and is not required once the filling is in.

Field notes

  • In Buenos Aires the usual filling is dulce de leche, milk cooked with sugar until it turns tan.
  • The batter is a thin wheat crepe. It is not a corn arepa and not a fritter.
  • Roll the crepes while they are warm so they bend instead of cracking.

Ingredients

  • 100 g wheat flour
  • 1 egg
  • 250 ml milk
  • 1 tablespoon melted butter, plus more for the pan
  • Pinch of salt
  • 200 g dulce de leche

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, egg, milk, melted butter, and salt into a thin batter. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Heat a skillet, film it with butter, and pour in just enough batter to coat the bottom. Swirl.
  3. Cook until the edge lifts and the underside spots, then flip and cook briefly.
  4. Spread dulce de leche on the warm crepe and roll it.
  5. Repeat with the rest of the batter and serve warm.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Dulce de membrillo, quince paste, is another Río de la Plata filling. The Uruguayan page uses it on purpose.
  • A savory ham filling adds meat and is not vegetarian.
  • Orange zest in the batter is a home perfume. It does not replace the dulce de leche.

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Breakfast
  • Snack
  • Café con leche
  • A glass of milk
  • Extra dulce de leche at the table
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Australian pancakes

Wide, thin lemon-and-sugar pancakes with a lacy brown rim and a soft center.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · About 8 wide pancakes

History

These are the larger Australian pancakes, thinner than pikelets and finished with lemon and sugar. British settlers brought that lemon-and-sugar habit, and weekend pans still use it. The batter on this page is looser than a pikelet batter, and the cakes are cooked wider in the pan. Pikelets remain the small thick tea cakes. They are a different recipe, with less milk and a smaller spoon, and they are not repeated here.

Cultural background

A home breakfast pan, often on a slow morning, is the setting. The pancakes are stacked or folded, with lemon squeezed over sugar so the juice makes a quick syrup. They are dessert when the same pan is cooked after supper. They are not the jam pikelets of afternoon tea.

Why it tastes this way

More milk makes a thinner batter, so the cake spreads and browns in a lacy rim instead of rising tall. Egg keeps it flexible. Sugar in the batter is slight. The real sweetness and the acid are lemon and sugar on the hot surface, which taste bright rather than like jam. Butter in the pan adds a nutty edge.

How it is made

Whisk flour with a little baking powder, sugar, salt, egg, and enough milk to make a batter that spreads in the pan. Cook wide rounds, flipping once. Sprinkle sugar on the hot pancake and squeeze lemon over it. Fold and serve.

Authentic touch. Use the looser batter and a wider round, then add lemon and sugar. If you thicken the batter and drop it by the tablespoon, you are making pikelets. Say the difference while you cook: these are the larger, thinner pancakes.

How it is used

These pancakes are breakfast or a simple dessert. Lemon and sugar are the finish. They can be a Shrove-style treat in houses that keep that calendar, and they are also an ordinary weekend breakfast. Pikelets are the tea-table cousin, not this plate.

Field notes

  • These pancakes are larger and thinner than Australian pikelets.
  • The batter has more milk than a pikelet batter, so it spreads.
  • Lemon and sugar, not jam, is the finish written here.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup wheat flour (about 125 g)
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1 tablespoon sugar, plus more to finish
  • 1/4 teaspoon fine salt
  • 1 egg
  • 1 1/4 cups milk
  • 1 tablespoon melted unsalted butter, plus more for the pan
  • 2 lemons, cut into wedges

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, baking powder, sugar, and salt. Add the egg, milk, and melted butter and whisk to a smooth, loose batter. It should spread in the pan, unlike a thick pikelet batter.
  2. Rest the batter for 10 minutes.
  3. Butter a wide pan over medium heat. Pour enough batter to make a round larger and thinner than a pikelet.
  4. Cook until the surface looks set and the underside is browned in spots, then flip once.
  5. Sprinkle sugar on the hot pancake and squeeze lemon over it. Fold and serve. Repeat with the rest.

The batter contains wheat, egg, and dairy. Lemon juice on a hot cake can spatter if you squeeze hard from above the pan.

Eat them as they come off the pan. Refrigerate leftovers and reheat covered. Add lemon and sugar after reheating so the juice does not make the crumb soggy in the refrigerator. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A squeeze of orange instead of lemon is sweeter and less sharp.
  • A handful of berries in the pan makes a different cake and is not required.
  • Maple syrup is a later pantry habit. This page stays with lemon and sugar.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Dessert
  • Lemon wedges
  • Sugar
  • Tea
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Bananas Foster pancakes

A plain buttermilk pancake under bananas in brown sugar, butter, cinnamon, and rum.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 pancakes with sauce

History

Bananas Foster is a 1951 dessert created at Brennan's in New Orleans, bananas cooked with butter, brown sugar, cinnamon, and rum, and named Bananas Foster. Pancakes with that sauce are a later adaptation, not the original restaurant dessert. The cakes here are a wheat stack with the banana sauce spooned over them. The restaurant dessert is flambéed, and this home sauce cooks the rum in the pan.

Cultural background

New Orleans already had bananas through the fruit trade. The 1951 dessert is a restaurant showpiece. Putting the sauce on pancakes came afterward, on brunch menus and in home kitchens.

Why it tastes this way

Brown sugar and butter make a caramel that sticks to the banana. Rum perfumes the sauce once the raw alcohol cooks off. Cinnamon is the spice. The pancake underneath is plain so the sauce stays the loud flavor. A very thick stack will not warm through under a spoon of sauce.

How it is made

Griddled buttermilk pancakes with a stovetop banana, brown sugar, and rum sauce.

Authentic touch. Make the sauce and the pancakes. A bottled banana syrup on a stack is not Bananas Foster. Simmer the rum. You do not need to light the pan.

How it is used

Pancakes on plates, sauce and bananas spooned over while hot.

Field notes

  • Bananas Foster was created in 1951 at Brennan's in New Orleans.
  • The pancake version is a later adaptation of that dessert.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup buttermilk
  • 1 large egg
  • 1 tablespoon unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the griddle
  • 4 tablespoons unsalted butter, for the sauce
  • 1/2 cup packed brown sugar
  • 2 ripe bananas, sliced
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground cinnamon
  • 2 tablespoons dark rum

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, sugar, baking powder, and salt. Stir in the buttermilk, egg, and melted tablespoon of butter.
  2. Cook 8 small pancakes on a buttered griddle over medium heat, flipping once, until the centers are set. Keep them warm.
  3. For the sauce, melt 4 tablespoons of butter in a skillet. Stir in the brown sugar and cinnamon until the sugar dissolves.
  4. Add the bananas and cook about 1 minute, until they soften at the edges.
  5. Add the rum and simmer 1 minute so the sauce thickens slightly. You do not need to set it on fire.
  6. Spoon the bananas and sauce over the pancakes and serve at once.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Skip the rum for an alcohol-free butter and brown-sugar banana sauce.
  • The original dessert was served over ice cream, which is the 1951 plate, not this pancake.

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Breakfast
  • The banana sauce
  • A little extra rum left out of the pan if you want it at the table
  • Coffee
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Russia · Eastern Europe

Blinchiki

Thin Russian crepes rolled around a sweet farmer-cheese filling.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 filled crepes

History

Blinchiki are the thin unleavened Russian crepes, often filled and rolled, as distinct from thicker yeasted blini. A sweet filling of tvorog, the farmer cheese, is written here. Meat fillings exist and would change the diet. The Ukrainian nalysnyky page is the rolled crepe baked in sour cream. These blinchiki are filled and served after a brief second fry, not baked in a dish of cream.

Cultural background

They are home dessert food and a way to use tvorog. The crepe has to be thin enough to roll without cracking. A dusting of sugar or a spoon of sour cream finishes them.

Why it tastes this way

The crepe is mild and a little eggy. Tvorog is tangy, sweetened just enough, with lemon to lift it. A second fry browns the outside of the roll so you get a crisp seam and a warm cheese center. They taste fresher than a heavy baked pasta, because the sauce is only what you add at the table.

How it is made

Thin wheat crepes are filled with sweetened tvorog, rolled, and fried briefly in butter.

Yeasted pancakes stuffed with cinnamon walnuts and soaked in a lemon-rose syrup.

Qatayef are the pancakes of Ramadan evenings in Egypt, and they are also a feast sweet in Lebanon, Palestine, and Syria. The coast has traded flour, sugar, and coconut for a long time. This page does not name one inventor or a European author for the crepe. The dish as written is the Goan rolled pancake: a wheat crepe, an egg and milk batter, and a fresh coconut center.

Cultural background

Panaji bakeries and home kitchens both turn them out in the festive season. The crepe should be thin enough to roll and pale rather than browned like a dessert pancake from a diner. The filling is coconut cooked with jaggery until it clumps, the same idea as a Bengal coconut pitha filling, in a Goan batter.

Why it tastes this way

Fresh coconut is sweeter and wetter than dried coconut, and jaggery gives it a dark, rum-like molasses note without any rum required. Cardamom is enough spice. The crepe tastes of egg, milk, and wheat, neutral so the filling leads. A little salt in the coconut keeps the roll from cloying.

How it is made

Cook a coconut-jaggery filling, make thin egg crepes, and roll them while they are still soft.

Authentic touch. Grind or grate the coconut fresh if you can. Dry sweetened coconut will taste like a candy bar and throw off the jaggery.

How it is used

Dust nothing on top. Serve the rolls warm on a festive afternoon, two to a person.

Field notes

  • Alebele is the Goan name for these coconut-filled crepes.
  • Christmas tables in Goan Catholic homes are one place they appear, not the only one.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup all-purpose wheat flour
  • 1 egg
  • 3/4 cup milk
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • A pinch of salt
  • 1 cup freshly grated coconut
  • 1/3 cup jaggery, chopped
  • 1/4 teaspoon cardamom
  • Butter or neutral oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Cook coconut, jaggery, cardamom, and a pinch of salt in a dry pan until the jaggery melts and the filling holds a shape. Cool it.
  2. Blend or whisk flour, egg, milk, sugar, and a pinch of salt into a thin crepe batter. Rest 10 minutes.
  3. Butter a crepe pan over medium heat. Pour a small ladle and swirl a thin crepe.
  4. Cook until the underside is pale gold and the top is dry. Turn briefly if needed.
  5. Spread filling in a strip and roll. Keep the rolls covered while you finish the batter.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

A thick batter of flour, egg, milk, and a little leavener is fried in olive oil as small cakes and drizzled with honey and cinnamon.

Hot, with honey, as a snack or a sweet plate.

History

Hotteok is a filled street pancake sold from griddles in Korean cities, especially when the weather is cold. A yeasted wheat dough is stuffed with brown sugar and nuts, pan-fried in oil, and pressed so the filling melts. This recipe uses walnuts, so the tree-nut tag is required. Peanuts are another stall's choice and are not in this bowl. The page does not assign the cake an invention year. It is a winter street sweet, not a savory jeon.

Cultural background

Vendors work a wide oiled griddle, turning and pressing each cake with a flat tool. The dough is faintly sweet. The drama is the filling, which will burn a tongue if you bite too fast. Seeds and nuts vary by cart.

Why it tastes this way

Yeast gives the dough a slight chew and a mild fermented aroma, different from a baking-powder hotcake. Brown sugar and cinnamon melt into a syrup inside the fry. Walnut adds bitterness and fat that keep the sugar from tasting one-note. Oil on the crust smells like a doughnut. The contrast, soft dough and hot syrup, is why people eat it standing up in the cold.

How it is made

Fill yeasted wheat rounds with brown sugar, cinnamon, and walnut, then fry and flatten them on an oiled griddle.

Authentic touch. Seal the sugar completely before it hits the pan. A leak caramelizes into a black patch and empties the center. Press only after the underside has set.

How it is used

Rest them a minute so the syrup sets slightly, then eat them warm as a street snack. They are dessert, not a meal.

Field notes

  • Hotteok is a filled street pancake. The usual pocket is brown sugar and nuts.
  • Walnuts are in this recipe, so it is not nut-free.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups all-purpose wheat flour
  • 1 teaspoon dry yeast
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 3/4 cup warm water
  • 1/3 cup brown sugar
  • 1 teaspoon cinnamon
  • 1/3 cup walnuts, chopped
  • Neutral oil for the griddle

Steps

  4. Heat a lightly oiled griddle on medium. Place the cakes seam-side down. Cook until the underside browns, then flip.
  5. Press each cake flat with a spatula so the filling melts. Cook until both sides are golden. Cool one minute before eating.

The sugar center is very hot. Wait a minute before you bite.

Imagawayaki

A round filled cake cooked in a hinged iron, with sweet red bean paste sealed inside a soft wheat shell.

15 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 8 · 8 filled cakes

History

Imagawayaki are round filled cakes cooked in a molded iron. Batter is poured, sweet red bean paste is set in the center, and a second skin of batter closes the cake before the iron is turned. The names vary by city. Imagawayaki is one name, and obanyaki is another, heard often in Kansai. This page does not pick a founding shop or a year. The method is the mold, which makes it a different sweet from dorayaki's two separate pancakes.

Cultural background

Street stalls still use heavy hinged pans over a gas flame. The cake should be brown, not pale, and the paste should reach neither the hinge nor the open air. A leak of anko welds the cake to the iron. Home cooks can use a small filled-cake pan or an electric mold with the same batter.

Why it tastes this way

The iron browns the batter on both sides at once, so the crust tastes more toasted than a dorayaki cooked in an open pan. Egg and milk keep the interior tender. Anko is sweet and beany, a filling rather than a sandwich layer you can see. Because the paste steams inside the mold, it stays moist against the crumb.

How it is made

Cook a wheat batter in a round mold, bury a spoon of anko, cover it with more batter, and turn the iron until both faces brown.

Authentic touch. Add the bean paste only after the first batter has skinned over. If you drop the paste into raw batter, it sinks and breaks the seal.

How it is used

Cool them until the center is no longer scorching. Eat them as a street snack with tea. They are a dessert, not a meal.

Field notes

  • Imagawayaki and obanyaki are two city names for this molded filled cake.
  • The iron cooks both sides. Dorayaki is two pancakes cooked in the open and then joined.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose wheat flour
  • 1 egg
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/2 cup milk
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1 cup sweet red bean paste
  • Neutral oil for the molds

Steps

  1. Whisk flour, egg, sugar, milk, and baking powder into a thick batter. Rest it 10 minutes.
  2. Oil a hot imagawayaki iron or a round filled-cake pan.
  3. Pour a little batter into each mold, add a spoon of anko, and cover with more batter so the paste is sealed.
  4. Cook, turning the iron or flipping the cakes, until both sides are deep brown and the batter is set.
  5. Cool briefly and serve.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Custard in place of anko
  • Obanyaki, the same mold cake under another city's name

  • Nothing sweet beside them

How it is made

Mix a baking-powder batter with egg and milk, cook thick rounds covered on low heat, and serve them with butter and syrup.

Serve two or three to a plate while hot, with butter melting on top. Coffee is the kissaten partner.

History

Kaiserschmarrn is an Austrian pancake torn in the pan, caramelized in butter and sugar, and served with plum compote. The name means the emperor's shredded pancake. Tales that put the dish on one particular emperor's table are popular and not something this page can verify. What is stable is the method: a thick soufflé-like batter, raisins, a tear with two forks, and powdered sugar. It is eaten in Austria and also in Bavaria. The pin is Austria. The region in this journal is Western Europe.

Cultural background

It is a dessert and a mountain-hut dish, sweet enough to be the meal. Zwetschkenröster, a plum compote, is the classic side. The shreds should be uneven. A neat stack is the wrong shape.

Why it tastes this way

Separated eggs make it light, then the tear and the sugar make crisp caramel edges. Raisins, soaked so they are not hard, add dark sweetness. Butter is the cooking fat and the flavor. Plum compote is tart and deep. Powdered sugar is the last layer. If you do not tear it, you have baked a thick pancake and missed the dish.

How it is made

A batter of flour, milk, egg yolks, sugar, and rum-soaked raisins is lightened with whites, cooked in butter, torn, caramelized, and dusted with sugar.

Authentic touch. Tear the pancake in the pan while it cooks, using two forks, and dust with sugar so the new surfaces caramelize. Serve plum compote. Do not present it as a tidy stack.

How it is used

In a heap, with plum compote, as dessert. It is sweet enough to be the whole course.

Field notes

  • Schmarrn means the pancake is torn. The shreds are the point.
  • Stories about a specific emperor's plate are not verified here.
  • Plum compote is the usual side.

Ingredients

  • 40 g raisins
  • 2 tbsp rum, or hot water
  • 120 g plain wheat flour
  • 2 tbsp sugar, plus more for the pan
  • 1 pinch fine salt
  • 3 large eggs, separated
  • 250 ml whole milk

  3. Melt the butter in a wide pan, pour in the batter, and cook over medium heat until the bottom is golden and the top is setting.
  4. Tear the pancake into pieces with two forks. Sprinkle with sugar, turn the pieces, and cook until the edges caramelize. Dust with powdered sugar and serve with plum compote.

Fry thin rice crepes and top them with a coconut-yolk paste and a sweet egg-white mixture, then fold.

Cook a rice and coconut batter in a dimpled pan, finishing each cup with a spoon of thick coconut cream.

A thick batter traps air, so the crumb tastes soft and slightly yeasty under a sweet crust. Butter and condensed milk make it rich. Cook one thick sweet pancake, cover it with butter, chocolate, cheese, and peanuts, and cut it in squares.

Mexican buñuelos

Thin, crisp fried dough with anise, broken into shards and soaked with piloncillo and cinnamon syrup.

History

These are the Christmas buñuelos of Mexican plazas and posadas: a wheat dough rolled thin, fried until blistered, and finished with piloncillo syrup. They are fried. Cultural background

Why it tastes this way

Frying drives water out of a thin sheet of dough, so the buñuelo shatters instead of chewing like bread. Anise and orange peel scent the syrup. Piloncillo, unrefined cane sugar, tastes of molasses rather than of white sugar alone. Cinnamon rounds it out. The contrast is crisp dough and a syrup that softens it a minute after it is poured.

How it is made

Make a simple wheat dough with egg and anise, rest it, roll thin rounds, and fry them until golden and blistered. Simmer piloncillo with cinnamon and orange peel and pour the syrup over the hot fritters.

How it is used

Break the buñuelos into a bowl, pour on warm syrup, and eat them at a Christmas gathering or a December street stand. They go soft if they sit in the syrup, which is how many people like them.

Field notes

  • These buñuelos are fried, not baked on a comal and not cooked like a pancake batter.

  4. Fry one round at a time in shimmering oil, turning once, until blistered and golden. Drain on a rack.
  5. Break into pieces, pour on the warm syrup, and serve.

In Montevideo, panqueques are a dessert crepe of the Río de la Plata, filled here with dulce de membrillo and fresh cheese. Quince paste and cheese is a confectioner's pair in Uruguayan shops, and the crepe is one way that pair is served warm. The batter is wheat, egg, and milk. This is not the Buenos Aires habit of rolling every crepe around dulce de leche, and it is not the Chilean birthday crepe finished with manjar and powdered sugar. Corn cakes are older elsewhere on the continent. These are a city dessert.

Cultural background

Confiterías in Montevideo sell quince sweets by the slab. At home, a stack of crepes and a block of membrillo make dessert after a late meal, or a plate for a birthday table that is not trying to copy a bakery case. Yerba mate is the drink that often sits nearby, bitter next to the sweet.

Why it tastes this way

Quince paste is floral, tannic, and firm. Fresh cheese is salty and cool. Together they keep the crepe from being only sweet, which is the point of this filling. The batter tastes of milk and a little butter, cooked until it spots. The cheese does not disappear the way a soft caramel does. You get a layer, then a ribbon of fruit, then egg-browned wheat.

How it is made

Cook thin crepes from wheat flour, egg, milk, butter, and salt. Lay on a slice of quince paste and a slice of fresh cheese, roll, and warm them a minute.

Authentic touch. Slice the quince paste thin so it can warm through without tearing the crepe. Lay the cheese on the paste, not in chunks that bulge. Roll while the crepe is warm and the paste is still sliceable, not melted.

How it is used

Serve them as dessert, warm, with mate or coffee. They are a sweet plate with a salty line through it, not a breakfast stack.

Field notes

  • This Montevideo crepe is filled with quince paste and fresh cheese, a shop pairing in the Río de la Plata.
  • The Argentine page in this journal rolls dulce de leche instead.
  • Mate beside the plate is a drink. It does not go into the batter.

Ingredients

  • 100 g wheat flour
  • 1 egg
  • 240 ml milk
  • 1 tablespoon butter, melted, plus more for the pan
  • Pinch of salt
  • 150 g quince paste, sliced thin
  • 80 g fresh white cheese, sliced thin

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, egg, milk, melted butter, and salt into a thin batter. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Butter a skillet lightly and cook thin crepes, flipping once, until they spot. Stack them.
  3. On each warm crepe, lay a thin slice of quince paste and a thin slice of cheese.
  4. Roll loosely.
  5. Return the rolls to a low pan for a minute so the cheese softens, then serve.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Dulce de leche is common in the region and is the filling on the Argentine page, not this one.
  • A savory ham crepe adds meat and is a different course.
  • A little lemon zest in the batter sharpens the quince. Use a strip, not a handful.

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Snack
  • Yerba mate
  • A short coffee
  • More quince paste on the side
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Welsh crempog

Welsh buttermilk griddle cakes, stacked with butter between the layers.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 small cakes

History

Crempog, plural crempogau, is the Welsh griddle cake. Buttermilk and bicarbonate are a common home batter, and yeasted overnight batters are also traditional. This page uses buttermilk. The cakes are stacked with butter between them, which is the serving that matters. Some families make a stack for a birthday, and some make crempog when eggs and fat are used up before Lent. Neither occasion is the only one.

Cultural background

The cake is Welsh-language food as well as a recipe. The country on the map is the United Kingdom. The culture is Welsh. A heavy pan or bakestone is the tool.

Why it tastes this way

Buttermilk and soda give a light tang and a soft crumb. Butter between hot cakes melts and salts each layer. The outside is only just browned. They taste less of sugar than a dessert crepe, unless you add jam.

How it is made

Flour, egg, buttermilk, melted butter, and baking soda are mixed to a pourable batter and cooked as small rounds, then stacked with butter.

Authentic touch. Stack them with a smear of butter on each hot cake, not only on the top one. A yeasted batter, left overnight, is the other honest version.

How it is used

In a buttered stack, at breakfast, tea, a birthday, or Shrove. Jam is optional.

Field notes

  • The plural is crempogau. The stack is buttered between the cakes.
  • Buttermilk versions and yeast versions both exist.
  • Some Welsh families stack them for a birthday.

Ingredients

  • 200 g plain wheat flour
  • 1/2 tsp baking soda
  • 1 pinch fine salt
  • 2 large eggs
  • 300 ml buttermilk
  • 25 g unsalted butter, melted, plus more for stacking and the pan

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, baking soda, and salt. Beat in the eggs, buttermilk, and melted butter until smooth.
  2. Grease a bakestone or frying pan over medium heat.
  3. Cook small ladlefuls until the top dulls, then turn and color the other side.
  4. Stack the cakes with butter between them. Serve the stack warm.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A yeasted crempog batter left to rise overnight
  • A little oatmeal in place of part of the wheat flour

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Feast or fast day
  • Dessert
  • Salted butter
  • Jam
  • Tea
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free
