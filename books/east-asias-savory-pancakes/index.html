Tianjin jianbing

A soft savory crepe wrapped around egg, sauce, herbs, and a shatter of crisp fried dough.

20 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 2 · 2 wrapped crepes

History

Tianjin jianbing is a street breakfast crepe. The cook spreads a thin batter, cracks an egg onto it, paints a savory sauce, adds scallion, cilantro, and chile, then presses in a crisp fried dough stick, the youtiao or guozi, and folds the crepe around it. Tianjin is the city most tied to this wrapped form. A Shandong jianbing is a different street crepe, often cracker-crisp and torn, and it has its own page. This one is the egg-and-fritter package.

Cultural background

Morning stalls work fast on a round griddle. The crepe stays flexible so it can wrap. The fritter supplies the crunch the soft sheet does not have. Sauces vary. A sweet-savory bean sauce and a chile sauce are the usual pair. This recipe uses those and skips any brand name.

Why it tastes this way

Egg fried onto the crepe tastes rich against a plain grain sheet. Fermented bean sauce is salty and slightly sweet. Cilantro and scallion freshen it. Chile keeps the heat mild unless you add more. The youtiao is dry, airy, and fried, so it crackles inside a tender wrap. The combination is the Tianjin breakfast: soft, crisp, savory, and eaten on the way to work.

How it is made

Cook a thin crepe, fry an egg onto it, add sauce and herbs, press in a crisp youtiao, and fold.

Authentic touch. Crack the egg onto the crepe while the batter is still wet and break the yolk with the spreader. An egg added after the sheet dries will not bond, and the wrap falls apart.

How it is used

Eat it at once, folded, while the fritter is still loud. It is a street breakfast.

Field notes

  • Tianjin jianbing guozi is the crepe wrapped around a crisp fried dough stick.
  • The egg is cooked on the crepe, not scrambled in a separate pan.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup all-purpose wheat flour
  • 2 tablespoons mung bean flour or more wheat flour
  • 3/4 cup water
  • 2 eggs
  • 2 crisp youtiao (fried dough sticks)
  • 2 tablespoons sweet bean sauce or fermented bean sauce
  • Chile sauce to taste
  • 2 scallions, chopped
  • A handful of cilantro
  • Neutral oil

Steps

  1. Whisk the flours and water into a thin crepe batter.
  2. Oil a griddle over medium heat. Pour half the batter and spread a thin round.
  3. Crack an egg on the crepe, break the yolk, and spread it. When the top sets, flip so the egg browns briefly, then flip back.
  4. Paint on bean sauce and chile sauce. Add scallion and cilantro. Press a youtiao onto the crepe so it cracks.
  5. Fold the crepe around the fritter. Repeat with the rest.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Lettuce or a cracker in place of youtiao
  • More chile sauce for a hotter wrap

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Street food
  • Savory meal
  • Soy milk
  • A second chile spoon if you want more heat
  • Pickled mustard tuber
  • Vegetarian
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Shandong jianbing

A very thin, cracker-crisp crepe, torn into pieces and eaten with scallion and a savory paste.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 crisp crepes

History

Shandong jianbing is a crisp street crepe from Shandong, and it is a different food from the Tianjin jianbing that wraps a youtiao. This one is often cracker-crisp. Cooks tear it into pieces. It is not always wrapped around a fried dough stick. The batter is thin, sometimes with millet or other coarse grain beside wheat, and it is cooked until it dries and blisters. Jinan is a fair place to meet it. The two jianbing share a family name and not a single assembly.

Cultural background

A brittle sheet can be broken over a plate of condiments or folded loosely. Treating it like the soft Tianjin wrap makes it crack in the wrong way and misses the texture Shandong stalls chase. Egg is common. A youtiao is not the defining center of this page.

Why it tastes this way

Cooking the batter dry concentrates a toasted wheat flavor and leaves almost no soft crumb. Millet, if you include it, adds a faint bitterness and a coarser bite. Scallion and fermented bean paste supply the salt and the green note the crisp sheet lacks. Because there is no juicy fritter inside, the taste is cleaner and more cracker-like than a Tianjin breakfast.

How it is made

Cook an extra-thin wheat and millet batter until it is brittle, then tear it and eat it with scallion and bean paste.

Authentic touch. Spread the batter thinner than a crepe you mean to fold, and leave it on the heat until it snaps. If it bends like cloth, it needs another minute.

How it is used

Break the sheet at the griddle and eat the pieces with the condiments. It is a street breakfast, not a wrapped sandwich.

Field notes

  • Shandong jianbing is often cracker-crisp and torn, not wrapped around a youtiao.
  • Tianjin's egg-and-fritter jianbing is the other famous street version.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup all-purpose wheat flour
  • 2 tablespoons millet flour
  • 3/4 cup water
  • 1 egg
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 2 scallions, chopped
  • 2 tablespoons fermented bean sauce
  • Neutral oil

Steps

  1. Whisk both flours, salt, and water into a very thin batter.
  2. Oil a griddle well and heat it until hot. Pour a small ladle and spread it as thin as you can.
  3. Break the egg onto the sheet if you want it richer, and spread it into a film.
  4. Cook without folding until the crepe is dry, blistered, and brittle enough to tear.
  5. Tear it into pieces. Eat them with scallion and fermented bean sauce.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • All wheat flour if you have no millet
  • An egg spread on the sheet before it dries

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Street food
  • Savory meal
  • Fermented bean paste
  • Chopped scallion
  • A spoon of chile oil if you want heat
  • Vegetarian
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Cong you bing

A flaky wheat cake layered with scallion and oil, crisp outside and chewy within.

30 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 pancakes

History

Cong you bing is a scallion pancake made from laminated dough, not from a poured batter. Wheat dough is rolled thin, painted with oil, scattered with salt and scallion, rolled into a log, coiled, and rolled out again so the oil and onion form layers. It is then pan-fried. Shanghai is one city strongly associated with it, and northern and Taiwanese kitchens make close versions. The flake comes from the laminating, the same idea as a pastry, done on a griddle.

Cultural background

Street griddles press the coil flat and fry it until the layers separate. At home the dough needs a rest so it rolls without springing back. This is a savory snack or a side, not a dessert and not a crepe. There is no egg in the written recipe.

Why it tastes this way

Oil trapped between sheets of dough fries the layers from the inside, which is why the pancake shatters and then chews. Scallion perfumes those layers instead of sitting in a batter. Salt on the oil makes each leaf savory. The toasted wheat tastes nuttier than a boiled noodle. Because nothing is fermented, the flavor is clean fat, onion, and crust.

How it is made

Laminate a wheat dough with oil, salt, and scallion, coil it, roll it flat, and pan-fry until the layers flake.

Authentic touch. Roll, oil, sprinkle, and coil. If you knead the scallions into a ball of dough, you lose the layers and the cake tastes like a bun. Fry from a cold-medium start or the outside browns before the coil cooks.

How it is used

Cut into wedges and eat hot. It is street food and a side with soup or congee. The crisp does not last.

Field notes

  • Cong you bing is laminated dough. It is not a batter you pour.
  • The coil is what makes the flake. Scallion alone does not.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups all-purpose wheat flour
  • 3/4 cup hot water
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt, plus more for the layers
  • 3 tablespoons neutral oil or sesame oil
  • 1 bunch scallions, finely chopped
  • More oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Stir flour and salt with hot water into a dough. Knead until smooth. Rest 20 minutes.
  2. Divide into four. Roll one piece very thin. Brush with oil, salt it, and cover it with scallion.
  3. Roll the sheet into a log, coil the log into a spiral, and gently roll the spiral into a thick round.
  4. Pan-fry over medium heat in a little oil until both sides are golden and the layers have separated at the edge.
  5. Repeat with the remaining dough. Cut and serve.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A pinch of white pepper in the oil
  • A few drops of chile oil on the finished wedge

Pairings and uses

  • Street food
  • Savory meal
  • Snack
  • A small dish of black vinegar
  • Soy milk
  • Congee
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Taiwanese danbing

A thin breakfast crepe with egg cooked on its face, brushed with soy sauce and rolled with scallion.

10 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 2 · 2 rolled crepes

History

Danbing is Taiwan's egg crepe, a breakfast from street griddles and from the windows of morning shops. Dan means egg. A thin wheat batter is spread, an egg is cooked on it, soy sauce and scallion go over the top, and the sheet is rolled. Fillings such as pork floss or cheese are common additions. This recipe keeps the egg crepe itself, without meat, so the tags stay vegetarian. It is Taipei breakfast food, fast and savory.

Cultural background

The griddle is flat and lightly oiled. The crepe has to stay flexible enough to roll and strong enough to hold the egg. Breakfast stands assemble it in a minute. At home the same batter works in a nonstick pan. Pork floss, if you add it, brings a meat allergen the written recipe does not have.

Why it tastes this way

Egg against a toasted wheat sheet is the whole flavor, salted hard by soy sauce. Scallion cuts the richness. Because the egg is spread thin, it tastes fried rather than like a folded omelet. The crepe itself is almost bland, a carrier, which is why the sauce matters. It is a breakfast built for eating out of a bag.

How it is made

Cook a thin wheat crepe, spread an egg on it with soy sauce and scallion, and roll it tight.

Authentic touch. Spread the egg while the crepe is still moist so they fuse. Brush soy sauce on the egg, not only on the plate, or the roll tastes flat.

How it is used

Eat it immediately, rolled, as a street breakfast. A hot soy milk is the usual cup beside it.

Field notes

  • Danbing means egg pancake. It is a Taiwanese breakfast crepe.
  • The plain egg roll is vegetarian. Pork floss is an addition, not the base recipe.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup all-purpose wheat flour
  • 2/3 cup water
  • A pinch of salt
  • 2 eggs
  • 1 tablespoon soy sauce
  • 2 scallions, chopped
  • Neutral oil

Steps

  1. Whisk flour, water, and salt into a thin batter.
  2. Oil a pan over medium heat. Pour half the batter and swirl a thin crepe.
  3. When the top is nearly set, crack an egg onto it and spread the white and yolk into a thin layer.
  4. Brush with soy sauce and scatter scallion. Cook until the egg sets.
  5. Roll the crepe tightly. Repeat with the second egg.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pork floss in the roll, which adds meat
  • A slice of processed cheese, which adds dairy

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Street food
  • Savory meal
  • Sweet or salty soy milk
  • A few slices of cucumber
  • Chile crisp on the side
  • Vegetarian
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Taiwanese oyster omelet

A soft, starch-set omelet crowded with oysters, finished with a sweet-savory sauce and greens.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 2 · 2 omelets

History

Taiwanese oyster omelet, o-a-jian, is a night-market cake of small oysters bound with sweet potato starch or tapioca starch and egg. Tainan is famous for it, and stalls across the island cook it on a well-oiled griddle. The starch sets into a tender, slightly chewy sheet around the oysters. A pink-brown sweet sauce is spooned on at the end. This page tags shellfish and names the oysters. There is no wheat in the written recipe, so it is gluten-free. It is not a wheat danbing.

Cultural background

The oysters should be small and cooked just until the edges ruffle. A thick flour batter would make a different pancake. Greens, often chrysanthemum greens or spinach, wilt in at the end. The sauce is part of the dish, not an optional ketchup.

Why it tastes this way

Oysters bring brine and sweetness. Sweet potato starch keeps the egg soft and a little elastic instead of dry and browned like a French omelet. Garlic and a mild chile cut the richness. The sauce adds sugar, tomato, and acid, which is why the cake tastes sweet-savory rather than purely marine. You eat it with the sauce in the same bite as the oyster.

How it is made

Set a sweet-potato-starch batter on a hot griddle with oysters and egg, then sauce it.

Authentic touch. Boil or rinse the starch slurry onto a hot oiled pan and let it set before you stir. If you scramble it like loose eggs from the start, you never get the sheet that defines o-a-jian.

How it is used

Serve immediately from the pan, sauce on top, as street food. It toughens if it sits.

Field notes

  • O-a-jian is the Taiwanese oyster omelet, set with sweet potato or tapioca starch.
  • Tainan is one of the cities most associated with the dish.

Ingredients

  • 200 grams small oysters, drained
  • 3 tablespoons sweet potato starch
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 2 eggs
  • 2 cloves garlic, sliced
  • A handful of spinach or chrysanthemum greens
  • 1 tablespoon tomato paste
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1 tablespoon rice vinegar
  • Neutral oil and salt

Steps

  1. Stir sweet potato starch and water into a slurry. Salt the oysters lightly.
  2. Simmer tomato paste, sugar, vinegar, and a spoon of water into a thick sauce. Keep it warm.
  3. Heat a generous film of oil. Fry the garlic briefly, add the oysters, and cook until the edges curl.
  4. Pour in the slurry and let it set. Add the eggs and greens. Cook until the eggs set and the starch turns translucent, turning the cake once if it is sturdy.
  5. Spoon the sauce over the top and serve at once.

Keep oysters cold until cooking, and cook them until the edges curl and the centers are firm.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Tapioca starch in place of sweet potato starch
  • More garlic shoots when they are in season

Pairings and uses

  • Street food
  • Savory meal
  • Snack
  • The sweet savory sauce in the recipe
  • A plate of pickled mustard greens
  • A cold tea
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Mandarin pancakes

Very thin wheat pancakes, soft and faintly sesame-scented, made to wrap crisp duck skin and meat.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · 12 thin pancakes

History

Mandarin pancakes are the thin wheat wrappers served with Peking duck in Beijing. They are cooked in pairs, with sesame oil between the two sheets, then peeled apart. Some kitchens steam the pairs and some griddle them. Either way they should stay pale and flexible. This page does not give them a founding date. Spring pancakes for the lichun festival are a different custom, with a spread of vegetable fillings, and they have their own page. These are the duck pancakes.

Cultural background

A duck dinner is built around the wrapper: a smear of sweet bean sauce, scallion, cucumber, and a slice of duck. The pancake itself is unleavened hot-water dough. It is an accompaniment. The written recipe is the pancake only, so it carries no meat allergen. The duck is the pairing.

Why it tastes this way

Hot-water dough tastes soft and wheaty, with almost no browning, so it will not compete with the duck. Sesame oil between the pair perfumes both faces. Cooking them together keeps them thinner than a single rolled bread could. The flavor is a neutral, slightly nutty sheet. Salt is minimal. The sauce and scallion at the table do the seasoning.

How it is made

Make a hot-water wheat dough, cook oiled pairs on a dry pan or in a steamer, and pull the pairs apart.

Authentic touch. Roll two rounds, oil one, and press them together before cooking. If you cook a single thick round and try to split it, it will not peel into two thin pancakes.

How it is used

Keep them covered with a cloth so they stay soft. Serve as the wrapper for Peking duck, with sauce, scallion, and cucumber. They are an accompaniment at a feast.

Field notes

  • Mandarin pancakes are cooked in pairs and then peeled apart.
  • They are the wrappers for Peking duck, separate from the spring pancakes of lichun.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups all-purpose wheat flour
  • 3/4 cup boiling water
  • 1 tablespoon toasted sesame oil
  • A pinch of salt

Steps

  1. Stir the flour and salt with boiling water until a dough forms. Knead until smooth. Rest 20 minutes.
  2. Divide into 12 pieces and roll each into a small thin round.
  3. Brush half the rounds with sesame oil. Press an unoiled round onto each oiled one so you have six pairs.
  4. Roll each pair thinner. Cook on a dry pan over medium heat, or steam, until the surface looks dry and a few spots color. Do not brown them hard.
  5. Pull each pair apart into two pancakes while warm. Cover them until serving.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A dry griddle instead of steam
  • A few extra drops of sesame oil

Pairings and uses

  • Accompaniment to a meal
  • Feast or fast day
  • Peking duck
  • Sweet bean sauce
  • Scallion and cucumber batons
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Osaka okonomiyaki

A thick savory pancake with cabbage mixed into the batter, topped with sauce, mayonnaise, and bonito flakes.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 2 thick pancakes

History

Osaka okonomiyaki mixes shredded cabbage into a wheat batter and griddles it as one thick cake. That mix is the Osaka method. Hiroshima's okonomiyaki is a different dish, built in layers, and it has its own page. Pork is a standard add-in. Bonito flakes on top are dried fish. The cake grew up as street and restaurant food in Osaka. This page does not give it a single invention year. It is not the scallion-only negiyaki, and it is not a sweet pancake.

Cultural background

Okonomi means as you like it, which explains the add-ins, not the shape. In Osaka the cabbage is still inside the batter. Grated yam helps the texture in many kitchens. Aonori and a brown savory sauce finish the crust. No trademark sauce is required. A homemade or unlabeled okonomiyaki sauce works.

Why it tastes this way

Cabbage wilts into the batter and tastes sweet and juicy, so the pancake is not a dry flour cake. Wheat and egg make a soft crumb. Pork browns at the edge and adds salt. The sauce is sweet and tangy, mayonnaise is rich, and bonito flakes add a smoky fish note that moves in the heat. The flavor is savory and layered on top of a vegetable-heavy interior.

How it is made

Mix cabbage into wheat batter, griddle it slowly with pork, flip once, and finish with sauce, mayonnaise, aonori, and bonito.

Authentic touch. Fold the cabbage in until every strand is wet, then cook covered on medium-low. A hot rush burns the outside and leaves the cabbage raw.

How it is used

Cut into wedges and share at the griddle if you have one. Eat it hot, while the bonito still twists. It is a savory meal.

Field notes

  • In Osaka the cabbage goes into the batter. Hiroshima stacks its ingredients in layers instead.
  • Bonito flakes are dried fish, so this plate is not vegetarian.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose wheat flour
  • 1 egg
  • 3/4 cup water or unsalted bonito dashi
  • 400 grams shredded green cabbage
  • 2 scallions, chopped
  • 80 grams thinly sliced pork belly
  • Neutral oil
  • Okonomiyaki sauce, mayonnaise, aonori, and bonito flakes to finish

Steps

  1. Whisk flour, egg, and dashi or water into a batter. Fold in cabbage and scallions until the shreds are coated.
  2. Oil a wide pan over medium-low heat. Spread half the batter into a thick round. Lay pork on top.
  3. Cover and cook about 8 minutes, until the underside is brown and the batter has set most of the way.
  4. Flip so the pork hits the pan. Cover and cook until the pork is done and the center is not raw.
  5. Turn the cake pork-side up. Spread sauce and mayonnaise, then aonori and bonito. Repeat with the rest.

Cook the pork through. The center of a thick cake needs the covered time.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Squid instead of pork, which swaps meat for more shellfish
  • A handful of shrimp, which adds shellfish

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Street food
  • Okonomiyaki sauce
  • Mayonnaise
  • Aonori and bonito flakes
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Hiroshima okonomiyaki

A layered savory crepe: thin batter, a mound of cabbage, noodles, pork, and egg, finished with sauce.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 2 · 2 layered cakes

History

Hiroshima okonomiyaki is built in layers on the griddle. A thin crepe is spread first. Cabbage goes on in a high pile, then pork and yakisoba noodles, and the stack is finished on a fried egg. The cabbage is not mixed into the batter the way it is in Osaka. Both cities call the result okonomiyaki. They are different dishes. This recipe writes the layers in order and keeps them separate from the Osaka page.

Cultural background

Hiroshima shops cook the stack on a wide teppan and flip it with large spatulas. At home a big pan works if you keep the portions small. The noodles are wheat. The egg is its own layer, not a binder stirred through cabbage.

Why it tastes this way

Each layer stays distinct. The crepe tastes toasted. The cabbage steams and turns sweet under the lid of the other ingredients. Yakisoba adds a seasoned, chewy wheat note. Pork salts the stack. The egg fries into a soft sheet that holds everything. Sauce on the outside is sweet and savory. You taste the sequence, not a single mixed batter.

How it is made

Layer a thin crepe, cabbage, pork, noodles, and a fried egg, flipping the stack so the egg becomes the base, then sauce it.

Authentic touch. Start with a truly thin crepe and a taller pile of cabbage than looks reasonable. The cabbage cooks down. If you mix it into the batter, you have left Hiroshima.

How it is used

Slide the whole stack onto a plate and cut it with a spatula. Eat it hot as a savory street meal.

Field notes

  • Hiroshima okonomiyaki is layered. Osaka mixes the cabbage into the batter.
  • The usual layers are crepe, cabbage, noodles, and egg, with pork in the pile.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup all-purpose wheat flour
  • 1/2 cup water
  • A pinch of salt
  • 300 grams shredded green cabbage
  • 80 grams thinly sliced pork
  • 1 portion cooked yakisoba wheat noodles, about 150 grams
  • 2 eggs
  • Neutral oil
  • Okonomiyaki sauce and aonori

Steps

  1. Whisk flour, water, and salt into a thin crepe batter.
  2. Oil a large pan. Spread a thin crepe of about half the batter. On it pile half the cabbage, then the pork.
  3. Cover and cook until the cabbage shrinks and the pork loses its raw color. Season the noodles and set them on the pile.
  4. In a clear spot of the pan, fry one egg as a flat sheet. Flip the whole stack onto the egg.
  5. Slide the cake onto a plate egg side down, spread sauce and aonori, and repeat for the second serving.

Cook the pork until it is done before you add the noodles and egg.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Bean sprouts with the cabbage
  • A second crepe on top if your pan runs dry

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Street food
  • Okonomiyaki sauce
  • Aonori
  • Pickled ginger
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Monjayaki

A loose, runny savory batter cooked on a griddle and eaten while it is still half set.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 2 · 1 shared pan

History

Monjayaki is Tokyo's runny savory pancake, especially the food of Tsukishima's monja street. The batter is much looser than Osaka okonomiyaki. Cabbage and other bits are fried first, then a wheat batter made with dashi is poured around them and cooked until it is partly set. People eat it from the griddle with small spatulas before it firms into a cake. This recipe uses bonito dashi, so it includes fish. It is a different dish from both Osaka and Hiroshima okonomiyaki.

Cultural background

The pleasure is the texture: a savory pool that stretches and then sets at the edges. Waiting for a firm pancake misses the point. Tsukishima shops expect you to cook it at the table. A home griddle or a wide pan is enough if you keep the batch small.

Why it tastes this way

Dashi is the flavor, a light fish stock that wheat and cabbage would not have on their own. The batter stays wet, so it tastes more like a savory custard than a browned pancake. Cabbage and a little onion sweetens it. The set edge is saltier and toastier. Eating it half-cooked on purpose is why the center tastes soft and marine rather than fried through.

How it is made

Fry cabbage, pour a very loose dashi batter over it, cook until the rim sets, and eat it from the pan half-set.

Authentic touch. Spread the batter thin and start eating from the firmed edge while the middle is still soft. If you flip it like a finished okonomiyaki, you have overcooked it.

How it is used

Serve it on the griddle with small spatulas. It is a shared savory snack, not a slice you pack for later.

Field notes

  • Monjayaki is meant to be eaten while the center is still soft.
  • Tsukishima in Tokyo is the neighborhood most tied to monja restaurants.

  2. Oil a griddle or wide pan on medium. Fry the cabbage and onion until they brighten.

  4. Cook without flipping until the edge looks set and the center is still soft and stretchy.
  5. Sprinkle aonori if you like and eat it from the pan with small spatulas, starting at the edge.

Negiyaki

A scallion-heavy savory pancake, sweeter and greener than cabbage okonomiyaki, with a soft wheat crumb.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 2 pancakes

History

Negiyaki is a scallion pancake of the Kansai, associated with Osaka and Kobe scallion cakes that predate the modern cabbage-heavy okonomiyaki. Negi means scallion. This page does not give the dish an invention year. The batter is wheat and egg, crowded with scallion, and cooked thicker than Korean pajeon. A kombu and shiitake stock keeps this version vegetarian. It is an older relative in the savory batter family, not a cabbage okonomiyaki with a new sauce.

Cultural background

Kobe and Osaka both still serve negiyaki, sometimes under names that vary by shop. The pancake is softer and more onion-sweet than a Korean jeon, and it lacks the tall cabbage of later okonomiyaki. Sauce and mayonnaise are optional. The scallion should be obvious in the crumb.

Why it tastes this way

A large amount of scallion steams inside the batter and turns sweet, with a green bite at the tips. Wheat and egg make a cushioned crumb rather than a thin cracker. Kombu and dried shiitake add a quiet savor without fish. Because cabbage is not the bulk, the cake tastes cleaner and more onion-like than Osaka okonomiyaki.

How it is made

Fold a large quantity of scallion into a wheat batter made with kombu-shiitake stock, then griddle it slowly.

Authentic touch. Cut the scallions fairly fine and fold them in until the batter looks green. A few strips on top will not make negiyaki.

How it is used

Cut and eat it hot, with sauce if you want it, or with only salt. It is a savory street cake.

Field notes

  • Negiyaki is tied to Kansai scallion cakes that came before cabbage-heavy okonomiyaki.
  • This page does not state an invention year for it.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose wheat flour
  • 1 egg
  • 3/4 cup kombu and shiitake stock
  • 6 scallions, finely chopped
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Neutral oil

Steps

  1. Simmer a piece of kombu and two dried shiitakes in 2 cups of water for 10 minutes. Measure 3/4 cup and cool it. Save the rest for another pot.
  2. Whisk flour, egg, stock, and salt. Fold in the scallions.
  3. Oil a pan over medium-low. Spread half the batter into a thick round.
  4. Cover and cook until the underside browns and the center sets. Flip and finish the other side.
  5. Repeat. Slice and serve.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A pinch of tenkasu, tempura bits, which are wheat
  • Bonito flakes, which would add fish

Pajeon

A savory wheat pancake packed with scallion, crisp at the rim and tender in the middle.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 2 large pancakes

History

Pajeon is Korea's scallion pancake, a wheat batter cooked on a griddle with a great quantity of scallion. Seoul rainy-day menus and home skillets both claim it. The batter often includes a little rice flour for crispness and an egg for binding. This page is the vegetable scallion pancake. Seafood pajeon and kimchi jeon are different batters with their own pages. The cake is wheat, so it is not gluten-free.

Cultural background

A dipping sauce of soy, vinegar, and a pinch of gochugaru is part of eating pajeon, more than a syrup would be. The pancake itself is only mildly hot if the dip carries the chile. Markets sell it by the piece. Homes cut it into squares.

Why it tastes this way

Scallion fries in the batter and turns sweet and green. Wheat browns at the contact with oil. Egg keeps the interior soft so the pancake is not a cracker. Rice flour, used in a small amount, dries the surface and helps the edge crackle. The dip adds salt, acid, and a mild chile heat that the batter does not need to carry alone.

How it is made

Mix a wheat batter, fold in a large bunch of scallion, and pan-fry it as one savory cake, finished with a soy-vinegar dip.

Authentic touch. Use more scallion than batter. If the pan looks like a plain pancake with a few greens, add another handful. Press the scallions into the batter as it sets.

How it is used

Cut it with kitchen scissors or a spatula and eat it hot with the dip. It is a snack, a side, or a rainy-day meal with rice.

Field notes

  • Pa means scallion. Pajeon is the scallion pancake, not the seafood one.
  • The batter is wheat. A gluten-free claim would not match this recipe.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose wheat flour
  • 2 tablespoons rice flour
  • 1 egg
  • 3/4 cup cold water
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 bunches scallions, cut into 2-inch lengths
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil
  • 2 tablespoons soy sauce
  • 1 tablespoon rice vinegar
  • 1/2 teaspoon gochugaru

Steps

  1. Whisk the flours, egg, water, and salt into a smooth batter. Stir in the scallions so they are coated, not drowned.
  2. Stir soy sauce, vinegar, and gochugaru together for the dip.
  3. Heat oil in a wide pan over medium-high heat. Spread half the batter into a round and press the scallions flat.
  4. Cook until the underside is deep gold and crisp, then flip and cook the other side through.
  5. Repeat with the rest. Slice and serve with the dip.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of rice flour for a crisper edge
  • Extra gochugaru in the dip

Haemul pajeon

A crisp scallion pancake threaded with shrimp and squid, mild chile in the dip.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 2 seafood pancakes

History

Haemul pajeon is the seafood scallion pancake. Haemul means seafood. Busan and other coastal tables pile shrimp, squid, and sometimes oyster or mussel into a wheat batter with scallion. This recipe uses shrimp and squid and tags shellfish. Squid is a mollusk. The allergen list here uses shellfish for it, and the ingredients name the squid plainly. It is a different pancake from the vegetable pajeon, even though the dip and the griddle are similar.

Cultural background

Coastal markets sell the seafood already cleaned. At home, pat it dry or the pancake steams. The heat of this version stays mild: gochugaru is in the dip, not a thick paste in the batter. Egg and wheat bind the cake.

Why it tastes this way

Shrimp and squid taste sweet and briny against the green scallion. Wheat and oil make the crust. A little rice flour keeps that crust from going limp under the moisture of the seafood. The dip's vinegar cuts the richness. Because the seafood is folded in, not laid on after, every wedge has some of it.

How it is made

Fold dried shrimp, squid, and scallion into a wheat batter and pan-fry until the center is cooked and the crust is brown.

Authentic touch. Dry the shrimp and squid well and cook the pancake longer than a vegetable one. Wet seafood in a thick batter leaves a raw middle.

How it is used

Cut into pieces and serve hot with the dip. It is a shared plate, not a dessert.

Field notes

  • Haemul pajeon is the seafood pancake. The vegetable scallion pancake is a separate page.
  • Squid is listed with shellfish in this allergen tag, and the recipe names it.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose wheat flour
  • 2 tablespoons rice flour
  • 1 egg
  • 3/4 cup cold water
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 bunch scallions, cut in lengths
  • 150 grams shrimp, peeled and patted dry
  • 100 grams squid, cleaned, sliced, and patted dry
  • Neutral oil
  • Soy sauce, rice vinegar, and gochugaru for the dip

Steps

  1. Whisk both flours, egg, water, and salt. Fold in scallions, shrimp, and squid.
  2. Mix a dip of 2 tablespoons soy sauce, 1 tablespoon vinegar, and 1/2 teaspoon gochugaru.
  3. Heat a generous film of oil in a wide pan. Spread half the batter and nestle any protruding seafood into it.
  4. Cook on medium until the underside is brown and the shrimp and squid are opaque. Flip and cook through.
  5. Repeat with the second half. Rest a minute, then cut and serve.

Cook shrimp and squid until they are opaque and firm, not translucent.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A few oysters added with the shrimp, still shellfish
  • More gochugaru if you want medium heat

  • Snack
  • Soy-vinegar dip

Kimchi jeon

A tangy, medium-hot pancake of ripe kimchi in a wheat batter, crisp at the edge.

15 min prep · 18 min cook · Serves 4 · 2 pancakes

History

Kimchi jeon is a pancake made to use ripe kimchi, the fermented napa cabbage that is a daily food in Korea. Sour kimchi is chopped and folded into wheat flour, often with onion and an egg, then pan-fried. This recipe uses kimchi seasoned with fish sauce and salted shrimp, which is a common jar, so the tags include fish and shellfish. It is not a vegetarian pancake unless the kimchi itself is free of seafood. The heat is medium from the gochugaru already in the cabbage.

Cultural background

Older, sour kimchi is preferred. Fresh sweet kimchi makes a duller cake. The pancake is a home way to finish a jar, and it is also sold as a snack. Scallion pajeon does not taste like this, because the sour cabbage is the point.

Why it tastes this way

Fermented cabbage brings acid, garlic, and chile that a raw scallion cannot. The longer the kimchi has aged, the sharper the pancake. Wheat batter tames that sharpness and turns the edges brown. Onion adds sweetness. Fish sauce and salted shrimp in the cabbage deepen the savor. The result is tangy and medium-hot, not a mild scallion cake.

How it is made

Chop sour kimchi, fold it into a wheat and egg batter with some of its juice, and pan-fry both sides.

Authentic touch. Squeeze the kimchi lightly so the batter is not flooded, but do not rinse it. The juice is the seasoning. Use that juice in the batter.

How it is used

Cut into squares and eat hot, with a small dish of vinegar and soy if you want more acid. Rice makes it a meal.

Field notes

  • Kimchi is fermented napa cabbage. Ripe, sour kimchi makes the better pancake.
  • Many kimchi recipes use fish sauce or salted shrimp. This one does, so it is not vegetarian.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup chopped ripe kimchi made with napa cabbage, garlic, ginger, gochugaru, fish sauce, and salted shrimp
  • 2 tablespoons kimchi juice
  • 1/2 small onion, sliced
  • 3/4 cup all-purpose wheat flour
  • 1 egg
  • 1/4 cup water
  • A pinch of salt only if the kimchi is mild
  • Neutral oil

Steps

  1. Chop the kimchi into small pieces. Save 2 tablespoons of the juice.
  2. Stir flour, egg, water, and kimchi juice into a thick batter. Fold in kimchi and onion.
  3. Heat oil in a pan over medium heat. Spread half the batter into a round.
  4. Cook until the underside is browned and crisp. Flip and cook until the center is set and the kimchi is hot.
  5. Repeat. Slice and serve.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of gochujang in the dip
  • Pork in the batter, which is a known heartier version and still meat

  • Snack
  • Steamed rice
  • A vinegar-soy dip

Bindaetteok

A coarse mung-bean pancake with pork, sour kimchi, and a toasted sesame edge.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 6 pancakes

History

Bindaetteok, also called nokdujeon, is Seoul's mung-bean pancake. Split yellow mung beans are soaked, ground coarsely, and fried with pork and kimchi. A spoon of wheat flour appears in some pans. This recipe leaves that flour out, so the gluten-free tag matches the bowl. The pork and a kimchi made with fish sauce and salted shrimp mean it is not vegetarian. It is street food and home food, especially in colder months.

Cultural background

The grind should stay a little gritty. A silky batter is a different cake. Vendors press the rounds flat so the beans crust. The name bindaetteok is the one Seoul menus use. Nokdu simply means mung bean.

Why it tastes this way

Mung bean tastes earthy and sweet when it fries, closer to a fritter than to a wheat jeon. Pork adds fat and salt. Kimchi adds acid and mild chile. Sesame oil on the pan perfumes the crust. Because there is no wheat gluten, the cake crumbles at the edge in a way a pajeon does not, and that crumble is part of the pleasure.

How it is made

Soak and coarsely grind mung beans, fold in pork and kimchi, and pan-fry thick savory cakes.

Authentic touch. Grind the beans coarse and drain them well. A wet, fine puree will not crust. Fry in enough oil that the edge sputters.

How it is used

Eat them hot, cut or whole, with a vinegary dip. They are a snack or a side with rice, not a dessert.

Field notes

  • Bindaetteok is a mung-bean pancake. Nokdu is the bean.
  • This version skips wheat flour so the gluten-free tag is honest. Many stalls add a spoonful.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup split yellow mung beans
  • 80 grams ground pork
  • 1/3 cup chopped kimchi with fish sauce and salted shrimp
  • 2 scallions, chopped
  • 1 garlic clove, minced
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Sesame oil and neutral oil

Steps

  1. Rinse and soak the mung beans for 3 hours. Drain very well.
  2. Grind coarsely with a spoon or two of water. The texture should be nubbly, not a smooth milk.
  3. Mix in pork, kimchi, scallions, garlic, and salt.
  4. Heat a pan with neutral oil and a teaspoon of sesame oil. Spread a small thick round.
  5. Fry on medium until both sides are deep brown and the pork is cooked through. Repeat.

Cook the pork until no pink remains in the center of each cake.

  • Bean sprouts folded in, a common addition

Banh xeo

A turmeric-yellow rice crepe, crisp at the edge, filled with pork, shrimp, and bean sprouts.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 folded crepes

History

Banh xeo is a Vietnamese turmeric rice crepe filled with pork and shrimp, eaten with herbs. The batter is rice flour and coconut milk, poured onto a hot pan so it crackles. The name is often explained as the sizzle of that pour. Central cities such as Da Nang and southern kitchens both have their own size and filling. A related crepe is also eaten in Cambodia, so Cambodia is listed beside Vietnam. The written recipe includes pork, shrimp, and fish sauce. It is not vegetarian.

Cultural background

The crepe is folded over the filling and brought to the table with a pile of lettuce, mint, and perilla. Diners tear pieces and wrap them. Nuoc cham, a dipping sauce of fish sauce, lime, sugar, and chile, is the usual bowl. The cake itself is only mildly hot. The dip can be hotter.

Why it tastes this way

Turmeric colors the rice and adds a light bitter note. Coconut milk softens that bitterness and helps the edge fry crisp. Pork and shrimp make the center savory and briny. Bean sprouts stay watery and fresh against the crackle. Herbs and the lime in the fish-sauce dip are what make a bite taste complete. The rice batter has no wheat, which is why a careful batch is gluten-free.

How it is made

Fry pork and shrimp, pour a turmeric rice batter over them in a hot pan, add bean sprouts, and fold the crepe.

Authentic touch. Use a hot pan and a thin pour, then leave it alone until the edge lifts and sounds dry. Stirring turns it into a scramble. The pork and shrimp should be nearly cooked before they are covered by the batter if they are in large pieces.

How it is used

Eat it in herb and lettuce wraps with nuoc cham. It is a savory street meal, shared from the pan.

Field notes

  • Banh xeo is a turmeric rice crepe with pork and shrimp, eaten with herbs.
  • Cambodia eats a related crepe, so it is listed beside Vietnam.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup rice flour, with no wheat added
  • 1 cup coconut milk
  • 1/2 teaspoon turmeric
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 150 grams thinly sliced pork belly or pork shoulder
  • 150 grams shrimp, peeled
  • 1 tablespoon fish sauce
  • 2 cups bean sprouts
  • Neutral oil
  • Lettuce and herbs to serve

Steps

  1. Whisk rice flour, coconut milk, turmeric, salt, and a splash of water into a thin batter. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Season pork and shrimp with a teaspoon of fish sauce. Stir the rest of the fish sauce into a small dip with lime, sugar, water, and chile if you want nuoc cham.
  3. Oil a hot pan. Fry a portion of pork and shrimp until the pork loses its pink and the shrimp turn opaque.
  4. Pour a ladle of batter over them and swirl a thin crepe. When the edge crisps, add bean sprouts, fold, and cook another minute.
  5. Repeat. Serve with herbs, lettuce, and the dip.

Cook the pork through and the shrimp until opaque.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A smaller crepe, one shrimp each, closer to some southern plates
  • Mung beans soaked and scattered in, a central-region habit

Pairings and uses

  • Street food
  • Lettuce, mint, and perilla
  • Nuoc cham
  • Pickled carrot and daikon

Banh khot

Tiny crisp-edged rice cakes from a dimpled pan, each topped with a shrimp and a soft coconut center.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 24 mini cakes

History

Banh khot are mini savory pancakes of southern Vietnam, cooked in a dimpled pan. Vung Tau is especially known for them. The batter is rice flour, turmeric, and coconut milk, close to a small banh xeo but shaped by the mold and usually topped with one shrimp rather than folded around pork. They are street food, served with herbs and a fish-sauce dip. This recipe has shrimp and fish sauce, so it is not vegetarian. It is a mold cake, not the large crepe.

Cultural background

The pans look like a cluster of small cups. A crisp rim and a slightly custardy center are the textures to chase. Quail eggs or pork appear in some stalls. Shrimp is the topping this page writes. The cakes are eaten as soon as they leave the mold.

Why it tastes this way

Coconut milk and turmeric give the same yellow, lightly bitter sweetness as a larger rice crepe, concentrated in a bite. The shrimp on top tastes briny and sweet. Fish sauce in the dip adds salt and acid. Because the cake is small and molded, the rim fries harder than the center. Herbs make each bite fresher than the batter alone.

How it is made

Pour turmeric-coconut rice batter into a hot dimpled pan, set a shrimp in each cup, and cook until the rims are crisp.

Authentic touch. Heat the molds until oil shimmers before you pour. A cool cup sticks and never crisps. One shrimp, pressed in while the batter is still wet, stays attached.

How it is used

Lift them out and eat them at once with herbs and nuoc cham. They are a snack or a street meal.

Field notes

  • Banh khot are the small molded cakes of southern Vietnam, especially Vung Tau.
  • They share a turmeric rice batter with banh xeo and use a dimpled pan instead of a wide crepe.
