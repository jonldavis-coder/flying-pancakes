English Shrove pancakes

Thin egg pancakes, lemon and sugar, made to clear the kitchen before Lent.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 thin pancakes

History

These are the thin English pancakes of Shrove Tuesday, the day before Lent. The batter uses the eggs, milk, and fat that a strict Lenten fast would set aside. Shrove Tuesday is one day, not a week, and it is not the French crepe holiday. In England the usual finish is lemon juice and sugar. Butter, jam, or a savory filling exist, but lemon and sugar is the plate this page records.

Cultural background

Pancake races and church bells are local Shrove customs in some English towns. The cake itself is older than those events and does not depend on them. A home cook only needs a pan and a toss they can afford to miss.

Why it tastes this way

The batter is loose with egg and milk, so it sets in a thin sheet instead of a fluffy stack. The surface browns in butter. Lemon cuts the fat, and sugar stays gritty for a moment on the hot cake. There is no baking powder, so the flavor is egg, milk, and browned butter rather than a soda tang.

How it is made

Whisk a thin batter of flour, egg, and milk. Cook one ladle at a time in a hot buttered pan, turning once, until both sides are spotted brown.

Authentic touch. Rest the batter for half an hour if you can. The first pancake is the test piece. Toss it or turn it with a spatula, then eat it while the next one cooks.

How it is used

On Shrove Tuesday, folded or rolled with lemon and sugar. They are a feast-day pudding more than a tall breakfast stack.

Field notes

  • Shrove Tuesday is the single day before Lent, not a butter week.
  • The first pancake is traditionally the cook's test, and it is still eaten.
  • Lemon and sugar, not syrup, is the usual English finish.

Ingredients

  • 100 g plain wheat flour
  • 2 large eggs
  • 300 ml whole milk
  • 1 pinch fine salt
  • 30 g unsalted butter, for the pan and for serving
  • 2 lemons, cut into wedges
  • 3 tbsp sugar

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour and salt. Beat in the eggs, then the milk, until the batter is as thin as light cream. Rest it for 20 minutes if you have the time.
  2. Heat a frying pan over medium-high heat and grease it with a little butter.
  3. Pour in a thin layer of batter and swirl. Cook until the top looks dry and the underside is golden, about 1 minute. Turn and cook the second side for about 30 seconds.
  4. Fold or roll onto a warm plate. Add a squeeze of lemon and a spoon of sugar. Repeat, buttering the pan lightly between pancakes.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Orange juice instead of lemon
  • A savory ham and cheese fold, which is a later meal and not the Shrove plate

Pairings and uses

  • Feast or fast day
  • Dessert
  • Breakfast
  • Lemon wedges and sugar
  • A little jam, if lemon is not on the table
  • Tea
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

English pikelets

Small freeform yeast cakes with a holey top, toasted and buttered.

1 hr 30 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 pikelets

History

In England a pikelet is a small yeast griddle cake, close to a crumpet but cooked without a ring. The batter sits in a free puddle, so the cake stays flatter and wider than a ring-bound crumpet. The name is used differently in Australia, and that cake has its own page. This one is the Midlands and northern English pikelet.

Cultural background

Pikelets belong to tea tables and breakfasts. They are toasted, then buttered, so the butter runs into the holes. Some home recipes now use baking powder. This one keeps the yeast.

Why it tastes this way

Yeast and a loose batter make an open, damp top. The bottom browns against the griddle. Toasting dries the surface just enough for butter to melt in. The taste is mildly sour from the yeast, not sweet unless you add jam.

How it is made

A yeast batter of flour, milk, and water rises, then a pinch of baking soda is stirred in. Spoonfuls cook on a greased griddle until the top is set and honeycombed.

Authentic touch. Do not use crumpet rings for this page. Spoon the batter freely. If the top is not full of holes, the batter is too thick or the pan is too hot.

How it is used

Toasted and buttered, with jam or honey if you want them sweet. Honey adds a honey allergen and means the plate is not vegan, which this batter was not anyway.

Field notes

  • English pikelets are cooked without rings. Crumpets use rings.
  • Australian pikelets are a different cake and a different page.
  • The holes are for melted butter, which is why they are toasted.

Ingredients

  • 225 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tsp instant yeast
  • 1 tsp sugar
  • 250 ml warm milk
  • 100 ml warm water
  • 1 large egg
  • 1/2 tsp fine salt
  • 1/2 tsp baking soda
  • Butter, for the griddle and for serving

Steps

  1. Stir the flour, yeast, sugar, warm milk, water, egg, and salt into a batter like thick cream. Cover and leave until bubbly, about 1 hour.
  2. Dissolve the baking soda in a spoon of water and stir it in. The batter should look busy with bubbles. Rest 10 minutes.
  3. Grease a griddle over medium heat. Spoon on small rounds and cook until the top is full of holes and no longer liquid.
  4. Turn and brown the other side briefly. Cool on a cloth. Toast later and butter them while hot.

The batter should smell yeasty. If it smells rotten, discard it.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A baking-powder pikelet, faster, with a different flavor
  • Cheese melted on a toasted pikelet

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Snack
  • Cold butter
  • Jam
  • Strong tea
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

English crumpets

Ring-cooked yeast cakes, toasted so butter sinks into the holes.

1 hr 40 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 crumpets

History

Crumpets are English yeast cakes cooked in rings. The ring holds a loose batter so the top stays wet and forms holes while the base sets. Pikelets, on the previous page, are the freeform cousin. A crumpet is meant to be toasted, not only eaten straight from the pan. Shop crumpets are the same idea baked to a schedule. This is a home batter.

Cultural background

They sit on a breakfast or tea table with butter. Cheese, jam, or a poached egg are all reasonable. The cake is a vehicle for something that melts.

Why it tastes this way

The inside stays soft and slightly tangy from yeast. Toasting makes a dry, browned rim so butter has somewhere to go. The holes are not decoration. They are the texture that separates a crumpet from a dropped scone.

How it is made

Yeast batter rises, baking soda is added, and the batter is poured into greased rings on a warm griddle. The cakes are cooled, then toasted.

Authentic touch. Grease the rings as well as the griddle. Cook the top until the bubbles have burst and the surface is set before you turn the cake. A wet turn makes a flat crumpet.

How it is used

Toasted on a fork or under a grill, with butter. They are a snack or breakfast, not a dessert course.

Field notes

  • The ring is what lets the batter stay deep enough to form holes.
  • Crumpets are toasted before they are eaten, even when just cooked.
  • Without rings, the same family of batter becomes pikelets.

Ingredients

  • 225 g strong white wheat flour
  • 1 tsp instant yeast
  • 1 tsp sugar
  • 200 ml warm milk
  • 150 ml warm water
  • 1/2 tsp fine salt
  • 1/2 tsp baking soda
  • Butter, for the rings, the griddle, and serving

Steps

  1. Mix the flour, yeast, sugar, milk, water, and salt into a smooth batter. Cover until foamy, about 1 hour.
  2. Stir the baking soda into a spoon of warm water, beat it in, and rest the batter 15 minutes. It should be pourable and bubbly.
  3. Grease crumpet rings and a griddle. Set the rings on medium-low heat and fill them halfway. Cook until the top is open with holes and dry to the touch.
  4. Remove the rings, turn the crumpets, and color the tops for a minute. Cool, then toast and butter.

Discard the batter if it smells spoiled rather than yeasty.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Wholemeal flour for part of the white flour
  • A cheese and chutney topping

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Snack
  • Butter
  • A slice of cheese, melted in the holes
  • Jam
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Staffordshire oatcakes

Large, soft, yeasted oatmeal pancakes from the Potteries, rolled around a savory filling.

1 hr 20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 large oatcakes

History

Staffordshire oatcakes are yeasted oatmeal pancakes from the Potteries, the towns around Stoke-on-Trent. They are large, thin, and flexible, sold in oatcake shops and rolled around bacon, cheese, or sausage. They are not the dry Scottish oatcake, and they are not the Derbyshire batter on the next page. Both counties use yeast and oats. The Staffordshire cake is oat-heavy and meant to fold.

Cultural background

An oatcake shop batter is a local product. Home cooks keep a jug of it warm so the yeast stays alive. The cake is breakfast and lunch food, eaten hot.

Why it tastes this way

Oatmeal makes the crumb soft and a little sweet, and the yeast adds a mild sour note. The griddle dries the surface without turning it into a biscuit. A salty filling, cheese or bacon, is the usual contrast. This recipe leaves the filling for the plate so the batter stays meat-free.

How it is made

Fine oatmeal and a smaller share of wheat flour are mixed with yeast and warm milk, left until bubbly, then poured as large thin rounds on a greased bakestone or frying pan.

Authentic touch. Cook them large and thin enough to roll. A cake that cracks when folded needs a looser batter or a slightly hotter pan and a shorter cook.

How it is used

Hot, rolled or folded with cheese. Bacon or sausage is the shop-counter filling and adds meat. The batter itself is vegetarian.

Field notes

  • Potteries oatcakes are soft and rolled. Scottish oatcakes are crisp and dry.
  • Oatcake shops in Stoke-on-Trent still sell them as everyday food.
  • The batter is yeasted. It is not a baking-powder pancake.

Ingredients

  • 200 g fine oatmeal
  • 80 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tsp instant yeast
  • 1 tsp sugar
  • 450 ml warm milk and water, mixed half and half
  • 1/2 tsp fine salt
  • Oil or butter, for the pan
  • 100 g cheddar, for serving

Steps

  1. Stir the oatmeal, flour, yeast, sugar, salt, and warm liquid into a batter like thin cream. Cover and leave until foamy, about 1 hour.
  2. Heat a wide pan or griddle and grease it lightly.
  3. Pour a thin round and cook until the top sets and the underside is pale gold. Turn once and cook through.
  4. Stack the cakes in a cloth so they stay flexible. Fill with cheese, roll, and eat hot.

Keep the rising batter covered. Discard it if it smells rotten.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Derbyshire oatcakes, a related but thicker local batter
  • A sweet roll with butter and honey, less typical than the savory shop cake

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • Melted cheddar
  • Bacon or a pork sausage, if you eat meat
  • A grilled tomato
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Derbyshire oatcakes

A yeasted oatmeal pancake from Derbyshire, a little thicker than the Staffordshire shop cake.

1 hr 20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 10 oatcakes

History

Derbyshire oatcakes are the Peak District's yeasted oatmeal pancakes. They share a method with Staffordshire oatcakes and they are not the same batter. This page uses equal parts oatmeal and wheat flour and an egg, and it cooks a smaller, thicker cake. Bakers in the two counties do not treat the recipes as interchangeable. Neither cake is a Scottish oatcake, which is unleavened and dry.

Cultural background

They are breakfast food in Derbyshire, eaten with butter, cheese, or bacon. The batter is mixed ahead so the yeast can work. A cold kitchen will take longer than an hour.

Why it tastes this way

The extra wheat and the egg make a cake that holds together when it is thicker. Oats keep it soft. Yeast keeps the sweetness in check. You taste grain first, then the salty filling.

How it is made

Oatmeal, wheat flour, yeast, egg, and warm milk ferment into a batter. Spoonfuls cook on a greased griddle until set.

Authentic touch. Do not roll these as thin as a Potteries oatcake. If the batter will not drop from a spoon in a thick ribbon, add a splash of milk.

How it is used

Buttered, or filled with cheese. They are a morning savory cake, not a dessert stack.

Field notes

  • Derbyshire and Staffordshire both keep yeasted oat pancakes, with different local batters.
  • This batter includes an egg. The Staffordshire page does not.
  • They are soft griddle cakes, not the crisp Scottish biscuit.

Ingredients

  • 150 g fine oatmeal
  • 150 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tsp instant yeast
  • 1 tsp sugar
  • 1 large egg
  • 400 ml warm milk
  • 1/2 tsp fine salt
  • Butter, for the griddle

Steps

  1. Mix the oatmeal, flour, yeast, sugar, egg, milk, and salt. Cover until the batter is bubbly, about 1 hour.
  2. Stir it down. It should drop from a spoon in a ribbon. Add a spoon of milk if it is stiff.
  3. Grease a medium-hot griddle. Cook small rounds until the top sets, then turn and brown the second side.
  4. Serve warm with butter or cheese. They reheat in a dry pan.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • The oat-heavier Staffordshire oatcake, made to fold
  • A spoon of honey on a plain one

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • Butter
  • Cheese
  • Bacon, for a meat plate
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Singing hinnies

Currant griddle cakes from Northumberland. The fat sizzles, which is the singing.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 cakes

History

Singing hinnies are currant griddle cakes from Northumberland and the north-east of England. The usual kitchen explanation of the name is that the fat sings on a hot griddle, and that hinny is a local word for someone dear. That is an explanation of a name, not a dated record, and this page treats it as that. The cake is a fat-rubbed dough cooked on a girdle, split, and buttered.

Cultural background

They belong to a tea table in the north-east. Currants are the fruit. There is no yeast. Chemical leavener in this recipe is a home convenience so the cake is not leaden.

Why it tastes this way

Butter in the dough and butter on the griddle brown at the edge. Currants sweeten single bites rather than the whole crumb. The inside stays short, closer to a scone than to a crepe. A hot griddle is what makes the fat sing and the base crisp.

How it is made

Fat is rubbed into flour with currants and a little leavener, mixed to a soft dough with milk, rolled into rounds, and cooked on a greased griddle.

Authentic touch. Use enough fat that you can hear it. A dry pan will not sing, and the cake will toughen. Split the hinny while warm and butter the cut face.

How it is used

Split and buttered, as a snack or a substantial tea. They are not a poured pancake.

Field notes

  • The singing is the sound of fat on a hot griddle.
  • They are a Northumberland cake, cut and buttered like a griddle scone.
  • Currants, not chocolate, are the fruit in the dough.

Ingredients

  • 250 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tsp baking powder
  • 1/2 tsp fine salt
  • 60 g cold unsalted butter, plus more for the griddle
  • 40 g currants
  • 120 ml whole milk

Steps

  1. Mix the flour, baking powder, and salt. Rub in the cold butter until the bowl looks like crumbs. Stir in the currants.
  2. Add the milk and bring the dough together. It should be soft, not wet. Pat into rounds about 1 cm thick.
  3. Cook on a buttered griddle over medium heat until the underside is brown, then turn. You should hear the fat.
  4. Split each cake and butter it. Eat while warm.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A few strips of candied peel with the currants
  • Lard in place of some of the butter, which adds a meat allergen and removes the vegetarian diet

Pairings and uses

  • Snack
  • Breakfast
  • Extra butter
  • A slice of cheese
  • Tea
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Lancashire potato cakes

Thick mashed-potato cakes, fried, from Lancashire breakfasts.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 cakes

History

Lancashire potato cakes are mashed potato bound with flour and fried. They are a breakfast cake, often made when there is mash left from the day before. They are not Irish boxty, which uses raw grated potato as well as mash, and they are not Scottish tattie scones, which are rolled thin and cooked on a dry girdle. This version is a thicker round, fried in butter.

Cultural background

Potato cakes of this sort belong to the industrial breakfast as much as to a farmhouse. They use a staple that stores. The flavor depends on the potato, so a watery mash makes a weak cake.

Why it tastes this way

Flour and a little butter keep mashed potato from falling apart, and frying browns the outside. The inside stays soft and salty. Pepper is the usual seasoning. There is no egg in this recipe, so the cake tastes of potato rather than of omelet.

How it is made

Cold mashed potato is worked with flour, butter, and salt, shaped into thick rounds, and fried in butter until crusted.

Authentic touch. Dry the mash. A cake that weeps in the pan was too wet. Flour is the binder here, which means the cake is not gluten-free.

How it is used

At breakfast, with eggs or bacon, or with butter and salt alone. Bacon makes the plate a meat meal.

Field notes

  • These use mashed potato only. Boxty also uses raw grated potato.
  • Leftover mash is the usual reason to make them.
  • They are fried rounds, not the thin triangles of a tattie scone.

Ingredients

  • 500 g cold mashed potato, made without much liquid
  • 80 g plain wheat flour, plus more for shaping
  • 30 g unsalted butter, melted, plus more for frying
  • 1/2 tsp fine salt
  • 1/4 tsp black pepper

Steps

  1. Mix the mash, flour, melted butter, salt, and pepper into a soft dough. Add a spoon more flour if it sticks.
  2. Shape eight thick rounds on a floured board.
  3. Fry in butter over medium heat until both sides are brown and the center is hot.
  4. Serve at once with pepper, eggs, or bacon.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of chopped chives in the mash
  • Scottish tattie scones, which are thinner and cooked more dry

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • Fried eggs
  • Bacon
  • Butter and black pepper
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Scotch pancakes

Small thick drop scones from a Scottish girdle, butter and jam.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 16 small pancakes

History

Scotch pancakes, also called drop scones, are small thick cakes cooked on a girdle. They are not the thin English Shrove pancake and they are not a crepe. A modern batter uses baking powder or buttermilk and soda. Earlier dropped cakes relied more on eggs. This recipe uses baking powder and buttermilk because that is the home cake people mean by the name now. The country pin is the United Kingdom, and the culture is Scottish.

Cultural background

They are a tea-table and breakfast cake across Scotland. A heavy girdle, or a thick frying pan, is the tool. The cakes are eaten the day they are cooked.

Why it tastes this way

Buttermilk and a quick leavener make a soft, slightly tangy crumb. The girdle browns the outside before the inside dries. Sugar in the batter is light. Jam does the rest. Overmixing makes them tough, because the flour is wheat.

How it is made

Flour, egg, buttermilk, and baking powder are mixed briefly and dropped onto a lightly greased girdle.

Authentic touch. Drop the batter from a spoon. Do not spread it into a crepe. Turn when bubbles sit open on the top, and only once.

How it is used

Warm, with butter and jam, at breakfast or tea. They are not rolled.

Field notes

  • Drop scone is the other name. The cake is thick and small.
  • They are a different batter from English Shrove pancakes.
  • A girdle is a griddle. The spelling is the Scottish one.

Ingredients

  • 150 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tsp baking powder
  • 1 tbsp sugar
  • 1 pinch fine salt
  • 1 large egg
  • 180 ml buttermilk
  • 20 g unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the girdle

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, baking powder, sugar, and salt. Beat the egg with the buttermilk and melted butter.
  2. Stir the wet ingredients into the dry until just combined. A few lumps are fine.
  3. Grease a girdle or heavy pan over medium heat. Drop spoonfuls and cook until bubbles open on top.
  4. Turn once and cook until the centers spring back. Butter them and serve with jam.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of raisins in the batter
  • Thick cream instead of butter

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Snack
  • Butter
  • Raspberry jam or another jam
  • Tea
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Scottish oatcakes

Thin, dry, unleavened oatcakes from a Scottish girdle.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · 12 oatcakes

History

Scottish oatcakes are thin unleavened oat breads cooked on a girdle. They are a staple accompaniment, not a dessert pancake, and they are not the yeasted oatcakes of Staffordshire or Derbyshire. Oats were the grain that reliably grew in much of Scotland. This recipe uses oatmeal, salt, a little fat, and hot water. There is no wheat flour in it.

Cultural background

An oatcake is travel food and table food. It is eaten with cheese, soup, or fish, and it keeps better than a soft pancake. The texture should be crisp after cooling, not floppy.

Why it tastes this way

Toasted oatmeal is nutty even though the recipe has no nuts. Salt and a little fat keep it from tasting raw. The girdle dries the cake. Hot water softens the oats just enough to roll. They taste plain on purpose, because cheese or a salty fish is supposed to do the seasoning.

How it is made

Medium oatmeal is mixed with salt, a pinch of baking soda, oil, and hot water, rolled thin, cut, and baked on a dry or lightly oiled girdle until firm.

Authentic touch. Roll them thin and cook them slowly. Use oats milled apart from wheat if you need them gluten-free. Ordinary oatmeal is often handled near wheat, so the gluten-free label depends on the oats you buy.

How it is used

With soup, cheese, or smoked fish. The cakes themselves are vegan. A herring or a buttered slice changes that plate.

Field notes

  • These are unleavened and crisp. Staffordshire oatcakes are yeasted and soft.
  • They are an accompaniment as often as they are a snack.
  • The recipe has no wheat unless you choose the variation that adds it.

Ingredients

  • 200 g medium oatmeal, plus more for rolling
  • 1/2 tsp fine salt
  • 1/4 tsp baking soda
  • 20 ml neutral oil
  • 120 ml hot water

Steps

  1. Stir the oatmeal, salt, and baking soda. Add the oil and hot water and mix to a stiff dough. Rest 5 minutes.
  2. Roll or press the dough thin on an oatmeal-dusted board. Cut rounds or triangles.
  3. Cook on a medium-low girdle until the edges lift and the cake is dry and pale gold, then turn and finish the other side.
  4. Cool on a rack until crisp. Eat with cheese, soup, or fish.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of wheat flour, which is common and removes the gluten-free diet
  • Fine oatmeal for a smoother cake, or pinhead oats kept coarse

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Accompaniment to a meal
  • Snack
  • Cheddar or a hard cheese
  • Smoked fish
  • A bowl of soup
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Tattie scones

Thin potato triangles from a Scottish girdle, eaten at breakfast.

15 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 triangles

History

Tattie scones are Scottish potato scones: mashed potato, a little flour and fat, rolled thin, cut into triangles, and cooked on a girdle. Tattie means potato. They are part of a fried breakfast or a weekend plate with eggs. They are not Lancashire's thicker fried rounds, and they are not boxty. Flour in the dough means they are not gluten-free.

Cultural background

Cold mash from the night before is the usual start. The scones are cooked in a dry or lightly greased pan so the outside toasts without becoming a fritter.

Why it tastes this way

Potato makes them mild and soft. A thin roll means the heat reaches the center and the surface dries. Salt and pepper matter because there is little else. Browning at the edge is the flavor. Butter on the hot scone melts into the face that was against the girdle.

How it is made

Mashed potato is kneaded with butter and flour, rolled into a thin round, quartered, and cooked on a hot girdle until spotted.

Authentic touch. Cut the round into quarters. A farl here is a practical cut, not the Ulster soda farl. Keep them thin enough that you can see the girdle color through the dough at the edge.

How it is used

At breakfast, with eggs, bacon, or butter. They are a savory cake.

Field notes

  • Tattie is potato. The scones are potato, not a sweet oven scone.
  • They are rolled thin and cut in triangles or quarters.
  • They belong on a Scottish breakfast plate.

Ingredients

  • 400 g cold mashed potato
  • 30 g unsalted butter, plus more for serving
  • 80 g plain wheat flour, plus more for rolling
  • 1/2 tsp fine salt
  • 1/4 tsp black pepper

Steps

  1. Mash the potato with the butter, salt, and pepper until smooth. Work in the flour to a soft dough.
  2. Roll into two thin rounds on a floured board and cut each into quarters.
  3. Cook on a hot dry girdle until brown spots appear, then turn and cook the other side.
  4. Serve warm with butter, eggs, or bacon.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of chopped chives
  • Lancashire potato cakes, thicker and pan-fried in more fat

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Eggs
  • Bacon or square sausage, for a meat breakfast

Welsh crempog

Welsh buttermilk griddle cakes, stacked with butter between the layers.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 small cakes

History

Crempog, plural crempogau, is the Welsh griddle cake. Buttermilk and bicarbonate are a common home batter, and yeasted overnight batters are also traditional. This page uses buttermilk. The cakes are stacked with butter between them, which is the serving that matters. Some families make a stack for a birthday, and some make crempog when eggs and fat are used up before Lent. Neither occasion is the only one.

Cultural background

The cake is Welsh-language food as well as a recipe. The country on the map is the United Kingdom. The culture is Welsh. A heavy pan or bakestone is the tool.

Why it tastes this way

Buttermilk and soda give a light tang and a soft crumb. Butter between hot cakes melts and salts each layer. The outside is only just browned. They taste less of sugar than a dessert crepe, unless you add jam.

How it is made

Flour, egg, buttermilk, melted butter, and baking soda are mixed to a pourable batter and cooked as small rounds, then stacked with butter.

Authentic touch. Stack them with a smear of butter on each hot cake, not only on the top one. A yeasted batter, left overnight, is the other honest version.

How it is used

In a buttered stack, at breakfast, tea, a birthday, or Shrove. Jam is optional.

Field notes

  • The plural is crempogau. The stack is buttered between the cakes.
  • Buttermilk versions and yeast versions both exist.
  • Some Welsh families stack them for a birthday.

Ingredients

  • 200 g plain wheat flour
  • 1/2 tsp baking soda
  • 1 pinch fine salt
  • 2 large eggs
  • 300 ml buttermilk
  • 25 g unsalted butter, melted, plus more for stacking and the pan

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, baking soda, and salt. Beat in the eggs, buttermilk, and melted butter until smooth.
  2. Grease a bakestone or frying pan over medium heat.
  3. Cook small ladlefuls until the top dulls, then turn and color the other side.
  4. Stack the cakes with butter between them. Serve the stack warm.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A yeasted crempog batter left to rise overnight
  • A little oatmeal in place of part of the wheat flour

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Feast or fast day
  • Dessert
  • Salted butter
  • Jam
  • Tea
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Irish boxty

Pan boxty: raw grated potato and mashed potato, fried as cakes.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 pan cakes

History

Boxty is an Irish potato cake. There are three forms: pan boxty, loaf boxty, and dumpling boxty. This page is only the pan cake. The batter mixes raw grated potato with mashed potato and flour, then the cakes are fried. Potatoes reached Irish kitchens and became a staple. The cake is one of the ways a raw potato was cooked without waiting for a roast. Flour means this recipe is not gluten-free.

Cultural background

Pan boxty is breakfast and supper food. It is associated with the north and the midlands as much as with any single county. This page does not assign it to one village.

Why it tastes this way

Raw potato brings starch and a clean flavor. Mash brings softness. Flour binds the two. Frying browns the starch. If you rinse all the starch off the grated potato, the cake falls apart and tastes watery. Salt is the seasoning. A crisp edge and a soft center is the point.

How it is made

Equal parts raw grated potato and mashed potato are mixed with flour, salt, and a little milk, then fried as flat cakes in butter or oil.

Authentic touch. Grate the raw potato and keep the liquid that settles into starch. Pour off the water and put the starch back. That paste helps the cake hold.

How it is used

Hot, with butter, eggs, or bacon. The cake on this page is vegetarian. Bacon is a serving choice, not part of the batter.

Field notes

  • Pan, loaf, and dumpling boxty are three methods. This is the pan cake.
  • The mix is raw grated potato plus mashed potato, not mash alone.
  • The starch from the grated potato helps bind the cake.

Ingredients

  • 250 g raw potatoes, peeled
  • 250 g cold mashed potato
  • 100 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tsp fine salt
  • 60 ml milk
  • 30 g unsalted butter, for frying

Steps

  1. Grate the raw potatoes onto a clean cloth. Squeeze the liquid into a bowl. Let it sit so the starch settles, pour off the water, and keep the starch.
  2. Mix the squeezed potato, the starch, the mash, flour, salt, and enough milk to make a stiff batter.
  3. Heat the butter in a frying pan. Shape flat cakes and fry over medium heat until both sides are brown and the raw potato is cooked through, about 4 minutes a side.
  4. Rest them a minute and serve with butter or eggs.

The raw potato must cook through. Cut one cake open. The center should be soft and hot, not glassy.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Boxty loaf, baked as a bread, which is a different method
  • Boxty dumpling, boiled, which is also a different method

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • Butter
  • Fried eggs
  • Bacon, if you want a meat breakfast
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Irish potato farls

Mashed-potato farls, cut in quarters and cooked on a griddle.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 farls

History

Potato farls are a mashed-potato bread cooked on a griddle and cut into quarters. Farl means a fourth. They are an Ulster breakfast bread eaten in Ireland, which is why this page is pinned to Ireland. The wheat soda farl, a different dough, is on the Ulster soda farl page under the United Kingdom. Potato farls use mash, flour, and fat. They do not use the raw potato that defines pan boxty.

Cultural background

They are weekend breakfast food beside eggs, bacon, or soda bread. The dough should be soft and the cakes thin enough to cook through on a dry griddle.

Why it tastes this way

Mashed potato makes a mild, stretchy crumb once it meets a hot griddle. Flour keeps the shape. Butter in the dough tastes nutty when the surface toasts. They are saltier and drier than a fried potato cake, because the pan is only lightly greased.

How it is made

Cold mash is mixed with butter, salt, and flour, pressed into rounds, quartered, and cooked on a hot griddle until spotted brown.

Authentic touch. Cut the round into four before you cook it. Do not grate in raw potato. If you do, you have started a boxty, and that recipe is the other page.

How it is used

At breakfast, with butter, eggs, or a fry. The farls themselves are vegetarian.

Field notes

  • A farl is a quarter of the round.
  • This cake is mashed potato. Boxty also uses raw potato.
  • The pin is Ireland. Ulster soda farls are the wheat cake on the United Kingdom page.

Ingredients

  • 500 g cold mashed potato
  • 40 g unsalted butter
  • 1 tsp fine salt
  • 100 g plain wheat flour, plus more for pressing

Steps

  1. Beat the mash with the butter and salt. Work in the flour until you have a soft dough that does not stick.
  2. Divide in two. Press each half into a round about 1 cm thick on a floured board and cut into quarters.
  3. Cook on a hot dry griddle until brown spots show, then turn and cook the other side.
  4. Serve warm with butter.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of chopped spring onion
  • Tattie scones, the Scottish thin triangle of the same family

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • Butter
  • Eggs
  • Bacon or sausage on a meat plate
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Ulster soda farls

Griddled soda-bread farls from Ulster, cut in quarters.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 large farls

History

Ulster soda farls are soda bread cooked on a griddle and cut into four. The country pin is the United Kingdom because Northern Ireland is the place on this map. The culture is Ulster. Buttermilk and baking soda are the leaven. There is no yeast and no potato in this dough. Potato farls are the other breakfast cake, pinned to Ireland. Both can appear on one Ulster plate. They are different doughs.

Cultural background

A soda farl is breakfast bread and an accompaniment to a fry, soup, or cheese. The griddle gives it a floury crust that an oven soda loaf does not have on the cut side.

Why it tastes this way

Buttermilk and soda taste slightly tangy and mineral. The griddle toasts the bottom. The inside stays close-grained and mild. Salt is low. Butter on the hot farl is most of the flavor people remember.

How it is made

Flour, salt, and baking soda are mixed with buttermilk to a soft dough, patted into a round, cut into farls, and cooked on a dry griddle.

Authentic touch. Handle the dough lightly and cook it as a round you have already quartered. A thin knife eased under the farl keeps it from tearing when you turn it.

How it is used

With butter, at breakfast, or beside soup. They are an accompaniment as much as a cake.

Field notes

  • The pin is the United Kingdom. The culture on the page is Ulster.
  • Soda farls are wheat and buttermilk. Potato farls are mashed potato.
  • Farl means the round was cut into four.

Ingredients

  • 250 g plain wheat flour, plus more for shaping
  • 1/2 tsp fine salt
  • 1/2 tsp baking soda
  • 180 ml buttermilk

Steps

  1. Sift the flour, salt, and baking soda. Stir in the buttermilk until a soft dough forms. Do not knead more than a few turns.
  2. Pat into a round about 2 cm thick on a floured board and cut into four.
  3. Cook on a medium-hot dry griddle for about 8 minutes a side, until the crust is brown and the center is cooked.
  4. Cool slightly, split, and butter.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of porridge oats on the board, pressed into the top
  • Oven soda bread, which is the loaf form and not this page

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Accompaniment to a meal
  • Butter
  • A fried breakfast
  • Cheese and an apple
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Irish oatmeal pancakes

Soft oatmeal pancakes with buttermilk, a breakfast cake from Irish kitchens.

20 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 10 pancakes

History

Oatmeal pancakes in Ireland use the porridge grain in a softer, egg-and-buttermilk batter. They are not Scottish oatcakes, which are thin and dry, and they are not Staffordshire's yeasted shop cakes. Soaking the oatmeal first keeps the texture from turning gritty. Wheat flour in this batter means the cakes are not gluten-free.

Cultural background

They are a home breakfast. Oats were everyday food, and a pancake was one way to serve them with butter and honey or jam. Honey, if you add it, is its own allergen.

Why it tastes this way

Soaked oats stay tender and taste toasted once the pan browns them. Buttermilk gives a quiet tang. The cakes are thicker than a crepe and less fluffy than a baking-powder drop scone. Butter on top is the richness. Sugar is not required in the batter.

How it is made

Oatmeal soaks in buttermilk, then flour, an egg, salt, and a pinch of baking soda are stirred in. The batter is cooked as small thick pancakes.

Authentic touch. Soak the oatmeal in the buttermilk for at least 15 minutes. Skipping the soak leaves hard flecks in the crumb.

How it is used

At breakfast, with butter and jam. They are a morning cake.

Field notes

  • The oatmeal is soaked so the pancake is not gritty.
  • This is a soft breakfast pancake, not a crisp Scottish oatcake.
  • Wheat flour in the batter means it is not gluten-free.

Ingredients

  • 100 g medium oatmeal
  • 250 ml buttermilk
  • 80 g plain wheat flour
  • 1/2 tsp baking soda
  • 1 pinch fine salt
  • 1 large egg
  • Butter, for the pan and for serving

Steps

  1. Stir the oatmeal into the buttermilk and leave it for 15 minutes.
  2. Whisk in the flour, baking soda, salt, and egg.
  3. Cook spoonfuls in a buttered pan over medium heat until the bubbles set, then turn and finish.
  4. Serve warm with butter and jam.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A handful of raisins
  • All oatmeal and no wheat flour, which is a different cake and would need checking before you call it gluten-free

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Butter
  • Jam
  • Honey, which adds a honey allergen
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free
