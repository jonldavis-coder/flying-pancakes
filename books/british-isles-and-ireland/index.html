Irish Shrove Tuesday Pancakes

Thin, lemony pancakes fried in butter for Shrove Tuesday.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 pancakes

History

In Ireland, Shrove Tuesday is Pancake Tuesday, the day before Lent. Households used up eggs, milk, and butter in thin pancakes before the fast. The custom is the same Christian calendar as England’s Shrove pancakes, and the Irish cake is typically thin, closer to a crepe than to a thick American stack. Lemon and sugar are the usual finish.

Cultural background

Families still cook a stack and argue over who flips. It is a one-day ritual that many people also cook on ordinary nights because the batter is simple.

Why it tastes this way

The taste is egg, butter, and lemon because those were the rich foods to finish before Lent, not because the pancake needed a special spice. A thin batter cooks fast and takes a sharp squeeze of lemon, which cuts the butter. The religious calendar, more than the climate, is why the cake has a date attached.

How it is made

A thin batter of flour, egg, and milk is cooked in a small skillet and finished with lemon and sugar.

Authentic touch. The pan should be hot and only filmed with butter. A thick pool of batter becomes a different, heavier cake and will not toss.

How it is used

Lemon juice and sugar. A few cooks use jam or a chocolate spread. Lemon and sugar are the Shrove plate.

Field notes

  • Pancake Tuesday in Ireland is Shrove Tuesday, the day before Ash Wednesday.
  • The cake is thin. A fluffy stack is a different breakfast.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 large egg
  • 1 1/4 cups milk
  • 1 tablespoon melted butter, plus more for the pan
  • Pinch of salt
  • Lemon and sugar, to serve

Steps

  1. Whisk the batter until smooth and rest it 15 minutes.
  2. Heat a small skillet and film it with butter.
  3. Pour a thin layer and swirl.
  4. Cook until the edge lifts, then flip for half a minute.
  5. Stack with lemon and sugar.
  6. The first pancake is the cook’s treat. It tells you the heat.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A thicker buttermilk drop scone is a different Irish cake, on its own page.
  • Orange instead of lemon is a fair home swap and tastes less sharp.

Pairings and uses

Boxty

Half grated raw potato, half mashed potato, browned in a pan.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 cakes

History

Boxty is an Irish potato bread especially associated with Ulster, Connacht, and the northwest. A traditional rhyme says boxty on the griddle and boxty in the pan. There are pan cakes, baked loaves, and boiled dumplings. This page is the pan cake: raw grated potato and mashed potato together. Potatoes became a central Irish food in the eighteenth century, and boxty is one of the breads that came out of that dependence, including through the years around the Famine.

Cultural background

It is a meal with butter, or with bacon and eggs. The raw potato keeps it from tasting like a leftover-mash patty.

Why it tastes this way

Grated raw potato brings water and a clean starch that crisps. Mashed potato brings a soft, cooked sweetness. Together they taste more potato than a flour pancake and more tender than a latke made only of shreds. The wheat flour is only a binder. Ireland’s cool, wet climate favored the potato as a staple, which is why a bread was built from it.

How it is made

Equal parts squeezed raw potato and mashed potato are mixed with a little flour, egg, and milk, then fried as cakes.

Authentic touch. Squeeze the grated potato dry, and keep the starch that settles from the liquid. Stir the batter just before frying. It darkens if it waits.

How it is used

Butter, or beside bacon and eggs. Some cooks serve it with a fried egg on top.

Field notes

  • Pan, griddle, and boiled boxty are three methods. This recipe is the pan cake.
  • Use a floury potato for the mash and a dry grate. A waxy potato stays gluey.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups grated raw potato, squeezed dry
  • 1 cup cold mashed potato
  • 1/2 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 large egg
  • 1/4 cup milk
  • 3/4 teaspoon salt
  • Butter or oil for frying

Steps

  1. Squeeze the grated potato in a towel. Add back any settled starch.
  2. Mix with mash, flour, egg, milk, and salt.
  3. Heat a buttered skillet over medium.
  4. Fry flattened rounds until brown on both sides and cooked through.
  5. Keep the heat moderate so the raw potato cooks before the crust burns.
  6. Serve hot with butter.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Boxty bread is the same idea baked as a round loaf.
  • Boxty dumplings are boiled. They are a different dish from the pan cake.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Supper
  • Side dish
  • butter

Irish Potato Farls

Soft potato flatbread, spotted brown, with a floury face.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 farls

History

Potato farls are a northern Irish griddle bread made from mashed potato and flour, cut into quarters. “Farl” means a quarter. They are an Ulster breakfast bread, cousins of boxty but smoother, because the potato is cooked first. Scottish tattie scones are a close relative. The bread belongs to the potato diet of Ireland and to the griddle that also cooked soda farls.

Cultural background

An Ulster fry often includes a farl alongside soda bread. The farl itself can be vegetarian. The fry around it usually is not.

Why it tastes this way

Mashed potato and flour, cooked dry on a griddle, taste mild and slightly sweet, with a fried spot where the surface dries. There is no egg and often no milk, so the flavor is potato rather than batter. The north’s breakfast griddle, used for several breads in one morning, is why farls are flat and quick instead of oven-risen.

How it is made

Hot mashed potato is mixed with flour and salt, rolled into a round, quartered, and cooked on a dry or lightly floured griddle.

Authentic touch. The dough should be soft and rolled thicker than a cracker, about a half inch. A dry dough cracks. Cook it on a moderate griddle so the center heats before the flour scorches.

How it is used

Butter, or as part of a breakfast fry. They are a bread.

Field notes

  • Farl means a quarter of the round.
  • Potato farls and boxty both use potato. Farls start with mash only.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups warm mashed potato, plain
  • 1/2 cup all-purpose flour, plus more for rolling
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Mix the warm mash, flour, and salt into a soft dough.
  2. Roll on a floured board into a round about 1/2 inch thick. Cut into quarters.
  3. Heat a dry griddle over medium.
  4. Cook the farls until spotted brown, a few minutes a side.
  5. They should be hot in the center.
  6. Serve with butter.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A knob of butter in the mash is richer and adds dairy.
  • Tattie scones in Scotland are the close cousin.

Pairings and uses

Irish Soda Farls

Soft soda bread, griddled, with a tender crumb and a floury crust.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 farls

History

Soda farls are the griddled form of Irish soda bread, especially in Ulster. Baking soda and buttermilk became a fast way to leaven bread in the nineteenth century, when baking soda was a shop good and many houses cooked on a griddle rather than in an oven. The round is cut into quarters, farls, and cooked on the griddle.

Cultural background

They sit beside potato farls at breakfast. The dough is a soft soda-bread dough, not a pancake batter.

Why it tastes this way

Buttermilk and soda react at once, so the bread rises without yeast. That chemistry is why soda bread spread in Ireland: it was fast, and it used the sour milk farms already had. A griddle browns the outside while the inside stays steaming and mild. There is little sugar. It tastes of wheat and tang.

How it is made

Flour, baking soda, salt, and buttermilk make a soft dough that is patted into a round, quartered, and cooked on a griddle.

Authentic touch. Handle the dough lightly and cook it a bit slower than you think. A fierce heat burns the flour and leaves a raw stripe in the middle.

How it is used

Butter, jam, or with a breakfast fry. They are best the morning they are cooked.

Field notes

  • Soda farls are bread cooked on a griddle, included here as the pancake’s thick northern neighbor.
  • Buttermilk is the acid that makes the soda work. Sweet milk will not do the same job.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups all-purpose flour
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup buttermilk

Steps

  1. Heat a griddle over medium-low.
  2. Stir flour, soda, and salt. Add buttermilk and mix to a soft dough.
  3. Pat into a round about 3/4 inch thick and cut into quarters.
  4. Cook on the griddle, turning once, until both sides are browned and the center is set, about 15 to 20 minutes.
  5. A low lid or a second pan helps the center cook.
  6. Cool slightly, then split and butter.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of sugar is a sweeter home version.
  • Oven soda bread is the same dough baked in a round loaf.

Pairings and uses

Ulster Oatcakes

Toasted oats, salt, and a dry crisp or a chewy round, depending on thickness.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 oatcakes

History

Oatcakes are a shared tradition of Ireland and Scotland. In Ulster they are a griddle bread of oats, fat, and hot water, eaten with butter, cheese, or a fry. Oats suited a cool, wet climate better than wheat for a long time. The Ulster cake sits between a Scottish oatcake and the other breads of an Ulster breakfast.

Cultural background

They can be thin and crisp or a little thicker. This version is a griddled round, not a sweet biscuit.

Why it tastes this way

Oats taste of the grain’s own oil, which is why a good oatcake smells nutty without any nut in it. Hot water softens the meal so it binds. The climate of the north, cool and damp, is why oats were the reliable crop and why the bread tastes of them instead of wheat.

How it is made

Oatmeal, a little flour, salt, fat, and hot water are rolled and cooked on a dry griddle.

Authentic touch. Roll them thin if you want a cracker, thicker if you want a bread. Either way, cook them slowly. Oats burn before they taste toasted if the griddle is roaring.

How it is used

Butter, cheese, or alongside soup. Honey is a later sweet use.

Field notes

  • A little wheat flour helps them roll. All-oat versions exist and are more fragile.
  • Ulster oatcakes and Scottish oatcakes are relatives cooked on the same kind of griddle.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups oat flour or finely ground oats
  • 1/4 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons oil or melted butter
  • 1/3 cup hot water, plus more if needed

Steps

  1. Mix the oats, flour, and salt. Stir in the fat and hot water to a dough.
  2. Roll thin and cut rounds.
  3. Cook on a dry medium-low griddle until the edges lift and both sides are faintly brown.
  4. Cool on a rack if you want them crisp.
  5. Serve with butter or cheese.
  6. Keep them dry. They soften in a tin that is not sealed.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Scottish oatcakes on their own page are the close cousin.
  • Fine oat flour makes a smoother cake. Pinhead oats need a longer soak and a coarser bite.

Pairings and uses

  • Bread course
  • Breakfast
  • Snack

Irish Buttermilk Drop Scones

Small, thick buttermilk cakes, lightly sweet.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 12 scones

History

Drop scones are thick little pancakes, dropped by the spoon onto a griddle. In Ireland they are a teatime and breakfast cake, distinct from the thin Shrove pancake. The method is the same family as Scotch pancakes. Buttermilk is the Irish liquid, because soda bread culture already kept sour milk in the kitchen.

Cultural background

They are eaten warm with butter and jam. They are not cut into farls and they are not tossed like crepes.

Why it tastes this way

A thicker batter and baking powder make a soft, cakey bite instead of a lacy crepe. Buttermilk adds tang that jam then sweetens. The contrast with Shrove pancakes is the point: one is a Lenten-eve crepe, the other is a soda-bread cousin cooked in rounds.

How it is made

A thick buttermilk batter is dropped by the spoon onto a warm griddle.

Authentic touch. Drop them thick and do not spread. They are ready to flip when the bubbles stay open.

How it is used

Butter and jam, still warm.

Field notes

  • Drop scones and Shrove pancakes are both Irish and are not the same thickness.
  • They belong to the soda and buttermilk kitchen.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/4 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1 large egg
  • 2/3 cup buttermilk
  • 1 tablespoon melted butter

Steps

  1. Stir the dry ingredients together, then stir in the wet ingredients until the batter is combined.
  2. The batter should be thick. Rest it 5 minutes.
  3. Heat a griddle or heavy skillet over medium heat and film it with butter.
  4. Spoon on the batter. Cook until the underside is browned and the top looks set.
  5. Flip once and cook the second side through.
  6. Drop by the tablespoon and flip when the bubbles hold.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Raisins in the batter are a common home addition.
  • Scotch pancakes are the Scottish twin.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Snack
  • butter
  • jam
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

English Shrove Tuesday Pancakes

Thin and eggy, with lemon and sugar.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 pancakes

History

English Shrove Tuesday pancakes are thin pancakes cooked to use up fats and eggs before Lent. The day is also called Pancake Day. A pancake race at Olney in Buckinghamshire is a real annual event. A local tradition dates it to 1445. That medieval date is a town story, not a record this guide can verify, so it is not stated here as fact. Lemon and sugar are the usual English finish. The cake is a crepe, not a Scotch pancake.

Cultural background

Schools and families still toss pancakes on the day. The toss is a game. The recipe does not require it.

Why it tastes this way

Eggs and milk were the perishable riches of a late winter kitchen, and the Church calendar said to finish them. The batter is thin so it fries quickly in a little butter and stays flexible for lemon and sugar. The sharpness of lemon is the English taste of the day, rather than a sweet filling.

How it is made

Flour, egg, and milk are cooked as thin pancakes in a small pan.

Authentic touch. Rest the batter so the gluten relaxes, or the pancake fights you when you swirl it. A nonstick or well-seasoned pan and a thin pour are the practical version of a race-day toss.

How it is used

Lemon and caster sugar. Golden syrup is a later pour some families prefer.

Field notes

  • The Olney pancake race is real. The 1445 origin date is a local tradition, not a verified record.
  • Lemon and sugar, not maple, are the classic English finish.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 2 large eggs
  • 1 1/4 cups milk
  • 1 tablespoon melted butter
  • Pinch of salt
  • Lemon and sugar, to serve

Steps

  1. Whisk into a thin batter and rest 20 minutes.
  2. Cook thin pancakes in a buttered skillet, flipping once.
  3. Serve with lemon and sugar.
  4. Stack them on a warm plate.
  5. A splash of milk loosens the batter if the second half thickens.
  6. Eat them the day they are made.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A savory cheese filling is a supper use, not the Shrove classic.
  • Scotch pancakes are thicker and are not this recipe.

Pairings and uses

  • Holiday table
  • Dessert
  • lemon
  • sugar
  • golden syrup
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Crumpets

Spongy, salty, and full of holes, made to be toasted and buttered.

1 hr 20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 crumpets

History

Crumpets are English griddle cakes cooked in rings so the batter stays tall and forms holes on top. They are related to earlier muffins and pikelets. The modern crumpet, with its yeast or leavener and its tea-table role, settled in the nineteenth century as a bakery and home cake. You toast them. You do not eat them as a floppy pancake straight from the first cook, though a cook may sneak one.

Cultural background

Butter is the topping that fills the holes. Cheese, or butter and jam, are the other honest uses. They are toast more than they are a stack.

Why it tastes this way

A wet, yeasted batter in a hot ring sets at the bottom while bubbles burst on top and leave holes. Those holes are the point, because melted butter runs into them. Baking soda at the end gives a last burst of bubbles. The taste is wheat and salt, not sugar. Sugar would darken the holes and turn them into a cake.

How it is made

A yeast batter is thinned, finished with baking soda, and cooked in greased rings on a griddle until the top is full of holes.

Authentic touch. The batter must be loose, almost pourable. If it is thick, you get a pikelet with no holes. Cook on medium-low so the bottom does not burn before the top sets.

How it is used

Toasted, with butter. They should be heated through so the butter melts.

Field notes

  • Holes are the test of a crumpet. A smooth top means the batter was too thick or the pan too cool.
  • They are meant to be toasted, not only eaten soft from the first griddle.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup warm milk
  • 1/2 cup warm water
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 2 tablespoons warm water, for the soda

Steps

  1. Stir flour, yeast, sugar, salt, milk, and 1/2 cup water into a thick batter. Cover 1 hour, until bubbly.
  2. Dissolve the soda in 2 tablespoons water and stir it in. The batter should be pourable.
  3. Grease crumpet rings and a medium-low griddle.
  4. Fill each ring halfway. Cook until the top is set and holey.
  5. Remove the rings, flip, and brown the top briefly.
  6. Toast before serving with butter.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Yorkshire pikelets are a close, often ringless relative.
  • Midlands pikelets are thicker drop scones and are a different page.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Snack
  • Bread course
  • butter
  • jam
  • cheese
  • Vegetarian

Yorkshire Pikelets

A free-form crumpet, spongy and ready for butter.

1 hr 20 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 5 · about 10 pikelets

History

In Yorkshire a pikelet is close to a crumpet cooked without a ring, so it spreads a little and still aims for holes. In the Midlands the same word often means a thicker drop scone. The two are different cakes that share a name. This page is the Yorkshire, crumpet-like pikelet.

Cultural background

They are toasted and buttered, sometimes with jam or a slice of cheese. They are tea food and breakfast food.

Why it tastes this way

Without a ring the batter spreads, so you get more browned edge and a slightly thinner sponge. The holes still come from a wet yeast batter and a late pinch of soda. The regional confusion in the name is a reminder that British griddle words moved with bakers, not with a single law.

How it is made

A crumpet-style batter is cooked in small free-form rounds on a griddle.

Authentic touch. Pour small rounds. If you pour a large one, the center stays wet after the edge burns.

How it is used

Split or toasted whole, with butter.

Field notes

  • Yorkshire pikelet and Midlands pikelet are not the same cake.
  • Holes still matter. A solid disk means the batter was stiff.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup warm milk
  • 1/2 cup warm water
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda

Steps

  1. Mix all but the soda into a batter and rest 1 hour.
  2. Stir in the soda. The batter should flow.
  3. Cook small rounds on a medium-low greased griddle until the tops set and bubble.
  4. Flip and brown lightly.
  5. Cool, then toast.
  6. Butter generously.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Midlands pikelets are the thicker, drop-scone meaning of the word.
  • Rings turn these into crumpets.

Pairings and uses

Midlands Pikelets

Thick, soft, lightly sweet griddle cakes.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 12 pikelets

History

Across much of the Midlands, a pikelet is a small thick pancake, close to a Scotch pancake or drop scone, not the holey Yorkshire kind. The shared name confuses visitors and is worth keeping straight. These are teatime cakes, buttered, sometimes with jam or syrup.

Cultural background

They are made from a thick egg batter and cooked in spoonfuls. They do not need yeast or rings.

Why it tastes this way

Egg and baking powder lift a soft crumb, and a spoon of sugar makes them tea cakes rather than bread. They taste milder and sweeter than a crumpet because they are not a salt sponge full of holes. The regional word is the cultural fact. The batter is the technical one.

How it is made

A thick sweetened batter is dropped onto a griddle and flipped once.

Authentic touch. Flip when the bubbles pop and stay open. They dry out if you cook them as long as a large pancake.

How it is used

Butter, jam, or a little golden syrup.

Field notes

  • Same word as the Yorkshire pikelet, different cake. The split is real.
  • They are the English drop-scone family.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1 large egg
  • 1/2 cup milk
  • 1 tablespoon melted butter

Steps

  1. Stir the dry ingredients together, then stir in the wet ingredients until the batter is combined.
  2. The batter should drop from the spoon and hold a shape.
  3. Heat a griddle or heavy skillet over medium heat and film it with butter.
  4. Spoon on the batter. Cook until the underside is browned and the top looks set.
  5. Flip once and cook the second side through.
  6. Cook small rounds and serve warm with butter.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Yorkshire pikelets are the holey ones.
  • Currants in the batter are a home variation.

Pairings and uses

Staffordshire Oatcakes

Soft, savory oat pancakes, meant to be filled and rolled.

1 hr 10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 large oatcakes

History

Staffordshire oatcakes are yeasted oat pancakes from the Potteries, especially Stoke-on-Trent. Oatcake shops still cook them on a griddle and sell them filled with bacon and cheese. They are a local daily bread, not a sweet Scottish biscuit, though both are called oatcakes. The yeast and the size, large and flexible, are the Staffordshire traits.

Cultural background

A typical shop filling is bacon, sausage, or cheese. The oatcake itself is the wrapper. This recipe is the pancake, with cheese as a meatless way to serve it.

Why it tastes this way

Oats, water, and a little wheat flour, fermented briefly, taste savory and slightly sour, which is why they want cheese or something salty rather than lemon sugar. The Potteries were an industrial district where a cheap, filling griddle bread sold from a shop window made sense. The yeast keeps them pliable enough to roll, unlike a crisp Highland oatcake.

How it is made

A yeast batter of oat flour, wheat flour, and water is rested, then cooked as large thin pancakes.

Authentic touch. Cook them thin and pale-gold, not dark. A flexible oatcake rolls. A crisp one cracks and becomes the other British oatcake.

How it is used

Warm, with cheese, or with bacon if you eat meat. Roll them while they are flexible.

Field notes

  • Staffordshire oatcakes are soft and usually savory. Scottish oatcakes are often a crisp bread.
  • Oatcake shops in Stoke-on-Trent are still the local way to buy them.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup oat flour
  • 1/2 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 1/2 cups warm water
  • Oil for the griddle

Steps

  1. Stir the flours, yeast, salt, and water into a thin batter. Rest 1 hour.

Yorkshire Pudding

Crisp, eggy, and hollow, meant to catch gravy.

15 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 puddings

History

Yorkshire pudding is the baked batter of an English roast dinner, known in print by the eighteenth century. It was a way to make a roast feed more people: batter cooked under the meat, or in the drippings, so it puffed and took up the fat. Modern kitchens often bake it in a separate tin with oil or drippings. It is a cousin of the popover and of the Dutch baby, and it is not a sweet pancake.

Cultural background

Sunday roast beef with pudding, potatoes, and greens is the plate most people mean. The pudding can be one large tray or individual cups. Toad in the hole bakes sausages in the same batter and is a variation, not the classic pudding.

Why it tastes this way

Hot fat and a wet batter make steam, and the steam puffs the pudding before the egg and flour set. Beef drippings taste meaty. This version uses oil so the pudding itself is vegetarian, with a note that drippings are the roast-dinner fat. The flavor is mostly browned batter and salt, which is why gravy is served with it. The original economy was stretching a joint of meat.

How it is made

A thin batter of egg, milk, and flour is poured into preheated oil and baked until puffed.

Authentic touch. The tin and the fat must be smoking hot. Cold fat makes a flabby disk. Do not open the oven while it rises.

How it is used

With roast beef and gravy, or as a first course with gravy, which is an older way of serving it. Onion gravy is a fair vegetarian stand-in and is a separate recipe.

Field notes

  • Yorkshire pudding is a batter pudding, included here as the roast-dinner cousin of oven pancakes.
  • Hot fat is not optional. It is the reason the pudding rises.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 2 large eggs
  • 1 cup milk
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 3 tablespoons neutral oil or beef drippings

Steps

  1. Whisk flour, eggs, milk, and salt into a smooth batter. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Heat the oven to 425°F. Divide the oil among a muffin tin and heat it until very hot.
  3. Pour in the batter, filling the cups about halfway.
  4. Bake about 20 to 25 minutes until puffed and brown.
  5. Do not open the door early.
  6. Serve at once with gravy.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Individual cups are the modern Sunday shape. A single tin is the older tray.
  • Toad in the hole adds sausages and is then not vegetarian.

Singing Hinnies

A rich griddle cake with currants, lard or butter, singing as it cooks.

History

Singing hinnies are a Northumberland griddle cake. The “singing” is the sound of the fat on a hot griddle, and “hinny” is a local term of affection. They are a rich dough of flour, fat, and currants, cooked in a round and cut, not a poured batter. Nineteenth-century northern kitchens kept them as a tea cake.

Cultural background

They are split and buttered. The currants are the fruit. They are closer to a griddled scone than to a crepe.

Why it tastes this way

A high proportion of fat is why they sing and why they taste short, like a scone. Currants were a traded dried fruit that kept, which is how a northern tea table got sweetness without fresh fruit. The griddle, rather than an oven, browns the fat in spots and leaves the inside tender.

How it is made

Flour, baking powder, fat, currants, and a little milk are patted into a round and cooked on a griddle, then cut.

Authentic touch. Do not add liquid beyond a spoon of milk if the dough is cracking. A wet dough will not sing. It will stew. Cook over medium so the currants do not burn black.

How it is used

Split, with butter, while warm.

Field notes

  • The name comes from the sound of fat on the griddle and from “hinny,” a term of affection.
  • They are a dough cake, included because they are cooked on the griddle like the other northern breads.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1/4 cup sugar
  • 1/4 cup cold butter
  • 1/3 cup dried currants
  • 1/3 cup milk

Steps

  1. Stir flour, baking powder, salt, and sugar. Rub in the butter.
  2. Toss in the currants and add milk to a soft dough.
  3. Pat into a round about 1/2 inch thick.
  4. Cook on a medium griddle, turning once, until both sides are brown and the center is set.
  5. Cool slightly and cut into wedges.
  6. Split and butter.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

English Muffins

A yeasted griddle bread with a toasted, nubby crumb.

1 hr 30 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 8 · 8 muffins

History

What Americans call an English muffin is a yeasted griddle bread, related to the muffins sold by muffin men in English streets in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Those muffins were cooked on a griddle, not baked in a tin like a modern American muffin. The fork-split, nooks-and-crannies style was industrialized later, especially in the United States, but the method is the griddle.

Cultural background

They are toasted and buttered, or used under eggs. This is a home version cooked in a covered skillet.

Why it tastes this way

Yeast gives a bread flavor that baking powder cannot, and a griddle crust on both sides is why the outside tastes toasted while the inside stays soft. Cornmeal on the board keeps them from sticking and adds a faint grit. They are a city bread that moved from street sellers to a breakfast industry.

How it is made

A soft yeast dough is shaped into rounds, dusted with cornmeal, and cooked slowly on a griddle.

Authentic touch. Cook them covered, on low heat, longer than a pancake. If the heat is high you get a raw ring inside a burnt crust. Split them with a fork, not a knife, if you want a rough surface for butter.

How it is used

Toasted, with butter, or as the base of eggs.

Field notes

  • English muffins are griddled, which is why they are in a pancake journal.
  • Splitting with a fork gives the rough crumb people toast.

Ingredients

  • 2 1/2 cups all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon instant yeast

  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup warm milk
  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • Cornmeal, for dusting

Steps

  2. Divide into 8 rounds, dust with cornmeal, and rest 20 minutes.
  3. Cook on a medium-low dry or lightly oiled griddle, covered, about 8 minutes a side.
  4. The sides should look set, not doughy.
  5. Cool before splitting.
  6. Toast to serve.

Scotch Pancakes

Small, thick, lightly sweet cakes with a soft middle.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 12 pancakes

History

Scotch pancakes, also called drop scones, are the thick small pancakes of Scotland. They are not the thin English Shrove pancake and not a crumpet. Tea tables and breakfasts both claim them. A girdle, the Scottish griddle, is the traditional pan.

Cultural background

They are eaten warm with butter and jam or syrup. They are a batter cake, leavened with baking powder, and they cook in minutes.

Why it tastes this way

A thick spoonful on a moderate girdle sets a tender crumb and a light crust, sweeter than an oatcake and less rich than a singing hinny. Scotland’s tea table took to baking powder in the nineteenth century because it was faster than yeast for a small cake. The flavor is flour, egg, and a little sugar, with browning from the iron.

How it is made

A thick sweetened batter is dropped by the spoon onto a warm griddle.

Authentic touch. Use a girdle or a heavy skillet, not a screaming hot pancake top. Drop scones burn on the outside while the middle stays doughy if the pan is too hot.

How it is used

Butter and jam, or butter and golden syrup.

Field notes

  • Scotch pancakes and English drop scones are the same family.
  • They are thick. Shrove pancakes are the thin ones.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1 large egg
  • 1/2 cup milk
  • 1 tablespoon melted butter

Steps

  1. Stir the dry ingredients together, then stir in the wet ingredients until the batter is combined.
  2. Rest 5 minutes. The batter should hold a soft peak on the spoon.

  4. Spoon on the batter. Cook until the underside is browned and the top looks set.
  5. Flip once and cook the second side through.
  6. Cook until both sides are golden and the center springs back.

History

How it is made

Oat flour, salt, a little fat, and hot water are rolled thin and cooked on a dry griddle.

How it is used

With cheese, butter, or soup. They are a staple bread.

Welsh Cakes

A currant girdle cake, crisp at the edge and soft like a scone.

History

Welsh cakes, picau ar y maen, are small rounds of a currant dough cooked on a bakestone or griddle. They are a tea cake, not a poured pancake, and they are one of the best-known Welsh griddle foods. The bakestone was a flat iron used on the hearth. Currants and a little spice are typical.

Cultural background

They are eaten plain or with butter, warm or cold, and they keep better than a crepe. Bake sales and family tins both rely on that.

Why it tastes this way

Fat rubbed into flour makes a short dough, and currants add a dark sweetness that traveled into Welsh cupboards as a dried fruit. The bakestone gives a spotted crust an oven does not. A pinch of spice, often mixed spice or nutmeg, is enough. Too much and they taste like a cookie rather than a cake from the griddle.

How it is made

A short dough with currants is rolled, cut into rounds, and cooked on a griddle.

Authentic touch. Cook them slower than pancakes. The dough is thick, and the currants burn if you rush. They are done when the sides look set and a central one, sacrificed to the cook, is not raw.

How it is used

Warm, with or without butter, with tea.

Field notes

  • Picau ar y maen means cakes on the stone, the bakestone.
  • They are a dough cooked on a griddle, which is why they sit with pancakes and are not pancakes.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1/4 teaspoon nutmeg
  • 1/4 cup sugar
  • 1/4 cup cold butter
  • 1/3 cup dried currants
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons milk

Steps

  1. Stir flour, baking powder, salt, nutmeg, and sugar. Rub in the butter.
  2. Add currants, egg, and milk to a soft dough.
  3. Roll 1/4 inch thick and cut rounds.
  4. Cook on a medium-low griddle, a few minutes a side, until browned and cooked through.
  5. Cool on a rack.
  6. Dust with a little sugar if you like.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of sugar on the cooked cake is optional.
  • Crempog is the batter pancake. Do not swap the names.

Pairings and uses

  • Snack
  • Dessert
  • butter
  • tea
  • Vegetarian
