Aebleskiver

Round Danish pancakes from a molded pan, cardamom, jam, and powdered sugar.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · about 28 aebleskiver

History

Aebleskiver are round pancakes cooked in a special pan with hemispherical cups. The name means apple slices. Older versions tuck in a bit of apple. Many Danish kitchens now cook them plain, with cardamom, and serve jam and powdered sugar. They are especially common in December, and they are also eaten at other times. Church breakfasts in the American Upper Midwest are a later home of the pan, not the origin of the cake. This recipe is the Danish one.

Cultural background

The pan is the recipe's tool, as with Dutch poffertjes, and the batters are not the same. Aebleskiver are turned with a knitting needle or a skewer so a ball forms. They are Advent and Christmas food in many families now.

Why it tastes this way

Whipped egg whites and baking powder make them light. Cardamom perfumes the crumb. The crust browns in butter while the inside stays like a small popover. Powdered sugar and a tart jam are the contrast. Apple, if you add it, steams inside and tastes like the name.

How it is made

A batter of flour, buttermilk, cardamom, and separated eggs is cooked in a buttered aebleskiver pan, turned once into rounds, and served with jam and powdered sugar.

Authentic touch. Turn each cake before the center is dry, so the unset batter runs into the cup and makes a ball. A flat pan cannot do this. A piece of apple in the cup is the older filling. Leaving it out is the common modern cake.

How it is used

Hot, with jam and powdered sugar, at breakfast, as a snack, or through December. The American church breakfast is a later table, not the source.

Field notes

  • The name means apple slices, and many current recipes leave the apple out.
  • Upper Midwest church breakfasts are a later home, not the origin.
  • The ball shape comes from turning the cake in a molded pan.

Ingredients

  • 180 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tsp baking powder
  • 1/2 tsp ground cardamom
  • 2 tbsp sugar
  • 1/4 tsp fine salt
  • 2 large eggs, separated
  • 250 ml buttermilk
  • 40 g unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the pan
  • Jam and powdered sugar, for serving

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, baking powder, cardamom, sugar, and salt. Stir in the yolks, buttermilk, and melted butter.
  2. Beat the whites to soft peaks and fold them in.
  3. Heat an aebleskiver pan, butter the cups, and fill them almost full. Cook until the bottoms are brown.
  4. Turn each cake with a skewer and cook until the ball is set and browned. Serve with jam and powdered sugar.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A cube of apple in each cup
  • A yeasted batter, which is an older style than baking powder

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Dessert
  • Feast or fast day
  • Snack
  • Jam
  • Powdered sugar
  • A small piece of apple hidden in the batter
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Äggakaka

A thick Scanian egg pancake, served with fried pork and lingonberries.

15 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 1 large cake, 4 wedges

History

Äggakaka is the thick egg pancake of Skåne, in southern Sweden. It is closer to a sturdy omelet than to a thin pannkaka. The plate is the cake plus fried pork and lingonberries. This recipe includes the pork because that is the Scanian plate, so the dish is not vegetarian. It is festival and home food in Skåne, not the Thursday pea-soup pancake of the pannkakor page.

Cultural background

Skåne cooking is often richer than a thin crepe tradition. The egg cake is cut in wedges. Lingonberries cut the fat of the pork and the eggs.

Why it tastes this way

Many eggs make it custardy and savory. A little flour keeps it from collapsing. Pork fat and browned edges add salt and crispness. Lingonberry is sour and sweet at once. Without the jam, the plate is heavier. The cake should be thick enough to cut.

How it is made

Eggs, milk, flour, and salt make a thick batter that cooks slowly in a pan of rendered pork, then is cut and served with lingonberries.

Authentic touch. Cook it slowly, covered, so the center sets without burning the bottom. Serve the pork and the lingonberries on the same plate. Do not roll it like a crepe.

How it is used

In wedges, with the fried pork and lingonberry jam, as a hearty meal.

Field notes

  • Äggakaka is Scanian. It is thick, and it is served with pork and lingonberries.
  • It is not the thin Swedish pannkaka.
  • The name means egg cake.

Ingredients

  • 200 g salt pork or thick bacon, sliced
  • 4 large eggs
  • 200 ml whole milk
  • 80 g plain wheat flour
  • 1/2 tsp fine salt
  • Lingonberry jam, for serving

Steps

  1. Fry the pork in a wide pan until browned. Leave the fat in the pan and set the pork aside.
  2. Whisk the eggs, milk, flour, and salt into a smooth thick batter.
  3. Pour the batter into the hot fat. Cook over medium-low heat, covered, until the center is just set. Turn in sections if you can, or brown the top under a hot grill.
  4. Return the pork to the plate, cut the cake in wedges, and serve with lingonberry jam.

The center should be set, not liquid egg. Salt pork is salty, so taste before you add more salt.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A meat-free egg cake, which is no longer the Scanian plate
  • Crisp bacon instead of salt pork, if that is what you have

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Feast or fast day
  • Lingonberry jam
  • The pork cooked in the pan
  • A glass of milk or beer
  • Nut-free

Åland pancake

A baked porridge pancake from Åland, cardamom-scented, with prune jam.

25 min prep · 45 min cook · Serves 8 · 1 baking dish, 8 squares

History

The Åland pancake is a baked pancake made from a cooked porridge, usually semolina or rice, with eggs, milk, sugar, and cardamom. This recipe uses semolina. A rice-porridge version is a real variation. It is the signature sweet of the Åland Islands, which are an autonomous part of Finland, so the country pin is Finland. Prune jam and whipped cream are the serving. It is not a pan-fried Finnish lettu.

Cultural background

It is dessert and celebration food, cut in squares from a baking dish. Cardamom is the spice of the batter. The texture is closer to a firm porridge pudding with a browned top than to a crepe.

Why it tastes this way

Semolina makes it creamy and slightly resilient. Cardamom is floral and piney. Sugar and milk round it. The oven browns the top so there is a crust over a soft interior. Prune jam is dark and tart against the mild cake. Cream carries the cardamom.

How it is made

Semolina is cooked in milk with cardamom into a porridge, enriched with eggs and sugar, baked until set and brown, and served with prune jam.

Authentic touch. Cook the porridge fully before the eggs go in, and cool it a little so the eggs do not scramble. Serve prune jam. Cardamom belongs in the batter.

How it is used

Warm or cold, in squares, with prune jam and whipped cream, as a dessert.

Field notes

  • Åland pancake is baked from porridge. It is not a fried lettu.
  • Semolina and rice versions both exist. This page uses semolina.
  • Prune jam and whipped cream are the usual plate.

Ingredients

  • 1 liter whole milk
  • 100 g semolina
  • 1 tsp ground cardamom
  • 1/2 tsp fine salt
  • 3 tbsp sugar
  • 3 large eggs
  • 20 g unsalted butter, for the dish
  • Prune jam and whipped cream, for serving

Steps

  1. Heat the milk with the cardamom and salt. Rain in the semolina, stirring, and cook until you have a thick porridge. Stir in the sugar. Cool for 10 minutes.
  2. Beat the eggs and stir them into the porridge.
  3. Spread the mixture in a buttered baking dish. Bake at 200°C until set and browned on top, about 40 minutes.
  4. Cut in squares and serve with prune jam and whipped cream.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Rice porridge instead of semolina, which changes the grain
  • Finnish lettu, the thin pan pancake, which is a different page

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Feast or fast day
  • Prune jam
  • Whipped cream
  • A little extra ground cardamom
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Danish pandekager

Thin Danish pancakes, rolled with sugar or jam.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 pancakes

History

Danish pandekager are thin wheat pancakes, rolled with sugar, jam, or ice cream. They are everyday food and also appear around Fastelavn, the Danish carnival before Lent. Fastelavn is not only a pancake day. The cream-filled fastelavnsboller are the buns of that festival. This page does not treat the pancake as the whole of Fastelavn. Aebleskiver, the molded round cakes, are a different page.

Cultural background

A Danish home pancake is closer to a crepe than to a thick American stack. Sugar on the hot cake, then a roll, is the simplest serving. Jam is just as common.

Why it tastes this way

Egg and milk make a soft, lightly sweet sheet. Browning in butter adds the toasted note. Sugar crystals melt on contact. A tart jam keeps the roll from being only sweet. They are thin, so the surface area is the flavor.

How it is made

A thin batter of flour, egg, milk, sugar, and salt is cooked in butter and rolled with sugar or jam.

Authentic touch. Roll them while hot so the sugar sticks. A little sugar in the batter is enough. Do not confuse these with aebleskiver, which need a molded pan.

How it is used

Rolled, as dessert, breakfast, or a Fastelavn table among other foods.

Field notes

  • Pandekager are thin and rolled. Aebleskiver are the round molded cakes.
  • Fastelavn includes these pancakes and, famously, the filled buns. The buns are not this recipe.
  • Sugar or jam is the usual filling.

Ingredients

  • 150 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tbsp sugar, plus more for serving
  • 1 pinch fine salt
  • 2 large eggs
  • 350 ml whole milk
  • 30 g unsalted butter, for the pan
  • Jam, for serving

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, sugar, salt, eggs, and milk into a thin batter. Rest 10 minutes.
  2. Cook thin pancakes in a buttered pan until spotted on both sides.
  3. Sprinkle with sugar or spread with jam.
  4. Roll and serve warm.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A squeeze of lemon
  • Aebleskiver, the molded cake on its own page

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Breakfast
  • Feast or fast day
  • Sugar
  • Jam
  • Vanilla ice cream, for a dessert plate
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Finnish lettu

Thin Finnish pancakes, served with jam, sugar, or whipped cream.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 10 pancakes

History

Lettu, plural letut, is the thin Finnish pancake. Ohukainen is a related name, often for a smaller cake. This page is the thin lettu cooked in a pan. It is everyday home food, served with jam, sugar, or whipped cream. The Åland oven pancake, made from porridge, is a different page. This batter is flour, egg, and milk, not a baked porridge.

Cultural background

Letut are a dessert and a weekend treat more than a ceremonial calendar food. A thin flexible cake is the goal. Families argue about jam versus sugar. Both are honest.

Why it tastes this way

They taste of egg, milk, and browned butter, with almost no spice. A thin sheet means more browned surface. Jam adds acid. Whipped cream adds richness the batter only hints at. If the batter is thick, you lose the lettu and gain a different pancake.

How it is made

A thin batter is rested and cooked like crepes, then served with jam or sugar.

Authentic touch. Cook them thin enough to roll. Ohukaiset, smaller and sometimes a little thicker, are the cousin. Do not bake this batter as an Åland pancake.

How it is used

Rolled or folded, with jam, sugar, or whipped cream, as dessert or a weekend breakfast.

Field notes

  • Lettu is the thin Finnish pancake. Ohukainen is a close name for a smaller cake.
  • The Åland pancake is a baked porridge cake and a different page.
  • Jam, sugar, or whipped cream are the usual finishes.

Ingredients

  • 150 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tbsp sugar
  • 1/4 tsp fine salt
  • 2 large eggs
  • 400 ml whole milk
  • 30 g unsalted butter, for the pan
  • Jam, for serving

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, sugar, salt, eggs, and milk into a thin batter. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Cook thin pancakes in a buttered pan until both sides are spotted.
  3. Spread with jam or sprinkle with sugar.
  4. Roll or fold and serve, with whipped cream if you want it.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Ohukaiset, cooked smaller
  • A savory filling of cheese, less typical than jam

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Breakfast
  • Jam
  • Whipped cream
  • Sugar
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Icelandic lummur

Small thick Icelandic pancakes with rye, buttered and sugared.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 small pancakes

History

Lummur are small thick Icelandic pancakes, often made with some rye flour and served with butter and sugar. They are a different cake from the paper-thin pönnukökur. Rye and wheat together mean this recipe is not gluten-free. They are a home snack and breakfast, cooked in spoonfuls on a griddle.

Cultural background

Rye has a long place in Icelandic baking because wheat was not always the easy grain. A lumma is hearty for its size. Brown sugar or white sugar on butter is the serving.

Why it tastes this way

Rye tastes deeper and slightly sour next to white flour. Baking powder lifts the crumb so it is not a cracker. Butter on the hot cake melts into the holes. Sugar is the contrast. They should be thick enough that the center stays soft.

How it is made

Wheat flour, rye flour, baking powder, egg, and milk make a thick batter that is cooked in small rounds and buttered.

Authentic touch. Keep rye as part of the flour, not the whole of it, for this page. Cook them as small thick rounds. Butter and sugar are the topping, not a rolled jam filling.

How it is used

Warm, with butter and sugar, as a snack or breakfast.

Field notes

  • Lummur are small and thick. Pönnukökur are thin.
  • Rye in the batter is part of this version.
  • They are buttered and sugared, not usually rolled around a filling.

Ingredients

  • 100 g plain wheat flour
  • 50 g rye flour
  • 1 tsp baking powder
  • 1 tbsp sugar
  • 1/4 tsp fine salt
  • 1 large egg
  • 250 ml whole milk
  • 30 g unsalted butter, for the pan and serving

Steps

  1. Whisk both flours, the baking powder, sugar, and salt. Beat in the egg and milk until you have a thick batter.
  2. Cook small rounds in butter over medium heat until bubbles set, then turn.
  3. The center should spring back and not taste of raw rye.
  4. Butter them and sprinkle with sugar.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • All wheat flour, which loses the rye note and is still not gluten-free
  • Pönnukökur, the thin rolled pancakes

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Snack
  • Butter
  • Sugar
  • A spoon of jam if sugar is not enough
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Icelandic pönnukökur

Very thin Icelandic pancakes, rolled with sugar or rhubarb jam.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 10 thin pancakes

History

Pönnukökur are Iceland's very thin pancakes, rolled with sugar, jam, or whipped cream. Rhubarb jam is a common home filling because rhubarb grows well in Iceland. They are everyday and party food. This page does not assign them to Bolludagur, which is the day for cream buns, or to Sprengidagur, which is the day for salted meat and peas. Lummur, the thicker small cakes, are the next page.

Cultural background

A wide pan and a thin batter are the method. The cakes are stacked and rolled just before serving so they stay soft. They belong at birthdays and at ordinary tables.

Why it tastes this way

They are thin enough that butter and sugar dominate. A little vanilla or cardamom in some homes perfumes them. This recipe keeps a pinch of sugar and a neutral batter so the jam shows. Rhubarb is sharp. The edge should be spotted, not stiff.

How it is made

A thin milk and egg batter is cooked like crepes and rolled with sugar or jam.

Authentic touch. Roll them thin enough to see the pan color through the batter as it sets. Sugar alone is an honest filling. Rhubarb jam is the other one this page recommends.

How it is used

Rolled, as a snack or dessert, with coffee. They are not a thick breakfast stack.

Field notes

  • Pönnukökur are very thin and usually rolled.
  • Rhubarb jam is a common Icelandic filling.
  • They are not the same cake as lummur, and they are not the cream buns of Bolludagur.

Ingredients

  • 150 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tbsp sugar, plus more for serving
  • 1 pinch fine salt
  • 2 large eggs
  • 400 ml whole milk
  • 30 g unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the pan
  • Rhubarb jam or another jam, for serving

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, sugar, salt, eggs, milk, and melted butter into a thin batter. Rest 10 minutes.
  2. Cook very thin pancakes in a buttered pan, turning once.
  3. Sprinkle with sugar or spread with jam.
  4. Roll and serve.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A pinch of cardamom in the batter
  • Lummur, the thicker rye-tinged cakes on the next page

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Snack
  • Sugar
  • Rhubarb jam
  • Whipped cream
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Kolbullar

A simple pork and flour pancake from the charcoal woods of central Sweden.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 pancakes

History

Kolbullar are the portable pancakes of charcoal makers and forest workers in central Sweden, including Dalarna and the old mining districts. Kol refers to charcoal. Pork, flour, water, and salt were cooked on a flat iron by the charcoal stack. This is a skillet version of that food. It is a meat dish. It is not a sweet pancake and not raggmunk.

Cultural background

The cake was working food: cheap grain, salt pork, and a fire that was already burning. At home it is still a plain, salty pancake. The romance of the woods is optional. The pork and the flour are not.

Why it tastes this way

Pork fat is the fat and the salt. The flour batter fries crisp at the rim and stays chewy. There is no egg and no sugar, so it tastes blunt and savory. Pepper helps. If you expect a dessert pancake, this will seem austere, which is accurate.

How it is made

Salt pork is diced and rendered. A batter of flour, water, and salt is poured into the fat, with the pork left in, and fried until set.

Authentic touch. Render the pork in the pan and fry the batter in that fat. The home pan stands in for the iron sheet by the charcoal stack. Do not add sugar.

How it is used

Hot, as a savory snack or a simple meal. It is not dessert.

Field notes

  • The name is tied to charcoal makers, kolare, who cooked them in the woods.
  • The batter is flour, water, salt, and pork. There is no sugar in the traditional cake.
  • This skillet version stands in for a flat iron over a charcoal fire.

Ingredients

  • 200 g salt pork, diced small
  • 150 g plain wheat flour
  • 300 ml cold water
  • 1/2 tsp fine salt
  • 1/4 tsp black pepper

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, water, salt, and pepper into a smooth batter.
  2. Fry the salt pork in a pan until the fat renders and the pieces brown.
  3. Pour in enough batter to make a thick pancake around the pork. Cook until the bottom is brown and the top is set.
  4. Turn carefully and finish the other side. Repeat with the remaining batter. Serve hot.

Salt pork is already salty. Taste the batter before you add the full amount of salt.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Bacon instead of salt pork
  • An egg in the batter, which changes the texture and adds an egg allergen

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Snack
  • Lingonberry jam, a later table addition
  • Black coffee
  • A crisp pickle
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Norwegian lefse

Potato lefse, rolled thin and griddled, soft enough to fold with butter and sugar.

1 hr 30 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 8 · 8 large sheets

History

This page is potato lefse, the soft Norwegian flatbread of boiled potato, butter, and flour, rolled thin and cooked on a dry griddle. There are other lefse, some without potato. Lompe is a smaller potato cousin often wrapped around a hot dog. Norwegian-American tables, especially in the Upper Midwest, are a later home of potato lefse, not the origin. The flour means this recipe is not gluten-free.

Cultural background

Lefse is holiday food and family food. Rolling it thin is a skill, often done with a grooved rolling pin and a cloth-covered board. The cakes are stacked under a cloth so they stay flexible.

Why it tastes this way

Potato keeps the sheet soft and mild. Butter in the dough tastes warm when the griddle spots it. Flour is there for strength, and too much of it makes the lefse tough and pale. Sugar and more butter at the table are the usual sweet serving. The cake itself is only lightly salted.

How it is made

Potatoes are boiled, riced, and mixed with butter, salt, and flour. The dough is chilled, rolled very thin, and griddled until spotted.

Authentic touch. Chill the potato dough before you roll it. Cook on a dry, hot griddle until brown spots appear, and stack the finished sheets in a towel. A cloth under the dough helps you turn the thin round.

How it is used

Cooled slightly, spread with butter and sugar, folded, as a snack or a holiday table bread. Savory uses exist. This page writes the butter-and-sugar fold.

Field notes

  • This is potato lefse, rolled thin and griddled.
  • Tables in the American Upper Midwest are a later home, not the origin.
  • Lompe is the smaller potato wrap. It is related and not the same sheet.

Ingredients

  • 700 g starchy potatoes, peeled and cut into chunks
  • 40 g unsalted butter
  • 1 tsp fine salt
  • 200 g plain wheat flour, plus more for rolling
  • Butter and sugar, for serving

Steps

  1. Boil the potatoes until tender. Drain well and rice or mash them dry. Stir in the butter and salt. Cool completely. Chilling the mash for an hour makes rolling easier.
  2. Work in the flour until you have a soft dough that does not stick badly. Do not add more flour than the dough needs.
  3. Roll a ball very thin on a floured cloth. Cook on a hot dry griddle until brown spots appear, then turn and spot the other side.
  4. Stack in a clean towel. Serve with butter and sugar, folded.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Lefse without potato, a different bread
  • Lompe, smaller and thicker, used as a wrap

Pairings and uses

  • Snack
  • Accompaniment to a meal
  • Feast or fast day
  • Butter and sugar
  • Cinnamon sugar
  • A hot dog inside lompe, which is the smaller cousin and not this sheet
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Sweden · Northern Europe

Raggmunk

Crisp Swedish potato pancakes from raw grated potato, with lingonberries.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 pancakes

History

Raggmunk is a Swedish potato pancake made with raw grated potato in a batter of flour, egg, and milk. It fries crisp at the edge. The usual plate adds fried pork and lingonberries. This page keeps the pancake vegetarian and names the pork as the traditional accompaniment, not as an ingredient. It is not äggakaka, which is an egg cake, and it is not lefse.

Cultural background

Potato pancakes of this kind are weekday food in Sweden. The raw potato has to be grated just before cooking or it darkens. A crisp raggmunk and a soft one are both defended. This recipe aims for a lacy, crisp edge.

Why it tastes this way

Potato starch browns and crisps. Milk and egg make the middle tender. Salt and white pepper season it. Lingonberry jam is the sour-sweet contrast, the same idea as applesauce beside a German potato pancake, and not the same plate. The wheat flour binds and means this is not gluten-free.

How it is made

Raw potato is grated into a batter of flour, egg, and milk, then fried thin in butter until the edges are brown.

Authentic touch. Grate the potato on a fine side and do not wash all the starch away. Spread the batter thin if you want the crisp version. Serve the jam on the side, not cooked into the batter.

How it is used

Hot, with lingonberry jam. Fried pork is the traditional meat side and is not in this batter.

Field notes

  • Raggmunk uses raw grated potato in a batter, then fries crisp.
  • Lingonberries, and often pork, are the plate. This recipe leaves the pork as a choice.
  • It is not the Scanian äggakaka and not Norwegian lefse.

Ingredients

  • 500 g raw potatoes, peeled
  • 1 large egg
  • 100 ml whole milk
  • 60 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tsp fine salt
  • 1/4 tsp white pepper
  • 40 g unsalted butter, for frying
  • Lingonberry jam, for serving

Steps

  1. Grate the potatoes. Stir in the egg, milk, flour, salt, and pepper at once so the potato does not sit and darken.
  2. Heat butter in a frying pan over medium-high heat.
  3. Spread thin rounds and fry until the edges are brown and crisp. Turn and cook through.
  4. Serve hot with lingonberry jam.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A thicker, softer cake
  • Onion grated into the batter

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Lingonberry jam

Norway · Northern Europe

Svele

Thick west-Norwegian pancakes, the ferry kiosk cake, with jam or brown whey cheese.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 thick pancakes

History

Svele are thick pancakes from western Norway, especially Vestland. They are the cakes sold at ferry kiosks, cooked to order and eaten while you cross a fjord. A typical serve is butter and jam, or a slice of brown whey cheese. They are thicker than a crepe and raised with baking powder in this home recipe. They are not lefse, which is a potato flatbread.

Cultural background

The ferry association is practical. The boat ride is long enough for a hot cake, and the kiosk already has a griddle. At home they are a snack or a simple dessert.

Why it tastes this way

Buttermilk or cultured milk makes them tangy. Cardamom is the spice, used lightly. A thick batter stays soft inside and brown outside. Sugar in the batter is moderate. Jam or the salty-sweet brown whey cheese does the rest. They should not be thin and lacy.

How it is made

Flour, baking powder, cardamom, egg, sugar, and buttermilk make a thick batter that is cooked in butter as soft round cakes.

Authentic touch. Cook them thicker than a crepe, one generous ladle each. Serve with butter and jam, or with brown whey cheese if you can get it. Cardamom belongs in the batter, not only on top.

How it is used

As a snack, especially the ferry version, with butter and jam or brown whey cheese.

Field notes

  • Svele are the thick pancakes associated with west-Norwegian ferry kiosks.
  • Brown whey cheese or jam and butter are the usual toppings.
  • They are not potato lefse.

Ingredients

  • 250 g plain wheat flour
  • 2 tsp baking powder
  • 1/2 tsp ground cardamom
  • 2 tbsp sugar
  • 1/2 tsp fine salt
  • 2 large eggs
  • 400 ml buttermilk
  • 40 g unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the pan

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, baking powder, cardamom, sugar, and salt. Beat in the eggs, buttermilk, and melted butter until just smooth.
  2. Cook a generous ladleful in a buttered pan over medium heat. The cake should be thick.
  3. Turn when the top is set and the underside is brown. Cook until the center is done.
  4. Serve warm with butter and jam, or with brown whey cheese.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of sour cream in place of some buttermilk
  • No cardamom, which is plainer and less typical of this page

Pairings and uses

  • Snack
  • Street food
  • Jam
  • Butter
  • Brown whey cheese
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Swedish pannkakor

Thin Swedish pancakes, pan-cooked, served with lingonberry jam.

15 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 10 pancakes

History

Swedish pannkakor are thin pancakes, cooked in a pan or, in another method, baked in the oven. This page is the pan cake. Thursday yellow pea soup followed by pancakes is a living Swedish habit. A common explanation ties that Thursday plate to a Friday fast. Treat the explanation as common, not settled. The thin pancake also traveled with Swedish immigrants to the American Upper Midwest, where it has a separate journal page. This recipe stays in Sweden. Whipped cream and lingonberry jam are the usual finish.

Cultural background

Pannkakor are home food and school-lunch food. They are dessert after soup more often than they are a tall breakfast stack. Plättar, the tiny pan cakes, are the next page.

Why it tastes this way

A thin egg batter browns in butter and tastes of milk and a little salt. Lingonberry jam is tart, which keeps the butter from feeling heavy. Sugar in the batter is modest. The cake should bend. A thick, dry pancake is a different recipe.

How it is made

Flour, egg, milk, salt, and a little sugar are whisked thin and cooked as crepes in butter.

Authentic touch. Serve lingonberry jam, or another tart jam if you cannot find lingonberries. Do not turn this into the Upper Midwest page. If you bake the batter in a dish, you have started the oven variation, not the pan cake written here.

How it is used

Rolled or folded, with jam and sometimes whipped cream, often after pea soup on Thursdays.

Field notes

  • Thursday pea soup and pancakes are a Swedish habit. The fast-day explanation is not settled.
  • The Upper Midwest Swedish pancake is a different page. This one stays in Sweden.
  • Plättar are the tiny ones, cooked in a special pan.

Ingredients

  • 150 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tbsp sugar
  • 1/2 tsp fine salt
  • 3 large eggs
  • 500 ml whole milk
  • 30 g unsalted butter, for the pan
  • Lingonberry jam, for serving

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, sugar, and salt. Beat in the eggs and milk until the batter is thin and smooth. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Heat a pan and grease it with butter.
  3. Cook thin pancakes until spotted brown on both sides.
  4. Serve with lingonberry jam, rolled or folded.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Ugnspannkaka, the oven pancake, often with pork, which is a different method and a meat dish
  • A savory fold of cheese

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Feast or fast day
  • Lingonberry jam
  • Whipped cream
  • Yellow pea soup, if this is the Thursday plate
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Swedish plättar

Tiny Swedish pancakes from a plätt pan, with jam and cream.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 35 small pancakes

History

Plättar are small Swedish pancakes cooked in a plättjärn, a pan with several round dips, often seven. The batter is the thin pannkaka batter, and the pan is what changes the cake. They are a home breakfast and a dessert, served with jam and sometimes cream. They are not poffertjes, which are yeasted and Dutch, and they are not the large pannkakor on the previous page.

Cultural background

A plätt pan is a normal tool in a Swedish kitchen. Without one, you can cook tiny pancakes in an ordinary pan, and you should say that is a substitute. The shape and the browned rim come from the mold.

Why it tastes this way

The small round browns at the edge and stays tender in the middle. Butter in the cups adds flavor fast because the cakes are thin. Jam is tart against a lightly sweet batter. They taste the same family as pannkakor, with more crust per bite.

How it is made

A thin egg batter is spooned into a buttered plätt pan, turned once, and served with jam.

Authentic touch. Grease each cup, not only the first one. Fill the dips shallowly. Turn the plättar with a thin skewer when the top has set.

How it is used

In a pile, with jam, at breakfast or as dessert. Cream is optional.

Field notes

  • A plätt pan often has seven dips.
  • The batter is close to pannkakor. The mold is the difference.
  • They are not Dutch poffertjes, which use yeast and buckwheat.

Ingredients

  • 120 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tbsp sugar
  • 1/4 tsp fine salt
  • 2 large eggs
  • 350 ml whole milk
  • 30 g unsalted butter, for the pan
  • Jam, for serving

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, sugar, salt, eggs, and milk into a thin batter. Rest 10 minutes.
  2. Heat a plätt pan and butter each cup.
  3. Spoon in a shallow layer. Cook until the surface sets, turn, and brown the other side.
  4. Serve in a pile with jam.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A savory plätt with cheese, less usual than jam
  • The same batter as larger pannkakor when you have no plätt pan

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Dessert
  • Lingonberry or strawberry jam
  • Whipped cream
  • Sugar
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free
