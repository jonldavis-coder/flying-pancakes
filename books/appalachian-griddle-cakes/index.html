Appalachian Griddle Cakes

Buckwheat, sorghum, cornmeal, and dried apple from the upland South.

These are the mountain cakes that can be tied to a real griddle: buckwheat, sorghum flapjacks, cornmeal, dried apple, and the plain buttermilk cake.

Scots-Irish and English settlers brought oat and griddle habits into the mountains. German-speaking settlers brought a taste for buckwheat and for sweet-sour keeping. Enslaved people and later Black Appalachian cooks worked the same farms and kitchens. Their labor is part of this food.

Cherokee and other Native nations of these mountains grew corn long before those settlements. This book does not assign the settler cakes to them, and it does not invent a Cherokee pancake to fill a gap. Spoonbread is here as the baked cornmeal cousin, labeled as baked.

Appalachian Buckwheat Cakes

Earthly buckwheat, a little sour, with a sturdy crumb.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 10 small cakes

History

Buckwheat cakes are an old mountain breakfast. Buckwheat grows on thin, cool soil where wheat struggles, which suited upland farms from Pennsylvania down the Appalachian chain. Settlers from German-speaking Pennsylvania and from the British Isles brought griddle habits. The cakes were often started with a bit of leftover batter that soured overnight.

Cultural background

They are winter food: dark, filling, and meant for molasses or sorghum. A lighter wheat pancake replaced them in many houses once white flour was cheap, but buckwheat never left the mountains entirely.

Why it tastes this way

Buckwheat is not a wheat. Its flavor is grassy and mineral, and cool mountain fields are why it was planted. An overnight rest gives a light sourness, the taste of a batter kept in a crock, not of a boxed mix. Molasses and sorghum match it because those were the local sugars. A little wheat flour in this home version keeps the cake from crumbling. Pure buckwheat is a different, more fragile cake.

How it is made

Buckwheat and a portion of wheat flour are mixed with buttermilk, egg, and leavener, rested, and cooked on a hot griddle.

Authentic touch. Cook them smaller than a diner pancake. Buckwheat batter sets slowly, and a huge cake stays raw in the middle.

How it is used

Butter and sorghum or molasses. Sausage is the classic side and makes the plate no longer vegetarian.

Field notes

  • Buckwheat thrived in cool uplands, which is the agricultural reason these cakes are mountain food.
  • This version uses some wheat so the cakes hold. The label is not gluten-free.

Ingredients

  • 3/4 cup buckwheat flour
  • 1/4 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon molasses
  • 1 large egg
  • 1 1/4 cups buttermilk
  • 2 tablespoons melted butter

Steps

  1. Stir the dry ingredients together, then stir in the wet ingredients until the batter is combined.
  2. Rest the batter 10 minutes. It will look thicker than a white-flour batter.
  3. Heat a griddle or heavy skillet over medium heat and film it with butter or bacon fat.
  4. Spoon on the batter. Cook until the underside is browned and the top looks set.
  5. Flip once and cook the second side through.
  6. Cook small cakes. The crumb should lose its shine before you serve them.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • For a gluten-free cake, use all buckwheat flour and accept a more fragile crumb.
  • A spoon of the batter saved overnight in the refrigerator is the old starter. Use it in the next batch if you like it sourer.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • sorghum
  • molasses
  • butter
  • sausage
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Sorghum Flapjacks

Mild wheat cake with the mineral sweetness of sorghum in the batter.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 pancakes

History

In American English a flapjack is a pancake. Sorghum, pressed from sweet sorghum cane and boiled to syrup, was a common Appalachian and upper-South sweetener in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, made on farms that could not rely on maple or imported sugar. Stirring a spoon of that syrup into the batter, and serving more at the table, is the local form of a wheat pancake.

Cultural background

The British flapjack, an oat traybake, is a different food and is not this recipe. Here the word means the griddle cake, which is how mountain cooks have long used it.

Why it tastes this way

Sorghum syrup tastes less purely sweet than maple and a little like the cane it was boiled from: mineral, faintly bitter, and dark. That flavor is the taste of a crop that ripens in a warm season and is processed in the fall. The cake itself stays plain so the syrup can be tasted twice, once inside and once on top.

How it is made

A buttermilk batter is sweetened with sorghum syrup and cooked as ordinary flapjacks, then served with more sorghum.

Authentic touch. Warm the sorghum so it blends into the milk. Cold syrup sits in a lump and burns in spots on the griddle.

How it is used

Butter and warm sorghum. They are a breakfast stack.

Field notes

  • American flapjacks are pancakes. British flapjacks are an oat bar and are not in this recipe.
  • Sorghum mills and stirring-off parties were a real part of upland farm life.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup buttermilk
  • 2 tablespoons sorghum syrup, plus more to serve
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons melted butter

Steps

  1. Stir the dry ingredients together, then stir in the wet ingredients until the batter is combined.
  2. Stir the sorghum into the buttermilk before it meets the flour, so the syrup dissolves.
  3. Heat a griddle or heavy skillet over medium heat and film it with butter.
  4. Spoon on the batter. Cook until the underside is browned and the top looks set.
  5. Flip once and cook the second side through.
  6. Serve with more warm sorghum. Do not cook the syrup on the bare griddle or it will scorch.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Molasses is darker and more bitter. It is a cousin, not the same pour.
  • Maple is a Northern swap and changes the story of the plate.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • sorghum syrup
  • butter
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Appalachian Cornmeal Cakes

Toasted meal, tangy milk, and a sturdy round.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 cakes

History

Cornmeal cakes were ordinary bread in the southern mountains, where corn was the grain people grew and milled. Scots-Irish and English settlers brought griddle and oat habits. Cherokee and other Native nations of these mountains had grown corn long before those settlements. This cake is later mountain home cooking. It is not presented here as a Cherokee recipe.

Cultural background

A mix of meal, buttermilk, and egg cooked in a skillet was breakfast or the bread for soup beans. It is humbler than spoonbread and closer to a hoe cake.

Why it tastes this way

Mountain farms stored corn as meal because the grain dried and kept. Buttermilk from a family cow softened it. The taste is toasted meal and acid, not vanilla. Cooks who had no wheat still had a bread. That economy is the flavor.

How it is made

Cornmeal is soaked briefly with buttermilk, bound with an egg, and cooked as thick rounds.

Authentic touch. Use a well-seasoned skillet and a moderate fire. A blazing griddle burns the meal before the center sets.

How it is used

Butter, sorghum, or beside soup beans. Both uses are honest.

Field notes

  • This book does not assign the cake to the Cherokee. Corn in these mountains is older than this recipe.
  • Soup beans and cornbread are a documented mountain plate. The cake can play the bread’s part.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup plain cornmeal
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1 cup buttermilk
  • 1 large egg
  • 1 tablespoon melted butter or oil

Steps

  1. Stir meal, salt, and soda. Add buttermilk, egg, and fat.
  2. Rest 10 minutes.
  3. Cook on a greased medium griddle as thick rounds.
  4. Flip when the underside is brown and the top is set.
  5. Cook through.
  6. Serve with beans or sorghum.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of wheat flour softens them and means they are no longer gluten-free.
  • Water instead of buttermilk is the leaner, older cake.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Bread course
  • sorghum
  • soup beans
  • butter
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Dried Apple Pancakes

Soft wheat cake with tart, plumped dried apple.

25 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 pancakes

History

Drying apple slices was a standard way to keep the orchard through an Appalachian winter. Dried-apple stack cakes, a layered cake, are a famous mountain dessert and are not this recipe. These pancakes use the same pantry fruit in a breakfast batter: apples soaked until soft, then folded in. The dried fruit, not a fresh supermarket apple, is the mountain detail.

Cultural background

Winter breakfast, when the root cellar and the dried-fruit strings were what remained of the orchard. Fresh apple pancakes are a different, juicier cake.

Why it tastes this way

Drying concentrates acid and perfume and removes water, so the fruit tastes tarter than a fresh slice and does not flood the batter. Soaking brings back enough moisture to make it tender. The method is about climate: a short growing season and a long winter made dried fruit the reliable sweetness.

How it is made

Dried apples are soaked, chopped, and folded into a buttermilk batter.

Authentic touch. Soak the apples until they are supple, then chop them. Hard chips stay dry in the crumb and scorch.

How it is used

Butter. They are already fruity, so syrup can be light.

Field notes

  • Dried-apple stack cake is a layered special-occasion cake. These are breakfast pancakes that use the same fruit.
  • Soaking is not optional if the apples are leathery.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup dried apple slices
  • 1 cup hot water, for soaking
  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup buttermilk
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons melted butter

Steps

  1. Soak the apples in hot water 15 minutes. Drain, chop, and pat dry.
  2. Mix the batter and fold in the apples.
  3. Rest 5 minutes.
  4. Cook on a buttered medium griddle.
  5. Flip once. Pieces of apple brown faster than the batter.
  6. Serve warm.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A pinch of cinnamon is common. Some mountain cooks use none.
  • Fresh apples are a fair summer swap and a different texture.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Snack
  • butter
  • sorghum
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Mountain Buttermilk Pancakes

Plain, tangy, and a little less sweet than a diner stack.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 pancakes

History

Once roller mills and baking powder reached mountain stores, wheat pancakes joined corn cakes at breakfast. Buttermilk from the family cow was the liquid. The cake is deliberately plain. Sweetness arrived at the table as sorghum or honey, not as a cup of sugar in the bowl.

Cultural background

This is the everyday wheat cake against which buckwheat cakes and corn cakes were judged. It is still what many mountain cooks mean by pancakes.

Why it tastes this way

Low sugar lets the toasted flour and the sour milk show. That balance comes from a kitchen that treated syrup as the sweet course and the cake as bread. Baking soda is here for the acid in the milk. Without it the crumb is heavy and the tang tastes harsh rather than lifted.

How it is made

A lightly sweetened buttermilk batter is cooked on a griddle in a moderate stack.

Authentic touch. Keep the sugar to a spoon. If you miss the diner taste, add syrup on the plate instead of turning the batter into cake.

How it is used

Butter and sorghum, honey, or fruit butter.

Field notes

  • The plainness is intentional. Syrup was the sweetener.
  • Apple butter on top is a mountain pantry match.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup buttermilk
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons melted butter or lard

Steps

  1. Stir the dry ingredients together, then stir in the wet ingredients until the batter is combined.
  2. Rest 5 minutes. The batter should fall from the spoon in a thick ribbon.
  3. Heat a griddle or heavy skillet over medium heat and film it with butter.
  4. Spoon on the batter. Cook until the underside is browned and the top looks set.
  5. Flip once and cook the second side through.
  6. Serve with syrup on the side, not cooked into the cake.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Half buckwheat flour moves this toward Appalachian buckwheat cakes.
  • A mashed banana is a later addition, not the old cake.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • sorghum
  • honey
  • apple butter
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Ozark Corn Cakes

Yellow corn, buttermilk, and a fried edge.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 cakes

History

The Ozarks of Missouri and Arkansas share the upland South’s corn kitchen. Corn cakes cooked on a griddle were a farm breakfast alongside biscuits when wheat was on hand, and instead of biscuits when it was not. They belong with Appalachian corn cakes and are listed separately because Ozark cooks talk about their own tables, not as a suburb of Kentucky.

Cultural background

A simple meal-and-milk cake, eaten with sorghum, honey, or a savory meal. German-speaking settlers in parts of the region also had potato cakes. This entry stays with corn.

Why it tastes this way

Dent corn meal, stone-ground when you can get it, tastes sweeter and rougher than a wheat pancake. Buttermilk is the tenderizer. The Ozark pattern is the same agricultural fact as the Appalachian one: corn would grow on ridges where a wheat field was a gamble.

How it is made

Cornmeal, buttermilk, and egg are cooked as small cakes on a greased griddle.

Authentic touch. If the meal is very coarse, give the batter fifteen minutes. Fine meal needs less time and burns faster.

How it is used

Sorghum and butter, or with beans and fried potatoes.

Field notes

  • Ozark corn cakes and Appalachian cornmeal cakes are relatives, not one recipe with two labels.
  • Stone-ground meal needs a longer rest than degerminated supermarket meal.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup plain yellow cornmeal
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1 cup buttermilk
  • 1 large egg
  • 1 tablespoon oil or melted butter

Steps

  1. Mix all the ingredients and rest 10 to 15 minutes.
  2. Heat a skillet with a thin film of oil.
  3. Cook small cakes until the bottoms brown.
  4. Flip and cook until set.
  5. Keep finished cakes warm in a low oven.
  6. Serve with sorghum or a savory plate.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of bacon drippings in the batter is a savory turn.
  • Adding wheat flour makes a lighter cake and removes the gluten-free label.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Bread course
  • sorghum
  • fried potatoes
  • beans
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Kentucky Spoonbread

A soft cornmeal custard, rich with butter and egg, spooned rather than sliced.

15 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 6 · one 8-inch dish

History

Spoonbread is a baked Southern cornmeal dish, soft enough to serve with a spoon. Kentucky and Virginia both claim it as a company table dish. It descends from cornmeal mushes and from soufflé-like breads that became popular in nineteenth-century American cooking. It is not a flipped pancake. It is the baked cousin of the corn griddle cake, included here because Appalachian and Southern tables treat it as part of the same corn-bread family.

Cultural background

It is a side for ham, beans, or a Sunday table, not a syrup stack. The crisp top and custard center are the contrast people wait for.

Why it tastes this way

Cornmeal scalded in milk tastes toasted and sweet, and eggs lift it in the oven the way they lift a Dutch baby. Wheat flour, when a recipe uses a spoon of it, is only a stabilizer. The flavor is corn, butter, and egg. Mountain kitchens that kept a cow and a sack of meal could make it without citrus, spice, or sugar.

How it is made

Cornmeal is cooked briefly with milk, enriched with butter and egg, and baked in a hot dish until set.

Authentic touch. Bake it in a preheated buttered dish so the edges fry a little. Serve it immediately. It sinks as it cools, which is normal, but it is best hot.

How it is used

Spoon it beside ham, greens, or beans. Butter on top is enough.

Field notes

  • Spoonbread is eaten with a spoon from the dish. It is not cut into squares like cornbread.
  • It belongs to the corn-bread family, which is why it sits in an Appalachian griddle book beside cakes that are actually flipped.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups whole milk
  • 3/4 cup plain cornmeal
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons butter, plus more for the dish
  • 2 tablespoons all-purpose flour
  • 3 large eggs, separated

Steps

  1. Heat the oven to 375°F. Butter a small baking dish and put it in the oven.
  2. Simmer milk, cornmeal, sugar, and salt, stirring, until thick, about 5 minutes.
  3. Stir in butter and flour. Cool 5 minutes.
  4. Beat in the yolks. Whip the whites to soft peaks and fold them in.
  5. Pour into the hot dish and bake about 35 minutes, until puffed and set.
  6. Serve at once with a spoon.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some recipes add a spoon of baking powder for a drier puff. This one stays custardy.
  • A spoon of white flour is traditional in some pots and is why this version is not marked gluten-free.

Pairings and uses

  • Side dish
  • Supper
  • Bread course
  • country ham
  • greens
  • butter
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Sweet Potato Pancakes

Roasted sweet potato, warm spice, and a soft wheat crumb.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 pancakes

History

Sweet potatoes have been a Southern and Appalachian staple for generations, grown in gardens where they stored well through winter. Pancakes that mash the cooked potato into a wheat batter are a home breakfast built on that pantry, not a single famous inventor’s recipe. The potato’s natural sweetness means the cake needs little sugar.

Cultural background

Families use leftover baked sweet potatoes. Cinnamon or nutmeg is common. The cake sits between a vegetable pancake and a wheat breakfast cake.

Why it tastes this way

Sweet potatoes store through a cool Appalachian winter and a humid Southern one, which is why they show up in breads as well as on the plate. Their moisture loosens the batter and their sugars brown fast, so the griddle has to stay moderate. Spice is optional. The potato is the flavor.

How it is made

Mashed roasted sweet potato is folded into a buttermilk pancake batter and cooked on a moderate griddle.

Authentic touch. Use a cooked, dry sweet potato, not a can of pie filling. Pie filling is already sweetened and spiced and will scorch.

How it is used

Butter and a light pour of sorghum are enough. Pecans on top are a Southern option and add a tree-nut allergen.

Field notes

  • A baked potato that has sat uncovered mashes in more cleanly than a steaming wet one.
  • This is a breakfast use of a staple crop, not a claim of one county’s invention.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup mashed roasted sweet potato
  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground cinnamon
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup buttermilk
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons melted butter

Steps

  1. Stir the dry ingredients together, then stir in the wet ingredients until the batter is combined.
  2. Fold the mashed sweet potato into the wet ingredients before they meet the flour. Rest 5 minutes.
  3. Heat a griddle or heavy skillet over medium heat and film it with butter.
  4. Spoon on the batter. Cook until the underside is browned and the top looks set.
  5. Flip once and cook the second side through.
  6. Serve warm. The centers should be set, not wet.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Skip the cinnamon if you want the potato to taste like itself.
  • Orange zest is a later idea. It is pleasant and not traditional.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Snack
  • sorghum
  • butter
  • pecans
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free
