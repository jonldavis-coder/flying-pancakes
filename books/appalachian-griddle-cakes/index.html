Appalachian Griddle Cakes

Buckwheat, sorghum, dried apple, and corn on an upland griddle.

These are the cakes that can be tied to the upland South: buckwheat, sorghum, dried apple, molasses, and a cornmeal griddle.

Scots-Irish and English settlers brought oat and wheat habits into the mountains. Corn was already there. Enslaved people and later Black Appalachian cooks worked the same kitchens. This book does not treat the mountains as one family's pantry.

Appalachian buckwheat cakes

Earthly and slightly sour, with a gray-brown crumb and a sorghum finish if you use it at the table.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 cakes

History

Appalachian buckwheat cakes use flour milled from buckwheat, a crop that grows on cooler mountain ground, usually mixed with wheat flour so the cake holds together. Scots-Irish and English settlement lines up with this griddle, and German-speaking neighbors lived in the same hills. Enslaved people and later Black Appalachian cooks worked many of the same farms. The cakes are a tangy breakfast, often finished with sorghum rather than maple.

Cultural background

Buckwheat is not a kind of wheat. This recipe still uses all-purpose flour, so the cakes are not gluten-free. Elkins stands in for a griddle that was widespread in the uplands.

Why it tastes this way

Buckwheat tastes stronger and earthier than white flour, and buttermilk sharpens it. Soda reacts with the buttermilk and lifts a batter that would otherwise be heavy. Sorghum at the table adds a mineral sweetness that white sugar does not.

How it is made

Griddled buttermilk and buckwheat batter.

Authentic touch. Keep at least half the flour as buckwheat so the cakes taste like the grain. Serve them with sorghum if you can get it.

How it is used

Hot, with butter and sorghum syrup.

Field notes

  • Buckwheat fell out of mountain fields as wheat flour became cheap, and the cakes got rarer with it.
  • Sorghum, not maple, was the everyday sweetener with these cakes in much of the upland South.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup buckwheat flour
  • 1/2 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 cups buttermilk
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the griddle

Steps

  1. Whisk both flours, the baking soda, and the salt.
  2. Whisk in the buttermilk, egg, and melted butter until just combined. The batter will be dark and a bit thick.
  3. Heat a buttered griddle over medium heat.
  4. Pour 1/4 cup rounds. Cook until the top is set and the underside is brown, then flip.
  5. The second side browns faster. Move the cakes off when the center springs back.
  6. Serve with butter and sorghum syrup.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A yeasted overnight batter is the older leavening and belongs to a longer method.
  • All-buckwheat cakes are more fragile and are not automatically gluten-free unless the flour is certified.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Sorghum syrup
  • Butter
  • Sausage, if you eat meat
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Appalachian sorghum pancakes

Malty and lightly bitter-sweet from sorghum, with a plain wheat crumb underneath.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 pancakes

History

Appalachian sorghum pancakes are wheat cakes sweetened with sorghum syrup, the juice of sorghum cane pressed and boiled at local mills. Sorghum, more than maple, was the everyday sweetener in much of the upland South. The syrup goes into the batter and onto the finished cakes. Whitesburg is a map pin in eastern Kentucky, where sorghum is still pressed in season.

Cultural background

Sorghum mills were neighborhood work in the fall. The syrup is not molasses made from sugarcane, and it is not maple. Those three sweeteners show up in different cakes in this journal.

Why it tastes this way

Sorghum tastes grassy and deep, with a slight bitterness that keeps the cake from tasting like candy. Baking soda balances some of that acid edge and helps the batter rise. Butter rounds the bitterness at the table.

How it is made

Griddled wheat batter sweetened with sorghum.

Authentic touch. Put sorghum in the batter and serve more on the side. Maple syrup turns this into a different breakfast.

How it is used

Stacked, with butter and a second pour of sorghum.

Field notes

  • Sorghum syrup comes from sorghum cane crushed at a mill, not from maple trees.
  • In much of Appalachia it was the sweetener people made nearby, while white sugar was bought.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 1/2 cups buttermilk
  • 3 tablespoons sorghum syrup, plus more to serve
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the griddle

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, baking powder, baking soda, and salt.
  2. Whisk the buttermilk, sorghum syrup, egg, and melted butter in a second bowl.
  3. Stir the wet ingredients into the dry ingredients and stop as soon as the flour is moist.
  4. Butter a griddle over medium heat. Pour 1/4 cup cakes.
  5. Flip when the bubbles set. Cook until the center is done.
  6. Serve with butter and more sorghum syrup.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Use the syrup only at the table if you want a plainer crumb.
  • Buckwheat flour in place of half the wheat flour moves the cake toward the buckwheat page.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Sorghum syrup
  • Butter
  • Black coffee
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Appalachian dried-apple pancakes

Soft wheat cakes with chewy, tart pieces of dried apple and a hint of cinnamon.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 pancakes

History

Appalachian dried-apple pancakes use apples that were dried for winter, a common mountain way to keep fruit after the fresh crop was gone. The slices are soaked and then folded into a wheat batter. The cake is a cold-weather breakfast in the upland South, not a fresh-apple diner special. Boone, in the North Carolina mountains, is the map pin.

Cultural background

Drying rings of apple on strings or racks was ordinary preservation before reliable refrigeration. The pancake is one way those apples came back to the table.

Why it tastes this way

Dried apples keep a sharper, chewier taste than fresh slices, which turn soft and watery in a batter. A short soak brings back enough moisture that they are not leathery. Cinnamon is restrained so the fruit still tastes like apple.

How it is made

Griddled wheat batter with rehydrated dried apples folded in.

Authentic touch. Start with dried apples and soak them. Fresh apples are a different cake, filed with the cider pancakes.

How it is used

Hot, with butter. Sorghum or a little sugar is enough sweetness.

Field notes

  • Dried apples were a winter pantry staple in the mountains, used when fresh fruit was gone.
  • Soaking them first keeps the dried edges from staying leathery inside the cake.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup dried apple slices
  • 1 cup boiling water
  • 1 1/2 cups all-purpose flour
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 2 teaspoons baking powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground cinnamon
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 1/4 cups buttermilk
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the griddle

Steps

  1. Put the dried apples in a bowl, cover them with the boiling water, and soak 15 minutes. Drain and chop them. The soaking liquid can be discarded.
  2. Whisk the flour, sugar, baking powder, baking soda, cinnamon, and salt.
  3. Stir in the buttermilk, egg, and melted butter until just combined. Fold in the apples.
  4. Butter a griddle over medium heat. Pour 1/4 cup cakes.
  5. Cook a little longer than plain pancakes, until the apple pieces are hot and the center is set. Flip once.
  6. Serve with butter and, if you want it, sorghum.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Leave out the cinnamon if the apples are strongly flavored.
  • A handful of the soaked apples, chopped finer, disappears into the crumb.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Butter
  • Sorghum syrup
  • Hot coffee
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Lacy cornbread

Crisp, salty corn with a brittle lace of holes and a tender spot in the middle.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 skillet cakes

History

Lacy cornbread is a very thin cornmeal batter cooked in a hot skillet until the surface opens into crisp holes. It belongs to Appalachian corn cooking, especially in West Virginia and southwestern Virginia. It is a griddle bread, closer to a crisp cake than to a deep pan of cornbread. The batter is meal, water, and salt.

Cultural background

Mountain cooks poured this batter into a hot greased skillet when they wanted cornbread in a hurry. The lace is what the heat does to a thin batter, not a separate ingredient.

Why it tastes this way

Extra water lets the batter spider across the pan instead of sitting in a mound. Hot fat fries the strands as they spread, so the cake tastes toasted and crisp. Coarse meal keeps a corn flavor that wheat cornbread would soften.

How it is made

Poured thin into a very hot oiled skillet so the batter laces.

Authentic touch. The batter should look thin, almost like cream. If it sits in a thick puddle, add water. The lace is the test that you got it right.

How it is used

Whole from the skillet, broken at the table, often with beans or greens.

Field notes

  • The holes are the batter frying as it spreads, which is why cooks call it lacy.
  • It is closer to a crisp griddle cake than to a square of baked cornbread.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup cornmeal
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 3/4 cups water
  • 3 tablespoons neutral oil, divided

Steps

  1. Whisk the cornmeal, salt, water, and 1 tablespoon of the oil into a thin batter. It should run off the spoon.
  2. Rest the batter 5 minutes and whisk again.
  3. Heat a small skillet over medium-high heat. Add 1 1/2 teaspoons of oil and let it get hot.
  4. Pour in about 1/3 cup batter and tilt the pan. The batter should spread and break into holes.
  5. Cook until the top looks dry and the underside is brown and crisp, then flip carefully and brown the other side.
  6. Repeat with the remaining oil and batter. Serve hot.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Bacon fat in place of oil is common in kitchens that cook pork.
  • A spoon of wheat flour stops the lace and makes a different bread.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Accompaniment to a meal
  • Pot beans
  • Cooked greens
  • Butter, if you eat dairy
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Southern hoecakes

Plain toasted corn, salt, and a fried edge, denser than a wheat pancake.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 hoecakes

History

Southern hoecakes are round cornmeal cakes cooked on a griddle. The story that they were baked on the blade of a hoe is widely repeated and disputed, and this page does not treat that story as fact. Hoecakes were ordinary food in the South, including at eighteenth-century Virginia tables such as Mount Vernon. This version is meal, salt, and boiling water, with no wheat.

Cultural background

Corn was the everyday grain. A hoecake was a way to cook a quick bread on a flat iron when there was no oven loaf. The name survives even though the hoe-blade explanation is not settled.

Why it tastes this way

Scalding the meal softens the coarse parts and lets the cake hold together without egg or flour. The surface against the fat tastes toasted. The inside stays moist and distinctly of corn.

How it is made

Shaped cornmeal rounds griddled in a thin film of oil.

Authentic touch. Make them thicker than a Rhode Island johnnycake, about a finger thick, and cook them through. Ignore the hoe story and use a griddle.

How it is used

Hot, as a bread beside beans or with syrup if you want them sweet.

Field notes

  • The hoe-blade baking story is famous and disputed.
  • George Washington's household at Mount Vernon ate hoecakes, which is well documented.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups cornmeal
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 1/4 cups boiling water
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil, plus more for the griddle

Steps

  1. Stir the cornmeal, salt, and oil in a bowl.
  2. Pour in the boiling water and stir to a dough stiff enough to hold a round. Rest 10 minutes.
  3. With wet hands, shape 8 rounds about 1/2 inch thick.
  4. Heat a film of oil on a griddle over medium heat.
  5. Cook the cakes until the underside is browned and the side looks set, about 4 minutes, then flip and cook until the center is no longer wet.
  6. Serve hot. They are bread, not a tall diner stack.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Grease the griddle with bacon drippings if you eat pork.
  • A spoon of wheat flour in the batter is a later, softer version and is no longer gluten-free.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Accompaniment to a meal
  • Potlikker or beans
  • Sorghum syrup
  • Butter, if you eat dairy
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Molasses pancakes

Strong, dark molasses in a tender wheat crumb, bittersweet rather than floral.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 pancakes

History

Molasses pancakes are a wheat breakfast sweetened with molasses, the sugarcane syrup bought from the store, rather than the sorghum pressed at a nearby mill. In Appalachia both sweeteners were used, and they are not the same product. This cake keeps a strong molasses note in the crumb. Clay, West Virginia, is the map pin in the upland South.

Cultural background

Molasses is a byproduct of sugar making. It was a trade sweetener in mountain stores. Sorghum has its own page because the flavor and the agriculture are different.

Why it tastes this way

Molasses tastes darker and more bitter than sorghum, with a note some people compare to licorice. That bitterness is the flavor, so do not drown it in extra sugar. Soda and buttermilk lift the batter and soften the edge. The cakes brown fast.

How it is made

Griddled wheat batter sweetened with molasses.

Authentic touch. Use unsulfured molasses in the batter and serve more at the table if you want it. Sorghum or maple makes a different pancake.

How it is used

Hot, with butter and, if you want it, a thin ribbon of molasses.

Field notes

  • Molasses comes from sugarcane refining. Sorghum syrup comes from sorghum cane pressed nearby.
  • Appalachian cooks used both, and this page is the molasses cake.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 1/4 cups buttermilk
  • 1/4 cup unsulfured molasses, plus more to serve
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the griddle

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, baking powder, baking soda, and salt.
  2. Whisk the buttermilk, molasses, egg, and melted butter until the molasses dissolves.
  3. Stir the wet mixture into the flour until just combined.
  4. Butter a griddle over medium-low heat. Pour 1/4 cup cakes.
  5. Flip when the underside is deep brown and the top is set. Do not wait for a pale cake to darken further or it will scorch.
  6. Serve with butter and more molasses if you want it.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A milder cake uses two tablespoons of molasses instead of a quarter cup.
  • Sorghum in the same amount is the other Appalachian sweetener and belongs to the sorghum page.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Butter
  • Molasses
  • Black coffee
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Southeastern corn griddle cakes

Plain corn, salt, and a toasted surface, with no sugar and no wheat.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 cakes

History

Southeastern corn griddle cakes sit in the family of corn breads cooked by Native nations of the Southeast, where corn was the staple grain long before wheat arrived. Those breads are not all the same. This plain cornmeal cake does not belong to one nation, and it is not a ceremonial bread rewritten as a diner recipe. The batter is meal, water, and salt, cooked on a griddle.

Cultural background

The Tennessee River valley is a geographic pin for the wider Southeast, not a claim that one town or one nation owns the cake. Hopi piki is a different food and has its own page. Wheat is left out on purpose.

Why it tastes this way

The flavor is toasted cornmeal and salt. Water instead of milk keeps it plain. A moderate thickness gives a soft center and a firm crust. Sugar, egg, and wheat would each pull it toward a later American pancake.

How it is made

Plain cornmeal batter cooked as medium rounds on a griddle.

Authentic touch. Cook meal, water, and salt. Do not present the result as the ceremonial bread of a nation, and do not say that every Southeastern nation made this exact cake.

How it is used

Hot, as a bread. It needs no syrup.

Field notes

  • Corn was the staple of Southeastern Native nations long before wheat flour arrived.
  • This recipe is a plain griddle cake in that family, and it is not owned by one nation.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup cornmeal
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup warm water
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil, plus more for the griddle

Steps

  1. Stir the cornmeal, salt, water, and oil into a batter that holds a shape and still spreads a little.
  2. Rest 5 minutes. If it stiffened, stir in a spoon of water.
  3. Film a griddle with oil over medium heat.
  4. Drop about 1/4 cup of batter for each cake and nudge it into a round about 1/2 inch thick.
  5. Cook until the underside is brown and the edge looks dry, then flip and cook until the center is set.
  6. Serve hot as a bread.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A thinner batter, cooked crisp, moves toward lacy cornbread.
  • Adding wheat flour, buttermilk, and egg makes a New England-style corn cake and adds those allergens.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Accompaniment to a meal
  • Salt
  • Cooked greens or beans
  • A drizzle of oil
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Sweet potato pancakes

Orange-fleshed sweet potato, a little cinnamon, and a moist wheat crumb.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 pancakes

History

Sweet potato pancakes mash an orange-fleshed sweet potato into a wheat batter. Sweet potatoes have been a Southern field crop for a long time, in Native gardens and later in the kitchens of enslaved and free Black cooks and of white farm families. The griddle cake is a breakfast use of the mashed root. Wilson, North Carolina, is a modern market town for the crop and the map pin, not a claim that the pancake was invented there.

Cultural background

The root and the pancake are different ages. This page is the wheat breakfast cake. It does not assign an older sweet-potato bread to one community.

Why it tastes this way

Mashed sweet potato adds moisture and a flavor closer to roasted squash than to white potato. Cinnamon sits on top of that sweetness. Because the mash is wet, the flour has to be enough to keep the cakes from slumping.

How it is made

Griddled wheat batter enriched with mashed sweet potato.

Authentic touch. Use a cooked, mashed sweet potato with a drier texture, not a can of syrup-packed pieces. Orange-fleshed varieties are what most Southern cooks mean.

How it is used

Hot, with butter. They are sweet enough that syrup can be light.

Field notes

  • Wilson, North Carolina, is a long-running market center for Southern sweet potatoes.
  • The mashed root makes the batter wetter than a plain buttermilk pancake, so the flour amount matters.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup mashed cooked sweet potato, cooled
  • 1 1/2 cups all-purpose flour
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 2 teaspoons baking powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground cinnamon
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup buttermilk
  • 2 large eggs
  • 2 tablespoons unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the griddle

Steps

  1. Mash the sweet potato until smooth.
  2. Whisk the flour, sugar, baking powder, baking soda, cinnamon, and salt.
  3. Whisk the sweet potato with the buttermilk, eggs, and melted butter. Stir that into the flour just until combined.
  4. Butter a griddle over medium-low heat. The sugar and sweet potato brown quickly.
  5. Pour 1/4 cup cakes. Flip when the underside is deep gold and the top has set.
  6. Cook the second side until the center is no longer wet. Serve with butter.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Roast the sweet potato instead of boiling it for a deeper flavor.
  • Skip the cinnamon for a plainer cake.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Butter
  • A thin drizzle of sorghum
  • Pecans only if you want a tree-nut topping
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free
