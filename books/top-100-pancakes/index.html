Southern Buttermilk Pancakes

Tangy buttermilk, a soft crumb, and a browned butter edge.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 pancakes

History

Buttermilk pancakes became a Southern breakfast standard once chemical leaveners and commercial flour were ordinary pantry goods in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. The sour milk itself is older: farms churned butter and cooked with the buttermilk that remained. A tall stack with butter is the diner and home version most people mean by a Southern pancake.

Cultural background

Sunday breakfast, church potlucks, and roadside diners all serve them. The cake is a canvas. Sorghum, cane syrup, or molasses often stands in for maple, which was never the local sugar.

Why it tastes this way

Heat and dairy explain the taste. Buttermilk’s acid reacts with baking soda, so the crumb lifts and tastes faintly sour instead of flat and sweet. Southern summers favored preserved sweetness, which is why sorghum and cane syrup, not maple, are the old pours. Wheat flour from a mill, not corn, is what makes this particular cake soft rather than gritty.

How it is made

A wheat batter of flour, egg, and buttermilk is leavened with baking powder and soda and cooked on a buttered griddle.

Authentic touch. Stop stirring when the flour disappears. A few lumps are better than a tough cake, and the batter should rest about five minutes so the soda starts working before it hits the griddle.

How it is used

Stack them with butter and sorghum, cane syrup, or honey. Bacon or sausage on the side is the usual plate.

Field notes

  • Maple syrup is a later pour in much of the South. Sorghum and cane syrup are the older local sweets.
  • The soda is there because buttermilk is acidic. Sweet milk needs a different balance.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/4 teaspoon fine salt
  • 1 cup buttermilk
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons melted butter, plus more for the griddle

Steps

  1. Stir the dry ingredients together, then stir in the wet ingredients until the batter is combined.
  2. Let the batter rest 5 minutes. It should be thick enough to mound slightly.
  3. Heat a griddle or heavy skillet over medium heat and film it with butter.
  4. Spoon on the batter. Cook until the underside is browned and the top looks set.
  5. Flip once and cook the second side through.
  6. Serve at once with butter and syrup.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoonful of cornmeal in the batter leans toward a corn griddle cake.
  • Self-rising flour can replace the baking powder if you cut the salt and powder, but keep the soda for the buttermilk.

Pairings and uses

Hoe Cakes

Toasted cornmeal, salt, and the taste of a hot greased griddle.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 cakes

History

Hoe cakes are a Southern cornmeal bread cooked on a griddle. The usual explanation of the name is that cooks fried the batter on the blade of a field hoe. Enslaved African American cooks, and later Black Southern cooks, kept corn griddle breads in daily use. West African millet and corn cakes cooked on a griddle are part of the wider history. The hoe story is a traditional account, and the bread itself is well documented.

Cultural background

Hoe cakes were field food and kitchen food: portable, cheap, and made from the corn that dominated Southern rations and farms. They still show up next to greens, beans, and fish.

Why it tastes this way

Corn, not wheat, is the flavor, and that corn is tied to Indigenous agriculture in the Americas and to the rations and fields of slavery. A thin batter and a hot pan toast the meal instead of steaming it, which is why a hoe cake tastes browned and a boiled dumpling from the same meal does not. Little or no sugar keeps it a bread.

How it is made

Cornmeal is stirred with boiling water or cold water, salt, and a little fat, then cooked as thin rounds on a greased griddle.

Authentic touch. Spread the batter thin and cook it slower than a fluffy pancake. The cake is done when the corn smells toasted and the center is set, not when it puffs.

How it is used

Eat them hot with butter, potlikker, greens, or fried fish. They are a bread, not a dessert.

Field notes

  • The hoe-blade story is traditional. Treat it as an explanation people tell, not as a documented patent.
  • Use plain cornmeal. A self-rising mix often contains wheat.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup plain cornmeal, not a wheat mix
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
  • 1 cup boiling water, plus more if needed
  • 2 tablespoons oil or bacon drippings for the pan

Steps

  1. Stir salt into the cornmeal. Pour on the boiling water and stir until you have a thick, spreadable batter. Rest 5 minutes.
  2. Add a spoon of water if the batter seized up.
  3. Heat a skillet over medium and film it with oil.
  4. Spread small rounds about 1/4 inch thick.
  5. Cook until the underside is browned and the center is set, then flip.
  6. Serve hot as a bread.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some cooks add an egg and a spoon of flour. That is a softer cake and no longer gluten-free.
  • A pinch of sugar is a modern breakfast tilt. The older cake is savory.

Appalachian Buckwheat Cakes

Earthly buckwheat, a little sour, with a sturdy crumb.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 10 small cakes

History

Buckwheat cakes are an old mountain breakfast. Buckwheat grows on thin, cool soil where wheat struggles, which suited upland farms from Pennsylvania down the Appalachian chain. Settlers from German-speaking Pennsylvania and from the British Isles brought griddle habits. The cakes were often started with a bit of leftover batter that soured overnight.

Cultural background

They are winter food: dark, filling, and meant for molasses or sorghum. A lighter wheat pancake replaced them in many houses once white flour was cheap, but buckwheat never left the mountains entirely.

Why it tastes this way

Buckwheat is not a wheat. Its flavor is grassy and mineral, and cool mountain fields are why it was planted. An overnight rest gives a light sourness, the taste of a batter kept in a crock, not of a boxed mix. Molasses and sorghum match it because those were the local sugars. A little wheat flour in this home version keeps the cake from crumbling. Pure buckwheat is a different, more fragile cake.

How it is made

Buckwheat and a portion of wheat flour are mixed with buttermilk, egg, and leavener, rested, and cooked on a hot griddle.

Authentic touch. Cook them smaller than a diner pancake. Buckwheat batter sets slowly, and a huge cake stays raw in the middle.

How it is used

Butter and sorghum or molasses. Sausage is the classic side and makes the plate no longer vegetarian.

Field notes

  • Buckwheat thrived in cool uplands, which is the agricultural reason these cakes are mountain food.
  • This version uses some wheat so the cakes hold. The label is not gluten-free.

Ingredients

  • 3/4 cup buckwheat flour
  • 1/4 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon molasses
  • 1 large egg
  • 1 1/4 cups buttermilk
  • 2 tablespoons melted butter

Steps

  1. Stir the dry ingredients together, then stir in the wet ingredients until the batter is combined.
  2. Rest the batter 10 minutes. It will look thicker than a white-flour batter.
  3. Heat a griddle or heavy skillet over medium heat and film it with butter or bacon fat.
  4. Spoon on the batter. Cook until the underside is browned and the top looks set.
  5. Flip once and cook the second side through.
  6. Cook small cakes. The crumb should lose its shine before you serve them.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • For a gluten-free cake, use all buckwheat flour and accept a more fragile crumb.
  • A spoon of the batter saved overnight in the refrigerator is the old starter. Use it in the next batch if you like it sourer.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • sorghum
  • molasses
  • butter
  • sausage
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Sorghum Flapjacks

Mild wheat cake with the mineral sweetness of sorghum in the batter.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 pancakes

History

In American English a flapjack is a pancake. Sorghum, pressed from sweet sorghum cane and boiled to syrup, was a common Appalachian and upper-South sweetener in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, made on farms that could not rely on maple or imported sugar. Stirring a spoon of that syrup into the batter, and serving more at the table, is the local form of a wheat pancake.

Cultural background

The British flapjack, an oat traybake, is a different food and is not this recipe. Here the word means the griddle cake, which is how mountain cooks have long used it.

Why it tastes this way

Sorghum syrup tastes less purely sweet than maple and a little like the cane it was boiled from: mineral, faintly bitter, and dark. That flavor is the taste of a crop that ripens in a warm season and is processed in the fall. The cake itself stays plain so the syrup can be tasted twice, once inside and once on top.

How it is made

A buttermilk batter is sweetened with sorghum syrup and cooked as ordinary flapjacks, then served with more sorghum.

Authentic touch. Warm the sorghum so it blends into the milk. Cold syrup sits in a lump and burns in spots on the griddle.

How it is used

Butter and warm sorghum. They are a breakfast stack.

Field notes

  • American flapjacks are pancakes. British flapjacks are an oat bar and are not in this recipe.
  • Sorghum mills and stirring-off parties were a real part of upland farm life.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup buttermilk
  • 2 tablespoons sorghum syrup, plus more to serve
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons melted butter

Steps

  1. Stir the dry ingredients together, then stir in the wet ingredients until the batter is combined.
  2. Stir the sorghum into the buttermilk before it meets the flour, so the syrup dissolves.
  3. Heat a griddle or heavy skillet over medium heat and film it with butter.
  4. Spoon on the batter. Cook until the underside is browned and the top looks set.
  5. Flip once and cook the second side through.
  6. Serve with more warm sorghum. Do not cook the syrup on the bare griddle or it will scorch.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Molasses is darker and more bitter. It is a cousin, not the same pour.
  • Maple is a Northern swap and changes the story of the plate.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • sorghum syrup
  • butter
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Rhode Island Thin Johnnycakes

Crisp, lacy corn, salted, with a toasted smell.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 thin cakes

History

Johnnycakes are cornmeal griddle cakes of southern New England. Narragansett and other Algonquian peoples of the region grew and prepared corn before English settlement, and colonial cooks adopted cornmeal breads. The word’s origin is disputed: “journey cake” and a Narragansett word often given as jonikin are both proposed, and neither is settled. In Rhode Island, thin johnnycakes, especially in South County, are a local standard, spread thin and cooked slowly.

Cultural background

White flint cornmeal is the local pride when you can find it. The cakes are breakfast and a side for chowder. A state full of opinions treats the thin cake and the thick cake as different foods.

Why it tastes this way

Flint corn meal is drier and more aromatic than a soft yellow meal, which is why Rhode Island cakes taste toasted rather than sweet. A thin spread on a barely greased griddle crisps the starch. There is no wheat to soften it and no sugar to fake a dessert. The corn is the whole story, and the corn is an Indigenous crop that became colonial daily bread.

How it is made

White cornmeal and salt are scalded or stirred with water into a thin batter and cooked slowly on a griddle.

Authentic touch. Spread them thin with the back of a spoon and do not rush the flip. They tear if you turn them before the underside has actually crusted.

How it is used

Butter. Some people add maple syrup. Purists in the thin-cake camp often stop at butter.

Field notes

  • The etymology of johnnycake is not settled. This page does not pick a folk origin and call it fact.
  • Thin and thick Rhode Island cakes are local styles, not a single recipe.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup white cornmeal
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
  • 1 cup boiling water
  • 1/4 to 1/2 cup cold water, as needed
  • Oil or bacon fat for the griddle

Steps

  1. Stir salt into the meal. Add boiling water and mix.
  2. Thin with cold water until the batter spreads.
  3. Heat a griddle over medium-low and grease it lightly.
  4. Spread thin rounds.
  5. Cook until the underside is browned and the cake releases, then flip.
  6. Serve with butter.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Thick johnnycakes, on their own page, use cold milk and a deeper cake.
  • Yellow meal works in a pinch and tastes sweeter and less flinty.

Pairings and uses

  • Bread course
  • Side dish

  • maple syrup
  • clam chowder

Potato Latkes

Shredded potato, onion, and a lacy fried edge.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · about 12 latkes

History

Latkes are Ashkenazi potato pancakes associated with Hanukkah. Potato pancakes became everyday food in eastern Europe after potatoes were widely grown there. The Hanukkah link is the oil: the holiday remembers the oil of the Temple, and fried foods became the table. Jewish immigrants brought latkes to the United States, where they are now a December staple far beyond immigrant kitchens. Cheese pancakes were an earlier Hanukkah food in some communities. The potato latke is the one most Americans mean.

Cultural background

They are served with sour cream or applesauce. The argument between those two sauces is a family argument, not a rule. In Israel, sufganiyot, jelly donuts, share the holiday. This page is the pancake.

Why it tastes this way

Potato and onion, squeezed dry and fried in oil, taste sweet and savory at once because the potato’s starch browns and the onion’s sulfur softens. Hanukkah practice explains the fry: oil is the point of the food, not an accident of the method. Eggs bind the shreds. A little starch replaces the matzo meal some families use, and keeps this version gluten-free. The American Northeast did not invent the latke. It became a place where the holiday cake is cooked in huge skillets for a crowd.

How it is made

Russet potatoes and onion are shredded, squeezed, bound with egg and starch, and fried in oil until lacy.

Authentic touch. Squeeze the shredded potato and onion until the liquid stops dripping, then let that liquid sit and pour off the water. The starch that settles can go back in. Wet shreds steam and never crisp.

How it is used

Applesauce, sour cream, or both. They are a holiday side, not a syrup breakfast.

Field notes

  • Oil is the Hanukkah reason for frying, tied to the Temple story as Jewish tradition tells it.
  • Sour cream and applesauce are both served. Neither one cancels the other.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds russet potatoes
  • 1 small onion
  • 2 large eggs
  • 2 tablespoons potato starch
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/4 teaspoon black pepper
  • Neutral oil for frying

Steps

  1. Grate the potatoes and onion. Squeeze them hard in a towel.
  2. If a lot of liquid came out, let the starch settle and add that paste back.
  3. Mix in eggs, potato starch, salt, and pepper.
  4. Heat 1/4 inch of oil over medium.
  5. Fry flattened spoonfuls until deep golden on both sides.
  6. Drain on a rack and salt them. Serve hot.

Shallow-fry over steady heat. Do not leave the pan.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Matzo meal instead of potato starch is common and is not gluten-free.
  • A sweet potato latke is a later American variation.

Pairings and uses

  • Holiday table
  • Supper
  • Side dish
  • applesauce
  • sour cream
  • salt

New York Blintzes

Thin egg crepes folded around sweet cheese, browned in butter.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 blintzes

History

Blintzes are the Ashkenazi filled pancake, descended from Slavic blini and crepes. Jewish immigrants from the Russian Empire and eastern Europe brought them to New York in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and dairy restaurants and home kitchens made cheese blintzes a city staple. They are a Shavuot dairy food in Jewish practice and an everyday deli plate the rest of the year.

Cultural background

A blintz is cooked on one side, filled, folded like an envelope, and browned. The New York cheese filling is farmer cheese or pot cheese, lightly sweet. Fruit sauce on the side is common in restaurants.

Why it tastes this way

The wrapper is almost a crepe: egg-rich and thin, so the cheese is the flavor. Eastern European Jewish cooks used the soft cheeses they had, lightly sweetened, because Shavuot is a dairy holiday and because those cheeses were the pantry. Browning in butter, a second cook, gives the outside a diner crust that a pale crepe does not have. The New York plate often adds a tart fruit sauce because the filling is mild.

How it is made

Thin egg pancakes are filled with sweetened farmer cheese, folded, and fried in butter.

Authentic touch. Cook the wrapper on one side only before filling. If you brown both sides first, it cracks when you fold it.

How it is used

Sour cream and a spoon of blueberry or cherry sauce, or simply sour cream.

Field notes

  • Blintzes and blini are relatives. A blintz is filled and folded. A blin is usually the pancake itself.
  • Dairy restaurants helped make cheese blintzes a New York dish, not only a holiday one.

Ingredients

  • 3/4 cup all-purpose flour
  • 2 large eggs
  • 1 cup milk
  • 2 tablespoons melted butter, plus more for frying
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup farmer cheese or drained ricotta
  • 1 egg yolk
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon vanilla

Steps

  1. Blend flour, eggs, milk, 2 tablespoons butter, and salt into a thin batter. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Cook thin crepes on one side in a buttered skillet. Stack them browned side up.
  3. Mix cheese, yolk, sugar, and vanilla.
  4. Fill each crepe, fold the sides in, and roll.
  5. Brown the packets in butter over medium heat.
  6. Serve with sour cream.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A potato and onion filling is the savory cousin and is closer to a knish in spirit.
  • Ricotta can stand in for farmer cheese. It is wetter, so drain it.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Holiday table
  • Stuffed or filled
  • sour cream
  • blueberry sauce
  • cherry preserves
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Pennsylvania Dutch Potato Pancakes

Shredded potato and onion, bound with egg and a little flour.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 10 cakes

History

German-speaking immigrants, later called Pennsylvania Dutch, settled in Pennsylvania in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries and brought potato pancakes with them. The cakes are everyday farm food, served with applesauce or sour cream, and they are part of the same family as German Kartoffelpuffer. They are not the same dish as Jewish latkes, though a hungry person could mistake the plate. The communities and the holiday meanings differ.

Cultural background

They show up at breakfast with apple butter and at supper beside meat. Church suppers still fry them by the dozen.

Why it tastes this way

A potato pancake tastes of starch browned in fat. German-speaking farmers in Pennsylvania had the potatoes, the onions, and the skillets. Flour in this version is the binder those recipes often use, which is why it is not gluten-free. Apple butter and applesauce on the side are the region’s fruit, preserved for winter, cutting the fry with acid.

How it is made

Grated potato and onion are mixed with egg and flour and fried as flat cakes.

Authentic touch. Squeeze the potatoes dry. Fry in a generous film of fat, not a dry nonstick pan, or the shreds never lace.

How it is used

Applesauce, apple butter, or sour cream.

Field notes

  • Pennsylvania Dutch means German-speaking settlers, from Deutsch, not a separate Dutch nation in this name.
  • These are relatives of German potato pancakes and are a different community’s food from Hanukkah latkes.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 pounds russet potatoes
  • 1 small onion
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons all-purpose flour
  • 3/4 teaspoon salt
  • Black pepper
  • Oil or butter for frying

Steps

  1. Grate potatoes and onion and squeeze dry.
  2. Mix with egg, flour, salt, and pepper.
  3. Heat a thin layer of oil over medium.
  4. Fry flattened spoonfuls until brown and crisp on both sides.
  5. Drain and salt.
  6. Serve with applesauce.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Leftover mashed potato makes a denser cake, closer to some boxty methods, and is a different texture.
  • Bacon fat is a farmhouse fat and adds meat.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Side dish
  • Supper
  • applesauce
  • apple butter
  • sour cream
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Swedish American Pancakes

Thin, eggy pancakes, often rolled, with lingonberry or butter and sugar.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 10 thin pancakes

History

Swedish immigrants in Minnesota, Wisconsin, and neighboring states kept the thin pancake, pannkaka, and the even smaller plättar. Church suppers and lodge breakfasts made “Swedish pancakes” a Midwestern event: stacks of thin cakes, lingonberry or strawberry preserves, and coffee. The batter is the Swedish one. The scale is American community cooking.

Cultural background

A special pan with round wells makes plättar. A ordinary skillet makes larger thin pancakes that are rolled. Both appear at the same suppers.

Why it tastes this way

More egg and a thinner batter than a fluffy American pancake give a crepe-like bite that rolls without breaking. Lingonberries are the tart northern fruit Swedes preserved, and imported preserves kept that taste on the prairie when the berry itself did not grow in the church kitchen. Butter and sugar, the other topping, is the plain home version.

How it is made

A thin egg batter is cooked like crepes, then rolled or stacked with preserves.

Authentic touch. The first cake will tell you if the pan is hot enough. It should set within a minute and come free in one piece. Thin the batter with a splash of milk if it will not spread.

How it is used

Lingonberry preserves, or butter and sugar. Whipped cream is a festive addition.

Field notes

  • Church-basement Swedish pancakes are a real Upper Midwest institution.
  • Lingonberry is traditional. Strawberry jam is the common substitute and should be called that.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 2 large eggs
  • 1 1/2 cups milk
  • 2 tablespoons melted butter
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • Lingonberry preserves, to serve

Steps

  1. Whisk everything except the preserves into a thin batter. Rest 10 minutes.
  2. Butter a skillet over medium-high.
  3. Pour a thin layer, swirl, and cook until the top looks dry.
  4. Flip briefly.
  5. Roll with preserves or stack with butter and sugar.
  6. Serve warm.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A plättar pan makes the smaller cakes. The batter is the same.
  • Swedish pancakes in Sweden are on their own page. This one is the Upper Midwest supper version.

Lefse

A soft potato flatbread, lightly browned in spots.

40 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 8 · 8 rounds

History

Lefse is a Norwegian potato flatbread cooked on a griddle. It traveled with Norwegian immigrants to the Upper Midwest of the United States, where church basements still roll it for holidays. There are several lefse types in Norway. This is the potato lefse most Norwegian-Americans mean: mashed potato, flour, a little fat, rolled thin.

Cultural background

It is served with butter and sugar, or butter and cinnamon sugar, folded or rolled. It is a bread and a holiday food, not a syrup stack. The Midwest page of the journal is this same bread, cooked far from Norway.

Why it tastes this way

Potato keeps the bread moist and mild, and a dry griddle spots it brown without frying it hard. Butter and sugar on a warm lefse melt into the cloth-soft crumb. Immigrants kept the bread because it marked Christmas and church, and because potatoes and flour were available on the prairie. The taste is potato, wheat, and browned spots, then whatever you spread.

How it is made

Cold mashed potato is mixed with butter, cream, and flour, rolled into rounds, and cooked on a dry griddle.

Authentic touch. Chill the mashed potato before you add flour, and roll it thin enough to see the board almost through it. Thick lefse tastes like dough. A sock-covered rolling pin is the traditional anti-stick tool. Parchment and cold dough also work.

How it is used

Butter and sugar, folded, while still flexible.

Field notes

  • Upper Midwest church lefse is this Norwegian bread in a new country.
  • Roll it thin. Thickness is the usual failure.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups riced cold mashed potato
  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • 2 tablespoons cream
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup all-purpose flour, plus more for rolling

Steps

  1. Mix potato, butter, cream, and salt. Work in enough flour to make a soft dough.
  2. Divide into 8 pieces and roll very thin on a floured cloth or board.
  3. Cook on a dry medium-hot griddle until spotted, about a minute a side.
  4. Stack under a towel so they stay flexible.
  5. Spread with butter and sugar and fold.
  6. Unused rounds can be frozen.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

History

Æbleskiver are Danish round pancakes cooked in a cast-iron pan with hemispherical wells. The name means apple slices, and some recipes tuck in a bit of apple, though many modern æbleskiver have no apple inside. They are a Christmas and winter treat in Denmark and at Danish American church events. The pan is essential.

Cultural background

Powdered sugar and jam, especially raspberry, are the finish. They are turned with a knitting needle or a skewer so the sphere browns all over.

Why it tastes this way

The well traps steam and batter so the cake becomes a ball instead of a disk. A little cardamom is a common Danish perfume. Sugar on the outside clings to the crisp shell. The apple in the name records an older filling. Leaving the apple out is common now and should be admitted, not hidden.

How it is made

A thick batter is cooked in an æbleskiver pan, turned until round, and sugared.

Authentic touch. Fill the wells two-thirds full. Turn the cakes before the batter on top is completely dry, so the seam seals into a ball. Too late, and you get a hemisphere.

How it is used

Hot, with powdered sugar and jam.

Field notes

  • The name refers to apples, but many æbleskiver today have none inside.
  • Danish American festivals still cook them as heritage food.

  • 1 cup milk
  • 2 tablespoons melted butter
  • Powdered sugar, to serve

Steps

  2. Butter an æbleskiver pan over medium heat.

Seattle Dutch Baby

A puffed oven pancake, crisp at the rim, eggy in the center.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · one 10-inch pancake

History

The Dutch baby is a German oven pancake. The “Dutch” in the name is generally explained as a muddle of Deutsch, not a recipe from the Netherlands. In Seattle, Manca’s Cafe is often credited with serving it in the early twentieth century and with the name that stuck in American restaurants. David Eyre’s pancake, a related skillet pancake published later, is a cousin. The Netherlands has its own pannenkoeken, which are a different food.

Cultural background

It is baked in a hot buttered skillet and brought to the table puffed, then collapses. Lemon and powdered sugar are the usual finish. Fruit is a fair variation.

Why it tastes this way

Steam from a hot pan and a wet, eggy batter inflates the rim before the center sets. That is why it looks like a popover the size of the skillet. Butter browns the bottom. Lemon is there because the cake is rich and needs acid. The Seattle story is immigration and restaurants, not a medieval Dutch guild.

How it is made

A blender batter of egg, milk, and flour is poured into a preheated buttered skillet and baked until risen.

Authentic touch. The skillet and the oven must be hot before the batter goes in. A cold pan makes a flat custard. Serve it as soon as it leaves the oven if you want anyone to see the puff.

How it is used

Lemon juice and powdered sugar, or fruit.

Field notes

  • Dutch here is widely understood as a twist on Deutsch. The cake is German in origin.
  • Manca’s Cafe in Seattle is the restaurant usually credited with the American name. Treat that credit as the common account.

Ingredients

  • 3 large eggs
  • 2/3 cup whole milk
  • 2/3 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 3 tablespoons butter
  • Lemon and powdered sugar, to serve

Steps

  1. Heat the oven to 425°F with a 10-inch ovenproof skillet inside.
  2. Blend eggs, milk, flour, sugar, and salt until smooth.
  3. Add the butter to the hot skillet and let it melt.
  4. Pour in the batter and bake 16 to 20 minutes, until puffed and browned.
  5. Do not open the door early.
  6. Dust with sugar and lemon and serve at once.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A savory baby skips the sugar and is finished with herbs and cheese.

New Mexico Blue Corn Pancakes

Slate-blue corn, a little sweet, with a toasted smell.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 cakes

History

Blue corn is a Southwestern crop tended for centuries by Pueblo peoples, including Hopi farmers, and by other Indigenous growers. Hopi piki is a paper-thin blue-corn bread made with culinary ash on a hot stone. This guide does not give a piki recipe, because the ash and the stone are a specific practice and an improvised ash ratio would be a guess. Blue corn pancakes are a modern New Mexican breakfast that uses the regional meal in a simple griddle batter.

Cultural background

Cafés in Santa Fe and Albuquerque serve them with piñon, honey, or red chile on the side. Piñon is a tree nut and is optional here. The batter itself is meal, milk, and egg.

Why it tastes this way

Blue corn tastes earthier and a little sweeter than yellow meal because of the variety, not because of food coloring. It is an Indigenous crop adapted to high, dry fields. The pancake format is new. Honey or a side of red chile, sweet or hot, is how New Mexican plates frame corn. The chile is a regional sauce, not part of the batter, so this cake is marked as no chili heat.

How it is made

Blue cornmeal is mixed with milk, egg, and a little leavener and cooked as small cakes.

Authentic touch. Blue cornmeal can be coarse. Rest the batter 10 minutes. Cook on moderate heat so the natural sugars do not scorch into black spots.

How it is used

Honey or butter. A side of red chile is a New Mexican option and adds heat the batter does not have. Piñon nuts add a tree-nut allergen.

Field notes

  • Piki is a different Hopi bread. It is acknowledged here and not copied.
  • Blue corn pancakes are a modern café and home use of an older crop.

Ingredients

  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/4 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup milk
  • 1 large egg
  • 1 tablespoon melted butter

Steps

  1. Stir all ingredients and rest 10 minutes.
  2. Cook small cakes on a buttered medium-low griddle.
  3. Flip when the underside is browned.
  4. The center should be set, not chalky.
  5. Serve with honey or butter.
  6. Red chile on the side is optional and is a separate condiment.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

  • Yellow cornmeal makes a different cake. Do not dye it blue.

Navajo Frybread

Hot fried dough, blistered, salty, and soft inside.

20 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 8 · 8 pieces

History

Frybread is widely associated with Diné (Navajo) cooking and with the ration foods of the Long Walk period in the 1860s, when people were forced from their land to Bosque Redondo and issued commodity staples such as white flour and lard. It is not a pre-contact bread. Many Native cooks claim it as survival food and also talk about its costs. This page follows that history and gives the plain home method, without turning the bread into a costume or a joke.

Cultural background

It is festival food, family food, and the base of a Navajo taco when it is topped with beans, meat, and chile. The plain bread is what this recipe is.

Why it tastes this way

White flour and fat were the ingredients on hand, which is why frybread tastes of fried wheat dough rather than of corn. A little leavener makes blisters. The history is forced relocation and rations, not a timeless desert recipe. Honey or powdered sugar is one way to eat it. Salt and chile stew is another. Both are current. Neither erases the origin.

How it is made

Flour, baking powder, salt, and water are mixed into a soft dough, rested briefly, patted into rounds, and fried.

Authentic touch. Fry at a moderate heat. If the oil smokes, the outside burns and the center stays raw. The dough should be soft, not as stiff as a biscuit.

How it is used

Honey, powdered sugar, or as a bread beside stew. A Navajo taco adds toppings and often meat.

Field notes

  • Frybread is tied to the Long Walk era and commodity foods. It is not presented here as an ancient pre-contact recipe.
  • Native cooks disagree about how often it should be eaten. The history on this page is not a nutrition lecture and not a dismissal.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups all-purpose flour
  • 1 tablespoon baking powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 3/4 cup warm water, plus more if needed
  • Neutral oil for frying

Steps

  1. Stir flour, baking powder, and salt. Add water and mix to a soft dough. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Divide into 8 pieces and pat each into a disk about 1/4 inch thick.
  3. Heat 1 inch of oil over medium, until a pinch of dough sizzles without smoking hard.
  4. Fry one or two at a time until blistered and golden, turning once.
  5. Drain on a rack.
  6. Serve hot.

Fry over steady heat and do not leave the pot. Let the oil cool before you discard it.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some cooks use powdered milk in the dough, which adds dairy.
  • The disk can be thicker or thinner. Thinner bread blisters more.

Hawaii Poi Pancakes

Mildly tangy taro paste in a soft wheat pancake.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 pancakes

History

Poi, mashed cooked taro, is a foundational Hawaiian food, thinned with water and often fermented until it tastes sour. Poi pancakes are a later home and restaurant breakfast that folds fresh or day-old poi into a wheat batter. They are not a substitute for the bowl of poi at a Hawaiian table. They are one way island cooks used a staple in a pancake shape that arrived with later cuisines.

Cultural background

Taro was a canoe-crop agriculture of immense skill, grown in flooded loʻi. The pancake is a small, modern use of the paste. If you cannot get poi, this is not the week to invent a taro candy and call it the same thing.

Why it tastes this way

Poi tastes clean when fresh and lightly sour when fermented, because the corm’s starch changes as it sits. That tang is the flavor in the cake, similar in role to buttermilk and entirely different in origin. Wheat flour is the later ingredient. The taro is the older one. A fermented poi makes a tangier cake. A fresh, sweet poi makes a milder, lavender-gray one.

How it is made

Unsweetened poi is whisked into a buttermilk pancake batter and cooked on a moderate griddle.

Authentic touch. Use unsweetened poi, stirred smooth. Sweetened taro bubble-tea powder is a different product and will make a dessert that is not this pancake.

How it is used

Butter and coconut syrup or honey. They are mild, so a tart fruit on the side helps.

Field notes

  • Poi pancakes are a later breakfast. The bowl of poi is the older dish.
  • Use real poi, not a sweetened taro flavoring.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup unsweetened poi, stirred smooth
  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 3/4 cup milk
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons melted butter

Steps

  1. Stir the dry ingredients together, then stir in the wet ingredients until the batter is combined.
  2. Whisk the poi into the milk before it meets the flour so there are no lumps of paste.
  3. Heat a griddle or heavy skillet over medium heat and film it with butter.
  4. Spoon on the batter. Cook until the underside is browned and the top looks set.
  5. Flip once and cook the second side through.
  6. Cook on medium. The cakes brown a little faster than a plain batter.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • More poi and less flour makes a denser, more taro-forward cake that is harder to flip.
  • If the poi is already very sour, use regular milk instead of buttermilk so the cake is not harsh.

Pairings and uses

  • pineapple
  • Vegetarian

Acadian Ployes

Mild buckwheat, soft on one side, with a few holes like a thick crepe.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 ployes

History

Ployes are Acadian buckwheat pancakes from the St. John Valley of northern Maine and neighboring New Brunswick. Acadians are the French-speaking people who were expelled from Nova Scotia by the British in 1755 and who rebuilt communities in places that include Louisiana and the Maritimes. In the north, buckwheat ployes stayed a daily bread. They are cooked on one side, not flipped, and eaten with butter, cretons, or maple syrup.

Cultural background

A ploye is closer to a bread than to a dessert pancake. Families keep a mix of buckwheat and wheat, though a pure buckwheat version is made. This recipe uses buckwheat only, with baking powder, so it is gluten-free.

Why it tastes this way

Buckwheat grows in a short, cool season, which is why it became the local grain. A wet batter on a hot griddle sets holes on top, a sign the water is steaming out. Cooking one side keeps the top soft for wrapping or for butter. The Acadian north and the Cajun south share a history of expulsion and do not share this bread. Louisiana’s rice calas are a different descendant.

How it is made

Buckwheat flour, water, salt, and baking powder make a thin batter that is cooked on one side.

Authentic touch. Do not flip them. When the top is set and full of holes, the ploye is done. A flip dries it into a cracker.

How it is used

Butter, maple syrup, or a savory spread. They can be wrapped around a filling like a soft bread.

Field notes

  • Ployes are cooked on one side. Holes on top are the sign they are ready.
  • They belong to the Acadian north, not to the Cajun rice kitchen, though the histories of the people are connected.

Ingredients

Steps

  4. Cook until the top is set and the underside is spotted brown. Do not flip.
  5. Stack and cover with a towel so they stay flexible.
  6. Serve with butter.

Canadian Bannock

Mild baking-powder bread, browned in a skillet, soft inside.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · one 8-inch round

History

Bannock in Scotland was an older griddle or hearth bread, often oat or barley. Fur traders and settlers carried a wheat, baking-powder version across Canada. Many Indigenous nations adopted and adapted bannock, and it is now a daily and ceremonial bread in a wide range of communities, with no single Indigenous recipe. This page gives a simple skillet version and does not assign it to one nation or pretend it is pre-contact.

Cultural background

It can be baked, pan-fried, or cooked on a stick over a fire. The skillet cake is the one that belongs among griddle breads. Toppings range from butter and jam to stew.

Why it tastes this way

Baking powder and white flour were trade and ration ingredients that did not need a sourdough crock or yeast, which is why the bread spread. It tastes plain and slightly toasted because there is little in it besides flour, fat, and salt. Indigenous cooks made it their own in different ways. A single written recipe cannot stand in for all of those.

How it is made

Flour, baking powder, salt, fat, and water are mixed, patted into a round, and cooked in a greased skillet, turned once.

Authentic touch. Keep the dough soft. A stiff dough fries into a hard biscuit. Pat it thicker than a crepe and cook it covered for a minute if the center is slow.

How it is used

Butter and jam, or beside stew. Berries are a fair seasonal side.

Field notes

  • There is no single Indigenous bannock. Nations and families make it differently.
  • The wheat baking-powder bread spread through trade. It should not be described as unchanged ancient bread.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons cold butter or oil

Steps

  2. Add water and mix to a soft dough.
  3. Pat into a round about 1/2 inch thick.
  4. Cook in a lightly greased covered skillet over medium-low, about 8 minutes.
  5. Turn and cook until the center is set.
  6. Cool a few minutes, then slice.

History

In Ireland, Shrove Tuesday is Pancake Tuesday, the day before Lent. Households used up eggs, milk, and butter in thin pancakes before the fast. Cultural background

Families still cook a stack and argue over who flips. It is a one-day ritual that many people also cook on ordinary nights because the batter is simple.

Why it tastes this way

The taste is egg, butter, and lemon because those were the rich foods to finish before Lent, not because the pancake needed a special spice. A thin batter cooks fast and takes a sharp squeeze of lemon, which cuts the butter. The religious calendar, more than the climate, is why the cake has a date attached.

How it is made

A thin batter of flour, egg, and milk is cooked in a small skillet and finished with lemon and sugar.

Authentic touch. The pan should be hot and only filmed with butter. A thick pool of batter becomes a different, heavier cake and will not toss.

How it is used

Lemon juice and sugar. A few cooks use jam or a chocolate spread. Lemon and sugar are the Shrove plate.

Field notes

  • Pancake Tuesday in Ireland is Shrove Tuesday, the day before Ash Wednesday.

Boxty

Half grated raw potato, half mashed potato, browned in a pan.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 cakes

History

Boxty is an Irish potato bread especially associated with Ulster, Connacht, and the northwest. A traditional rhyme says boxty on the griddle and boxty in the pan. There are pan cakes, baked loaves, and boiled dumplings. This page is the pan cake: raw grated potato and mashed potato together. Potatoes became a central Irish food in the eighteenth century, and boxty is one of the breads that came out of that dependence, including through the years around the Famine.

Cultural background

It is a meal with butter, or with bacon and eggs. The raw potato keeps it from tasting like a leftover-mash patty.

Why it tastes this way

Grated raw potato brings water and a clean starch that crisps. Mashed potato brings a soft, cooked sweetness. Together they taste more potato than a flour pancake and more tender than a latke made only of shreds. The wheat flour is only a binder. Ireland’s cool, wet climate favored the potato as a staple, which is why a bread was built from it.

How it is made

Equal parts squeezed raw potato and mashed potato are mixed with a little flour, egg, and milk, then fried as cakes.

Authentic touch. Squeeze the grated potato dry, and keep the starch that settles from the liquid. Stir the batter just before frying. It darkens if it waits.

How it is used

Butter, or beside bacon and eggs. Some cooks serve it with a fried egg on top.

Field notes

  • Pan, griddle, and boiled boxty are three methods. This recipe is the pan cake.
  • Use a floury potato for the mash and a dry grate. A waxy potato stays gluey.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups grated raw potato, squeezed dry
  • 1 cup cold mashed potato
  • 1/2 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 large egg
  • 1/4 cup milk
  • 3/4 teaspoon salt
  • Butter or oil for frying

Steps

  1. Squeeze the grated potato in a towel. Add back any settled starch.
  2. Mix with mash, flour, egg, milk, and salt.

Crumpets

Spongy, salty, and full of holes, made to be toasted and buttered.

1 hr 20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 crumpets

History

Crumpets are English griddle cakes cooked in rings so the batter stays tall and forms holes on top. They are related to earlier muffins and pikelets. The modern crumpet, with its yeast or leavener and its tea-table role, settled in the nineteenth century as a bakery and home cake. You toast them. You do not eat them as a floppy pancake straight from the first cook, though a cook may sneak one.

Cultural background

Butter is the topping that fills the holes. Cheese, or butter and jam, are the other honest uses. They are toast more than they are a stack.

Why it tastes this way

A wet, yeasted batter in a hot ring sets at the bottom while bubbles burst on top and leave holes. Those holes are the point, because melted butter runs into them. Baking soda at the end gives a last burst of bubbles. The taste is wheat and salt, not sugar. Sugar would darken the holes and turn them into a cake.

How it is made

A yeast batter is thinned, finished with baking soda, and cooked in greased rings on a griddle until the top is full of holes.

Authentic touch. The batter must be loose, almost pourable. If it is thick, you get a pikelet with no holes. Cook on medium-low so the bottom does not burn before the top sets.

How it is used

Toasted, with butter. They should be heated through so the butter melts.

Field notes

  • Holes are the test of a crumpet. A smooth top means the batter was too thick or the pan too cool.
  • They are meant to be toasted, not only eaten soft from the first griddle.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup warm milk
  • 1/2 cup warm water
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 2 tablespoons warm water, for the soda

Steps

  1. Stir flour, yeast, sugar, salt, milk, and 1/2 cup water into a thick batter. Cover 1 hour, until bubbly.
  2. Dissolve the soda in 2 tablespoons water and stir it in. The batter should be pourable.
  3. Grease crumpet rings and a medium-low griddle.
  4. Fill each ring halfway. Cook until the top is set and holey.
  5. Remove the rings, flip, and brown the top briefly.
  6. Toast before serving with butter.

Variations

Yorkshire Pudding

Crisp, eggy, and hollow, meant to catch gravy.

15 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 puddings

History

Yorkshire pudding is the baked batter of an English roast dinner, known in print by the eighteenth century. It was a way to make a roast feed more people: batter cooked under the meat, or in the drippings, so it puffed and took up the fat. Modern kitchens often bake it in a separate tin with oil or drippings. It is a cousin of the popover and of the Dutch baby, and it is not a sweet pancake.

Cultural background

Sunday roast beef with pudding, potatoes, and greens is the plate most people mean. The pudding can be one large tray or individual cups. Toad in the hole bakes sausages in the same batter and is a variation, not the classic pudding.

Why it tastes this way

Hot fat and a wet batter make steam, and the steam puffs the pudding before the egg and flour set. Beef drippings taste meaty. This version uses oil so the pudding itself is vegetarian, with a note that drippings are the roast-dinner fat. The flavor is mostly browned batter and salt, which is why gravy is served with it. The original economy was stretching a joint of meat.

How it is made

A thin batter of egg, milk, and flour is poured into preheated oil and baked until puffed.

Authentic touch. The tin and the fat must be smoking hot. Cold fat makes a flabby disk. Do not open the oven while it rises.

How it is used

With roast beef and gravy, or as a first course with gravy, which is an older way of serving it. Onion gravy is a fair vegetarian stand-in and is a separate recipe.

Field notes

  • Yorkshire pudding is a batter pudding, included here as the roast-dinner cousin of oven pancakes.
  • Hot fat is not optional. It is the reason the pudding rises.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 2 large eggs
  • 1 cup milk
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 3 tablespoons neutral oil or beef drippings

Steps

  2. Heat the oven to 425°F. Divide the oil among a muffin tin and heat it until very hot.
  3. Pour in the batter, filling the cups about halfway.
  4. Bake about 20 to 25 minutes until puffed and brown.
  5. Do not open the door early.
  6. Serve at once with gravy.

  • Individual cups are the modern Sunday shape. A single tin is the older tray.
  • Toad in the hole adds sausages and is then not vegetarian.

History

A thick sweetened batter is dropped by the spoon onto a warm griddle.

How it is used

Butter and jam, or butter and golden syrup.

History

Welsh cakes, picau ar y maen, are small rounds of a currant dough cooked on a bakestone or griddle. They are a tea cake, not a poured pancake, and they are one of the best-known Welsh griddle foods. The bakestone was a flat iron used on the hearth. Currants and a little spice are typical.

Cultural background

They are eaten plain or with butter, warm or cold, and they keep better than a crepe. Bake sales and family tins both rely on that.

How it is made

A short dough with currants is rolled, cut into rounds, and cooked on a griddle.

How it is used

Warm, with or without butter, with tea.

  • Picau ar y maen means cakes on the stone, the bakestone.

History

Injera is the fermented teff flatbread of Ethiopia, and a close relative is eaten in Eritrea. Teff is a tiny grain domesticated in the highlands. The batter ferments for days, then is poured on a covered mitad, a clay griddle, and cooked on one side until it is spongy with holes. It is the plate and the utensil for stews. This recipe uses 100 percent teff so it is gluten-free. Many restaurant injeras mix in wheat or barley, which changes the taste and the gluten status.

Cultural background

A meal is injera with several stews, wat, shared from one platter. The sourness is desired. A sweet injera is a different, less typical bread.

Why it tastes this way

Teff grows in the highlands where other grains struggle, which is why the daily bread tastes of teff rather than of wheat. Fermentation makes the sourness and the bubbles. Cooking on one side, covered, steams the top into a sponge instead of a cracker. The eyes, the holes, hold the stew. Climate, grain, and fermentation are the whole flavor.

How it is made

Teff flour and water ferment, then the batter is cooked like a large pancake on one side in a covered pan.

Authentic touch. Ferment until the batter smells sour and looks bubbly, usually one to three days in a warm kitchen. Pour in a spiral on a hot nonstick pan, cover, and do not flip. If it browns black, the pan is too hot.

How it is used

Under and with stews, or rolled up beside them. It is not a syrup bread.

Field notes

  • One hundred percent teff injera is gluten-free. Many restaurant versions add other grains.
  • It is cooked on one side. The holes are the sign it worked.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups teff flour
  • 3 cups water, plus more as needed
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Stir teff and water into a thin batter. Cover loosely and ferment 1 to 3 days until sour and bubbly.

  3. Heat a nonstick skillet, wipe it with a drop of oil, and pour a thin round.
  4. Cover and cook until the top is set and full of holes, about 2 minutes. Do not flip.
  5. Cool on a cloth.
  6. Stack and serve with stew.

Ferment in a clean container. If the batter smells rotten or grows mold, discard it. Sour and yeasty is the goal.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 crepes

History

Pesarattu is an Andhra crepe made from whole green gram, moong, often with ginger and green chile. It is frequently served with upma, a semolina porridge, as pesarattu-upma. The crepe itself is the page. Upma is a partner and contains wheat. The pesarattu batter does not.

Cultural background

It is a breakfast crepe, thinner than a cheela and more specific than a generic moong pancake. Ginger is not optional if you want it to taste like pesarattu.

Why it tastes this way

Green gram tastes earthy and slightly sweet, different from urad dal in a classic dosa. Ginger and green chile cut that earthiness. There is often no long ferment, or only a short one, so the flavor stays beany rather than sour. Andhra breakfasts pair it with upma for a soft, salty contrast. The crepe is the protein. The upma is the wheat side.

How it is made

Soaked green gram is ground with ginger and chile and cooked as thin crepes.

Authentic touch. Grind the soaked moong very smooth with ginger. Cook it thinner than a cheela and oil the edges so it crisps. Do not expect dosa sourness.

How it is used

With ginger chutney, or with upma if you want the classic Andhra plate. Upma is a separate wheat dish.

Field notes

  • Pesarattu is green gram, an Andhra breakfast crepe.
  • The crepe is gluten-free. Upma beside it is not.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup whole green gram (moong), soaked 4 hours and drained
  • 1 inch ginger
  • 1 green chile
  • 1/2 teaspoon cumin
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Water, to grind
  • Oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Grind dal, ginger, chile, cumin, salt, and enough water to make a spreadable batter.
  2. Cook thin crepes on a hot oiled tawa until the edges lift and the underside browns.
  3. Flip briefly.
  4. Serve warm.
  5. The batter should be smooth.
  6. Ginger should be visible as a flavor, not a hint.

Adai

A thick, savory pancake of mixed dals and rice, spiced with chile and curry leaf.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 adai

History

Adai is a Tamil pancake of rice and several lentils, coarser and spicier than dosa, often cooked for dinner as much as breakfast. A common accompaniment is jaggery and butter, or avial, a vegetable stew. The batter is ground and may rest a few hours. It is not a thin crisp dosa.

Cultural background

The mix of dals is the point: toor, chana, and moong with a little urad, or a similar house blend. Curry leaves and dried chile go into the grind.

Why it tastes this way

Several lentils make it taste beany, hearty, and more savory than a rice-dominant dosa. Chile and curry leaf are ground in, so the heat is in the cake, not only in a side chutney. A coarser grind keeps it textured. Jaggery on the side is a Tamil contrast, sweet against a spicy pancake, and it surprises people who expect only chutney.

How it is made

Soaked rice and mixed dals are ground coarse with chile and curry leaves and cooked as thick pancakes.

Authentic touch. Grind it coarse, like wet sand, not a smooth dosa cream. Cook it thicker, with oil, until the center is no longer raw. A thin adai dries out and misses the point.

How it is used

With jaggery, or with a vegetable stew, and a spoon of yogurt if you eat dairy.

Field notes

  • Adai is thicker and spicier than dosa, and it uses several dals.
  • Jaggery on the side is a real way to eat it.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup rice
  • 2 tablespoons toor dal
  • 2 tablespoons chana dal
  • 2 tablespoons moong dal
  • 1 tablespoon urad dal
  • 2 dried red chiles
  • 8 curry leaves
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Soak the rice and dals together 4 hours. Drain.
  2. Grind coarse with chiles, curry leaves, salt, and a little water.
  3. Rest 2 hours if you can. It is good the same day.
  4. Spread thick rounds on a hot oiled tawa and cook, turning, until both sides are browned and the center is cooked.
  5. Serve with jaggery or a stew.
  6. Oil the edges so they crisp.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A smoother grind makes it more like dosa and less like adai.
  • Fewer chiles make it milder. The classic is noticeably spiced.

Kerala Appam

A bowl-shaped rice pancake, lacy at the rim and soft in the center.

40 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 appams

History

Appam, or palappam, is a Kerala rice pancake cooked in a small rounded pan, an appachatti. The edges are thin and lacy. The center is thick and soft. Fermented rice batter, often with coconut, is traditional. Yeast or toddy can start the ferment. This home version uses a pinch of yeast and coconut milk, and it notes that toddy is the older starter where it is used.

Cultural background

Appam is eaten with stew, egg roast, or sweetened coconut milk. The shape is the dish. A flat dosa pan will not make a bowl.

Why it tastes this way

A rounded pan sends the thin batter up the sides, where it crisps, and leaves a custardy center of rice and coconut. Coconut is Kerala’s fat and sweetness. Fermentation, whether from toddy or yeast, adds a light sourness and the lift. The result tastes of rice and coconut, not of wheat, which is why it matches a coconut-milk stew.

How it is made

Fermented rice and coconut batter is swirled in a rounded pan and cooked covered.

Authentic touch. Swirl the batter in a hot, oiled appam pan and let the excess sit in the middle. Cover it. Do not flip. A wok is a poor substitute that can work if you treat it gently.

How it is used

With vegetable stew or sweet coconut milk. An egg cooked in the center is a common variation and adds egg.

Field notes

  • The bowl shape comes from the pan and the swirl.
  • Toddy is traditional. Yeast is the home starter written here.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup rice, soaked 4 hours
  • 1/2 cup cooked rice
  • 1/2 cup coconut milk
  • 1/4 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1/2 teaspoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Grind soaked rice and cooked rice with a little water until smooth.
  2. Stir in coconut milk, yeast, sugar, and salt. Ferment 6 to 12 hours until bubbly.
  3. Heat an oiled appam pan.
  4. Pour a ladle, swirl to coat the sides, and let the rest pool in the center.
  5. Cover and cook until the center is set and the rim is lacy. Do not flip.
  6. Serve with stew.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Sri Lankan hoppers are the island cousin, often with an egg.
  • Kallappam is a toddy-fermented cousin.

Besan Cheela

History

Besan cheela, or chilla, is a North Indian savory pancake of chickpea flour, water, onion, and spices, cooked on a tawa. It is a fast breakfast, related to Gujarati pudla. It does not need a ferment. Besan is the flour left when chana dal is ground, and it is a pantry staple.

Cultural background

It is eaten with chutney, pickle, or yogurt. The pancake is vegan as written. Yogurt on the side adds dairy.

Why it tastes this way

Chickpea flour tastes nutty and binds with water alone, so the cake needs no egg or wheat. Ajwain or cumin, chile, and onion make it savory rather than flat. It is a breakfast of the chickpea-growing north, quick because there is no ferment. The gram flavor is the difference from a rice dosa.

How it is made

Besan, water, onion, and spices are mixed into a batter and cooked as pancakes.

Authentic touch. Rest the batter 10 minutes so the besan hydrates, or the center tastes raw. Cook it on medium. Besan burns at the edge before the middle sets if the pan is screaming.

How it is used

With green chutney or pickle.

Field notes

  • Besan cheela is an instant savory pancake. It is not fermented.
  • Chickpea flour keeps it gluten-free if the besan is pure.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup besan (chickpea flour)
  • 3/4 cup water
  • 1 small onion, finely chopped
  • 1 green chile, minced
  • 1/2 teaspoon cumin
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons chopped cilantro
  • Oil

Steps

Malpua

A fried sweet pancake soaked in sugar syrup, scented with fennel.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 5 · about 10 malpuas

History

Malpua is a fried sweet pancake of the Indian subcontinent, eaten at Holi and other festivals and also as a dessert. Flour, milk, and fennel are a common batter. The cakes are fried and soaked in syrup. Bengali and North Indian versions differ in richness. This is a home syrup malpua, not a restaurant cream one.

Cultural background

They should be eaten the day they are made. The syrup keeps them soft. Fennel is the spice that makes them taste like malpua rather than a plain fritter.

Why it tastes this way

Fennel and cardamom in a milk batter, fried and then bathed in syrup, taste floral and rich because the sugar cooks into the crumb. Festival sweets use ghee or oil and a syrup because the occasion calls for something lavish. Milk is the dairy. The fry is why the edge is crisper than a baked pancake before the syrup softens it.

How it is made

A milk batter with fennel is fried as small pancakes and dipped in sugar syrup.

Authentic touch. Fry at a moderate heat so the center cooks before the sugar in the batter burns. Soak briefly. A long soak makes them heavy. Fennel should be noticeable.

How it is used

Warm or at room temperature, as a sweet.

Field notes

  • Malpua is a festival sweet, including Holi, and also an ordinary dessert.
  • Fennel is the spice to taste.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup flour
  • 1 cup milk
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon fennel seeds, crushed
  • 1/4 teaspoon cardamom
  • Pinch of salt
  • Oil for frying
  • 1/2 cup sugar and 1/2 cup water, for syrup

Steps

  1. Whisk flour, milk, 2 tablespoons sugar, fennel, cardamom, and salt into a thick batter.
  2. Simmer 1/2 cup sugar and 1/2 cup water until slightly syrupy.
  3. Fry small pancakes in a shallow layer of oil over medium until cooked through.
  4. Dip each in the warm syrup for a few seconds.
  5. Serve warm.
  6. Do not crowd the pan.

Fry over steady heat.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

Thin rice-and-wheat crepes are filled with coconut cooked in jaggery and rolled.

Sri Lankan Hoppers

History

Hoppers, aappa, are Sri Lanka’s bowl-shaped fermented rice pancakes, close kin to Kerala appam. Coconut milk is usual. Hoppers are eaten with sambol and curries. The center is soft. The rim is crisp. Yeast or a ferment starts the batter.

Coconut milk and a curved pan are the flavor and the shape. The rim crisps because it is thin. The center stays creamy because the batter pools. Fermented rice and coconut batter is swirled in a hopper pan and cooked covered.

With sambol or curry. An egg in the center is the variation.

Pajeon

A scallion pancake, crisp at the edge and full of green onion.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 2 pancakes

History

Pajeon is a Korean scallion pancake. Pa is scallion, jeon is a pancake or fritter. It is rainy-day food in a popular saying, and it is also restaurant and home food any day, served with a dipping sauce of soy, vinegar, and chile. This batter is flour, water, and egg, with enough scallions that they are the body of the cake.

Cultural background

Haemul pajeon adds seafood and is a different page. Kimchi jeon is another. Plain pajeon can be vegetarian if the dipping sauce has no fish sauce. This recipe uses soy and vinegar.

Why it tastes this way

Scallions taste sweet when they fry and sharp when they are raw, so a pancake that is mostly scallion tastes green and browned at once. A thin batter only binds them. The crisp edge comes from oil and a hot pan, not from baking powder. Vinegar in the sauce is the acid that makes the fry make sense. Rainy-day custom is culture. The cake is still the same on a dry day.

How it is made

A thin batter is mixed with a mass of scallions and fried in oil until crisp, then cut.

Authentic touch. Use a lot of scallions, cut on a long bias, and press them into the batter so they touch the pan. Do not stack the cakes. The crust dies under steam.

How it is used

Cut into wedges, with a soy-vinegar dipping sauce.

Field notes

  • Pajeon is the scallion pancake. Jeon is the broader category.
  • A dipping sauce of soy and vinegar is the usual partner. This one has no fish sauce.

Ingredients

  • 3/4 cup cold water
  • 1 egg
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

  5. Mix soy, vinegar, and sugar for the sauce.
  6. Cut into wedges and serve with the sauce.

Chopped sour kimchi is mixed into a flour-and-egg batter and fried as pancakes.

With a light soy-vinegar dip, or on its own if the kimchi is already salty.

History

Hotteok is a Korean street pancake: a yeasted dough filled with brown sugar and cinnamon, pressed flat on a griddle so the sugar melts. Seeds or nuts are common in the filling. Yeast dough tastes bready, and the sugar melts into a syrup inside because the cake is pressed on a hot, oiled griddle. Cinnamon is the perfume. How it is made

A yeasted dough is filled with brown sugar and cinnamon, then cooked and pressed on a griddle.

Immediately, in a napkin.

History

Okonomiyaki is a savory Japanese pancake. In Osaka it is mixed: cabbage, batter, and other ingredients cooked together as one cake. The name is often explained as “grilled as you like it.” Hiroshima’s version is layered and includes noodles, and it has its own page. The Osaka cake is the thick mixed one. Bonito flakes and a sweet-savory sauce are the usual finish.

Cultural background

It is restaurant food and home food. Pork is common and is left out of this recipe so the written cake has no meat. The fish is the bonito and the dashi.

Why it tastes this way

Cabbage wilts into the batter and tastes sweet, which is why the pancake is mostly vegetable and still rich. Dashi, made here with instant bonito stock, is the sea savor. The brown sauce is sweet and salty, a cousin of Worcestershire-style sauces adapted in Japan. Mayonnaise is the cool fat. The griddle browns the flour. Osaka mixes everything. That mix is the regional method.

How it is made

Cabbage is stirred into an egg batter and cooked as a thick pancake, then topped with sauce, mayonnaise, and bonito.

Authentic touch. Use more cabbage than you think, and do not press the cake into a thin crepe. Cook it covered so the cabbage softens before the crust burns. Flip with confidence, or use two spatulas.

How it is used

Cut, on the griddle or a plate, immediately so the bonito still moves in the heat.

Field notes

  • Osaka okonomiyaki is mixed. Hiroshima okonomiyaki is layered.
  • Bonito flakes are fish. A kombu dashi and no bonito would be a different, meatless-sea version.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups finely shredded cabbage
  • 1 cup flour
  • 2 eggs
  • 3/4 cup dashi or water plus 1 teaspoon instant dashi
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • Okonomiyaki sauce or a mix of ketchup and Worcestershire
  • Mayonnaise
  • Bonito flakes

Steps

  1. Stir flour, eggs, dashi, and salt. Fold in the cabbage.
  2. Oil a skillet and spread half the batter into a thick cake.
  3. Cook covered on medium until the bottom browns and the cabbage softens, then flip.
  4. Cook until the center is set.
  5. Spread sauce and mayonnaise. Top with bonito.
  6. Cut and serve.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pork slices on the cake are a common addition and add meat.
  • Hiroshima style is layered with noodles.

Hiroshima Okonomiyaki

A layered savory pancake: crepe, cabbage, noodles, and egg.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 2 · 2 layered pancakes

History

Hiroshima okonomiyaki is built in layers on the griddle: a thin crepe, a large pile of cabbage, noodles, often pork, and an egg, then flipped together and sauced. It is the city’s version, distinct from Osaka’s mixed batter. This home version skips the pork and keeps the layers, including yakisoba-style noodles.

Cultural background

The cabbage pile looks excessive and cooks down. The crepe is only a base. Noodles are what make a visitor recognize the Hiroshima plate.

Why it tastes this way

Layering keeps the cabbage sweeter and fresher-tasting than a fully mixed batter, and the noodles make it a full meal. The same sauce and bonito finish tie it to other okonomiyaki. Hiroshima’s shops specialize in the stack. The method is the local identity. Pork is common and omitted here so the allergens stay with egg, wheat, fish, and soy.

How it is made

A thin crepe is topped with cabbage, then noodles and egg, flipped, and sauced.

Authentic touch. Cook the crepe thin, pile the cabbage and cover it until it wilts, then add noodles and egg. Flip the whole stack onto the egg. Do not stir it into one batter.

How it is used

With okonomiyaki sauce, mayonnaise, and bonito flakes.

Field notes

  • Hiroshima okonomiyaki is defined by layers and usually by noodles.
  • The cabbage pile shrinks. Start with more than looks reasonable.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup flour
  • 1 egg, plus 2 eggs for topping
  • 1/3 cup water
  • 4 cups shredded cabbage
  • 2 cups cooked noodles
  • Oil
  • Okonomiyaki sauce
  • Mayonnaise
  • Bonito flakes
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Make a thin batter with flour, 1 egg, water, and a pinch of salt. Cook a thin crepe.
  2. Pile half the cabbage on it, cover, and cook until wilted.
  3. Add half the noodles. Crack an egg onto a clear spot of the griddle, break the yolk, and flip the stack onto the egg.
  4. Cook until the egg sets. Repeat for the second cake.
  5. Sauce, mayonnaise, and bonito.
  6. Serve at once.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pork slices are the usual market addition.
  • Osaka style mixes the batter instead of layering.

Dorayaki

Two small honey-sweet pancakes sandwiched with sweet red bean paste.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 sandwiches

History

Dorayaki are two small cast-pan pancakes filled with anko, sweet red bean paste. They are a wagashi, a Japanese sweet, sold in shops and made at home. The pancakes are honey-sweetened and cakier than a crepe. The bean paste is the filling that makes them dorayaki rather than a plain hotcake.

Cultural background

Tsubuan is chunky bean paste. Koshian is smooth. Either is used. This recipe uses smooth canned or homemade paste and does not pretend a chocolate spread is the same sweet.

Why it tastes this way

Honey in the batter browns and perfumes the crumb. Anko is sweetened azuki beans, earthy under the sugar, a sweet that predates imported chocolate in Japanese confectionery. The sandwich shape keeps the paste from burning on the pan. The cakes are small because they are a sweet, not a breakfast stack.

How it is made

A honey batter is cooked as small thick cakes and sandwiched with red bean paste.

Authentic touch. Cook the cakes on low heat so the honey does not scorch, and sandwich them while they are still slightly warm so they stick to the paste. Cool them in pairs.

How it is used

At room temperature or slightly warm, as a sweet.

Field notes

  • Anko, red bean paste, is the classic filling.
  • The cakes are honey pancakes, not American hotcakes.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup flour
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking powder
  • 2 tablespoons honey
  • 2 eggs
  • 1/4 cup water
  • 1/2 cup smooth red bean paste
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Whisk flour, baking powder, honey, eggs, and water into a thick batter.
  2. Cook small rounds on a lightly oiled medium-low pan until both sides are brown and the centers are set.
  3. Cool one minute.
  4. Sandwich pairs with red bean paste.
  5. Serve the same day.
  6. Keep the heat low so the honey does not scorch.

Jianbing

A crisp savory crepe with egg, scallion, sauce, and a cracker.

15 min prep · 10 min cook · Serves 2 · 2 large wraps

History

Jianbing is a northern Chinese street breakfast. A thin batter, often mung-bean or wheat, is cooked on a round griddle, brushed with egg, and topped with scallion, cilantro, fermented bean sauce or hoisin, chile, and often a crispy cracker, the guozi, or a fried dough stick. Tianjin and Shandong both have strong claims. This is a home wheat-and-egg version with bean sauce and a store-bought cracker.

Cultural background

It is wrapped and eaten on the way to work. The sauce is savory and slightly sweet. The herbs are raw. The crepe is crisp at the edge.

Why it tastes this way

Mung-bean batter, where it is used, tastes cleaner and crisper than wheat. Wheat is the easier home flour and is what this recipe uses, so the page does not pretend otherwise. Egg fries onto the crepe. Fermented bean sauce is the deep savory note, a northern pantry flavor. Cilantro and scallion are fresh against that. The cracker is there for noise and texture. Street griddles run very hot, which is why the edge shatters.

How it is made

A thin batter is cooked, topped with egg, sauce, herbs, and a cracker, then folded.

Authentic touch. Spread the batter paper-thin on a hot pan and crack the egg on immediately so it bonds. Wrap it while it is still flexible enough to fold and crisp enough to crackle.

How it is used

Wrapped, hot, as breakfast.

Field notes

  • Jianbing is a northern street breakfast. Tianjin and Shandong are the places most often named.
  • The cracker is traditional texture. The crepe and egg are the meal if you cannot find one.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup flour
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 2 eggs
  • 2 tablespoons fermented bean sauce or hoisin
  • 2 scallions, sliced
  • 2 tablespoons chopped cilantro
  • Chile sauce, to taste
  • 2 crackers or a piece of fried dough
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Whisk flour and water into a thin batter.
  2. Oil a hot skillet and pour a paper-thin crepe.
  3. Beat an egg and spread it on the crepe. Cook until just set.
  4. Spread sauce, scallion, cilantro, and a little chile. Press on a cracker.
  5. Fold and serve.
  6. Repeat.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A mung-bean flour batter is closer to some Tianjin stalls and can be gluten-free if the sauce is too.
  • Hoisin instead of plain bean sauce is sweeter.

Scallion Pancake

A flaky pan-fried bread layered with scallion and oil.

40 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 pancakes

History

Scallion pancakes, cong you bing, are a Chinese pan bread: a dough rolled with oil and scallions into a coil, then fried until flaky. They are eaten across China and in Taiwan as a snack and a side. They are not a batter pancake. They are included because they are a griddled bread people reasonably meet next to jianbing.

Cultural background

The flake comes from oil and the coil, the same idea as other coiled flatbreads in this journal, with scallion as the flavor. A dipping sauce of soy and vinegar is common.

Why it tastes this way

Hot oil between thin layers of dough separates into flakes, and scallions steam and sweeten inside. Wheat dough is the structure. The pan fry, rather than an oven, browns the face. Salt and sesame oil, if you use a few drops, make it savory. It is a wheat snack of the north and of teahouses, not a sweet cake.

How it is made

A soft dough is coiled with oil and scallions and pan-fried.

Authentic touch. Roll, oil, sprinkle scallion and salt, coil, and roll again. Fry on medium so the inside cooks before the outside is black. A high heat only burns the scallion.

How it is used

Cut into wedges, with a soy-vinegar dip.

Field notes

  • Cong you bing is a laminated pan bread, not a poured batter.
  • The coil is how the layers are made.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups flour
  • 3/4 cup hot water
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 3 tablespoons oil, plus more for the pan
  • 6 scallions, chopped
  • Soy sauce and vinegar, to dip

Steps

  1. Mix flour and hot water into a dough. Rest 20 minutes.
  2. Roll each piece, brush with oil, sprinkle scallion and salt, and roll into a coil. Flatten.
  3. Fry on a medium oiled pan until both sides are golden and the center is cooked.
  4. Cut and serve.
  5. The layers should separate when torn.
  6. Dip in soy and vinegar.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A cracked egg on the frying pancake is a breakfast variation.
  • More oil means more flake and a heavier bread.

Mandarin Pancakes

Thin, steamed-then-pan wheat pancakes for wrapping Peking duck or stir-fries.

30 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 pancakes

History

Mandarin pancakes, or duck pancakes, are thin wheat rounds used to wrap Peking duck with scallion and hoisin. A related spring pancake, chunbing, is eaten at the start of spring with vegetables. The two are often described as relatives. This recipe is the thin wrapper. It does not include a whole roast duck. The pancake is cooked in pairs so the faces stay soft.

Cultural background

They should be flexible, not brown and crisp. Steam or a dry pan with a cover finishes them. Restaurants buy them. Home cooks roll them.

Why it tastes this way

A hot-water dough stays soft, which is why the wrapper bends around crisp duck skin instead of cracking. Cooking two rounds together and peeling them apart makes one side especially tender. The pancake is almost tasteless on purpose. Hoisin, scallion, and cucumber, or duck, are the flavor. Spring pancakes use the same idea for a seasonal vegetable meal.

How it is made

A hot-water dough is rolled into thin rounds, cooked briefly in pairs, and peeled apart.

Authentic touch. Roll them thin and do not brown them hard. Keep the stack under a towel. If they dry out, a few seconds in a covered steamer brings them back.

How it is used

Warm, as wrappers. Hoisin, scallion, and cucumber are the classic duck accompaniments and are not all in the dough.

Field notes

  • These are wrappers, not a sweet or savory filled cake.
  • Cooking them in pairs is the usual way to keep a tender side.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup flour
  • 1/2 cup boiling water
  • 1 teaspoon oil
  • Pinch of salt

Steps

  1. Stir flour, salt, and boiling water into a dough. Add the oil. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Divide into 8, pair them up with a drop of oil between, and roll each pair thin.
  3. Cook on a dry medium pan until the surface looks dry and a few spots appear, under a minute a side.
  4. Peel the pairs apart.
  5. Stack under a towel.
  6. Serve warm as wrappers.

History

Bánh xèo is the sizzling rice-flour crepe of Vietnam, named for the sound of batter on a hot pan. Turmeric colors it. How it is made

A thin rice and coconut batter is cooked crisp, filled with shrimp and sprouts, and folded.

With lettuce, mint, and a dipping sauce of lime, fish sauce, and chile. Fish sauce adds fish. A soy-lime dip is the fish-free option and should be called that.

Field notes

Two coconut-rice batters are cooked in a well pan until the rims brown and the centers set.

Warm, in pairs.

An oiled dough is rested, stretched, folded, and cooked on a griddle.

With dal or a simple curry.

History

Pupusas are El Salvador’s stuffed corn cakes, a national dish also loved in Honduras. Masa is filled with cheese, beans, or chicharrón, sealed, and cooked on a comal. Curtido, a lightly fermented cabbage slaw, and a tomato salsa are the usual sides. This recipe is the cheese pupusa. Beans are the vegan variation if you omit cheese. Chicharrón adds pork.

Cultural background

They are supper and street food. The masa is closed around the filling so nothing leaks. Curtido’s tang is part of the plate.

Why it tastes this way

A thick masa cake steamed on a comal tastes of toasted corn and melted cheese, salty against the sour cabbage. Nixtamal is the Indigenous maize technique under a later national dish. Curtido is quick-pickled, which is why it tastes bright and cuts the fat of the cheese. The comal, not a fryer, is the traditional heat.

How it is made

Masa is stuffed with cheese, flattened, and cooked on a comal.

Authentic touch. Keep a little masa in your palm, cup it, add a small amount of cheese, and close it. Flatten gently. If the cheese leaks, the cake was too thin or the heat too low to set the crust.

How it is used

Hot, with curtido and salsa.

Field notes

  • Pupusas are El Salvador’s stuffed corn cakes.
  • Curtido is the cabbage relish that belongs on the plate.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups masa harina
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 1/2 cups warm water
  • 1 cup grated quesillo or mozzarella

Steps

  1. Mix a soft masa.
  2. Cup a ball of masa, add a little cheese, close, and flatten gently.
  3. Cook on a medium comal until both sides are spotted and the center is hot.
  4. Serve with curtido and salsa.
  5. Do not overfill.
  6. Keep cooked pupusas covered.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Bean pupusas can be vegan if the beans are.
  • Loroco, an edible flower bud, is a traditional addition with the cheese when you can get it.

Australian Pikelets

Small thick drop scones, buttered, a tea-table cake.

10 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · about 12 pikelets

History

Australian pikelets are small thick pancakes in the British drop-scone family, a standard in older home-economics cooking and at tea. They are not crumpets, though both get butter. Golden syrup or jam is the finish. They are the settler British cake in Australian homes, not an Aboriginal bread.

Cultural background

They are cooked by the spoonful and eaten warm. Cream is a generous addition. The batter is close to Scotch pancakes.

Why it tastes this way

A thick egg batter on a moderate girdle makes a soft, lightly sweet cake, the tea food British cooks brought and kept. Golden syrup, a cane invert syrup, is the colonial pantry sweet that stuck in Australia and New Zealand. The cake tastes of butter and egg. Jam is the fruit. It is a borrowed recipe that became local afternoon tea.

How it is made

A thick batter is dropped onto a griddle and served with butter and jam or golden syrup.

Authentic touch. Drop them small. Flip when the bubbles set. Serve warm so the butter melts. They dry out if they are overcooked.

How it is used

Warm, with butter.

Field notes

  • Australian pikelets are drop scones for tea.
  • They are not crumpets and not an Aboriginal bread.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1 egg
  • 1/2 cup milk
  • 1 tablespoon melted butter

Steps

  1. Mix a thick batter.
  2. Drop small rounds on a buttered medium griddle.
  3. Flip when the bubbles hold.
  4. Serve warm with butter and jam or golden syrup.
  5. Do not cook them large.
  6. They dry out if overcooked.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • New Zealand pikelets are the close cousin.
  • Crumpets are the holey yeast cakes and are not these.
