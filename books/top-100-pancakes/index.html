Southern buttermilk pancakes

Tangy buttermilk, a little sugar, and a soft crumb with a browned rim.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 pancakes

History

Southern buttermilk pancakes are thick wheat cakes that became a regional breakfast after chemical leaveners and readily available dairy were both in the kitchen. The acid in buttermilk reacts with baking soda and keeps the crumb tender. Atlanta is only the map pin: the same stack is a home and diner breakfast across the South. Butter and a pour of cane syrup or sorghum usually finish the plate.

Cultural background

This is the later wheat stack, separate from cornmeal hoecakes and from rice cakes. Baking powder and soda are nineteenth-century tools, and they are what make this cake rise as high as it does.

Why it tastes this way

Buttermilk tastes slightly sour, and that sourness is the point, not a flaw. Melted butter in the batter and on the griddle browns at the edge. Stop stirring once the flour disappears: extra stirring tightens gluten and turns the crumb tough.

How it is made

Griddled in butter over medium heat until the center is set.

Authentic touch. Use real buttermilk, not regular milk with a splash of lemon. The griddle is ready when a drop of water skitters.

How it is used

Stacked hot, with butter and cane syrup or sorghum.

Field notes

  • Chemical leaveners, not yeast, are what lift this particular Southern stack.
  • A watery batter makes them spread thin, and a stiff batter makes them heavy.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups all-purpose flour
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 2 teaspoons baking powder
  • 1 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 cups buttermilk
  • 2 large eggs
  • 3 tablespoons unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the griddle

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, sugar, baking powder, baking soda, and salt in a bowl.
  2. In a second bowl, whisk the buttermilk, eggs, and melted butter.
  3. Pour the wet ingredients into the dry and stir just until no dry flour remains. A few lumps are fine.
  4. Heat a griddle over medium heat and film it with butter. A drop of water should skitter.
  5. Pour about 1/4 cup batter for each cake. Cook until the bubbles on top pop and stay open, then flip and cook until the center is set.
  6. Serve at once with butter and cane syrup or sorghum.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Leave the sugar out when the plate is savory.
  • Replace two tablespoons of the flour with cornmeal for a coarser crumb.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Butter
  • Cane syrup
  • Sorghum syrup
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Rhode Island johnnycakes

Toasted corn, salt, and a crisp edge, with almost no sweetness in the cake itself.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 10 thin cakes

History

Rhode Island johnnycakes are thin cornmeal cakes, traditionally made with white flint cornmeal scalded with boiling water. The name is disputed: journey cake and Shawnee cake are both proposed sources, and neither explanation is settled. South County cooks spread the batter thin so the cakes crisp, while other Rhode Island versions are thicker and use milk. This recipe is the thin water-scalded kind.

Cultural background

White flint corn was the local grain that millers stone-ground for these cakes. Spellings vary in the state: johnnycake and jonnycake both appear on menus and in cookbooks.

Why it tastes this way

Boiling water swells the cornmeal and gives the cake a soft center under a brittle crust. There is no wheat gluten to make it bready. The flavor is the grain itself, plus whatever fat the griddle contributes.

How it is made

Scalded cornmeal spread thin on a hot griddle.

Authentic touch. Use stone-ground white cornmeal if you can find it. Spread the batter thin. A thick, cakey round is the other Rhode Island style.

How it is used

Hot from the griddle. Butter and maple syrup are common at the table and are not part of this batter.

Field notes

  • Journey cake and Shawnee cake are both disputed explanations of the name.
  • Both the spellings johnnycake and jonnycake are used in Rhode Island.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup stone-ground white cornmeal
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup boiling water, plus 1 to 2 tablespoons cold water if needed
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil, for the griddle

Steps

  1. Stir the cornmeal and salt in a heatproof bowl.
  2. Pour the boiling water over the meal and stir until a thick batter forms. Rest 5 minutes.
  3. If the batter is too stiff to spread, stir in cold water a spoon at a time. It should move slowly off the spoon.
  4. Film a griddle with oil and heat it over medium.
  5. Spread about 2 tablespoons of batter into a thin round. Cook until the edge is crisp and the underside is browned, then flip and finish.
  6. Serve at once. The cakes harden as they cool.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A milk batter, cooked thicker, is the other well-known Rhode Island style.
  • Some cooks grease the griddle with bacon fat instead of oil.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Accompaniment to a meal
  • Maple syrup
  • Butter, if you eat dairy
  • Salt fish cakes, in an old Rhode Island breakfast
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Southern hoecakes

Plain toasted corn, salt, and a fried edge, denser than a wheat pancake.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 hoecakes

History

Southern hoecakes are round cornmeal cakes cooked on a griddle. The story that they were baked on the blade of a hoe is widely repeated and disputed, and this page does not treat that story as fact. Hoecakes were ordinary food in the South, including at eighteenth-century Virginia tables such as Mount Vernon. This version is meal, salt, and boiling water, with no wheat.

Cultural background

Corn was the everyday grain. A hoecake was a way to cook a quick bread on a flat iron when there was no oven loaf. The name survives even though the hoe-blade explanation is not settled.

Why it tastes this way

Scalding the meal softens the coarse parts and lets the cake hold together without egg or flour. The surface against the fat tastes toasted. The inside stays moist and distinctly of corn.

How it is made

Shaped cornmeal rounds griddled in a thin film of oil.

Authentic touch. Make them thicker than a Rhode Island johnnycake, about a finger thick, and cook them through. Ignore the hoe story and use a griddle.

How it is used

Hot, as a bread beside beans or with syrup if you want them sweet.

Field notes

  • The hoe-blade baking story is famous and disputed.
  • George Washington's household at Mount Vernon ate hoecakes, which is well documented.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups cornmeal
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 1/4 cups boiling water
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil, plus more for the griddle

Steps

  1. Stir the cornmeal, salt, and oil in a bowl.
  2. Pour in the boiling water and stir to a dough stiff enough to hold a round. Rest 10 minutes.
  3. With wet hands, shape 8 rounds about 1/2 inch thick.
  4. Heat a film of oil on a griddle over medium heat.
  5. Cook the cakes until the underside is browned and the side looks set, about 4 minutes, then flip and cook until the center is no longer wet.
  6. Serve hot. They are bread, not a tall diner stack.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Grease the griddle with bacon drippings if you eat pork.
  • A spoon of wheat flour in the batter is a later, softer version and is no longer gluten-free.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Accompaniment to a meal
  • Potlikker or beans
  • Sorghum syrup
  • Butter, if you eat dairy
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Appalachian buckwheat cakes

Earthly and slightly sour, with a gray-brown crumb and a sorghum finish if you use it at the table.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 cakes

History

Appalachian buckwheat cakes use flour milled from buckwheat, a crop that grows on cooler mountain ground, usually mixed with wheat flour so the cake holds together. Scots-Irish and English settlement lines up with this griddle, and German-speaking neighbors lived in the same hills. Enslaved people and later Black Appalachian cooks worked many of the same farms. The cakes are a tangy breakfast, often finished with sorghum rather than maple.

Cultural background

Buckwheat is not a kind of wheat. This recipe still uses all-purpose flour, so the cakes are not gluten-free. Elkins stands in for a griddle that was widespread in the uplands.

Why it tastes this way

Buckwheat tastes stronger and earthier than white flour, and buttermilk sharpens it. Soda reacts with the buttermilk and lifts a batter that would otherwise be heavy. Sorghum at the table adds a mineral sweetness that white sugar does not.

How it is made

Griddled buttermilk and buckwheat batter.

Authentic touch. Keep at least half the flour as buckwheat so the cakes taste like the grain. Serve them with sorghum if you can get it.

How it is used

Hot, with butter and sorghum syrup.

Field notes

  • Buckwheat fell out of mountain fields as wheat flour became cheap, and the cakes got rarer with it.
  • Sorghum, not maple, was the everyday sweetener with these cakes in much of the upland South.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup buckwheat flour
  • 1/2 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 cups buttermilk
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the griddle

Steps

  1. Whisk both flours, the baking soda, and the salt.
  2. Whisk in the buttermilk, egg, and melted butter until just combined. The batter will be dark and a bit thick.
  3. Heat a buttered griddle over medium heat.
  4. Pour 1/4 cup rounds. Cook until the top is set and the underside is brown, then flip.
  5. The second side browns faster. Move the cakes off when the center springs back.
  6. Serve with butter and sorghum syrup.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A yeasted overnight batter is the older leavening and belongs to a longer method.
  • All-buckwheat cakes are more fragile and are not automatically gluten-free unless the flour is certified.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Sorghum syrup
  • Butter
  • Sausage, if you eat meat
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Hopi piki

Very thin toasted blue corn, delicate and dry enough to fold, with a mineral note if ash is used.

20 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 thin sheets

History

Hopi piki is a paper-thin corn sheet, often made with blue corn, cooked on a hot stone and folded or rolled. It is a Hopi food from the Hopi mesas. This page describes the food only and does not describe ceremony. A home cook can approximate the thin sheet on a griddle, while the prepared stone and a practiced hand are what make piki itself.

Cultural background

Blue corn is a Hopi crop, and the sheet is often blue corn rather than a white-meal pancake. Culinary ash is part of many batters. This journal does not give instructions for ceremony or for burning plants to make ash.

Why it tastes this way

The sheet is thin enough that almost every bit of meal touches the hot surface, so the flavor is toasted corn rather than a soft crumb. Ash, when it is used, changes the color and adds a mineral taste. Oil on the stone keeps the sheet from sticking and adds a faint richness.

How it is made

Paper-thin blue-corn batter spread on a very hot oiled stone or griddle.

Authentic touch. Spread the batter paper-thin. If the result is a thick hotcake, it is not piki. Omit ash rather than inventing a substitute ceremony or burning random plants.

How it is used

Folded or rolled while still flexible, then eaten as a crisp sheet.

Field notes

  • Piki is paper-thin and often made with blue corn on a hot stone.
  • This journal describes the food and does not describe ceremony.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup very finely ground blue cornmeal
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon food-grade culinary ash made for blue-corn foods, sifted, if you have it
  • 1 3/4 cups water, plus more if needed
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil, for the cooking surface

Steps

  1. Whisk the blue cornmeal, salt, and ash if you are using it. Do not burn plants to invent an ash. If you have no culinary ash, leave it out.
  2. Whisk in water until the batter is as thin as heavy cream. Rest 10 minutes and whisk again. Add water if it thickened.
  3. Heat a flat griddle or stone until a drop of water sizzles away at once. Rub on a thin film of oil.
  4. Pour a small pool of batter and immediately spread it paper-thin with the back of a spoon.
  5. Cook until the edges lift and the top looks dry. This is quick. Peel the sheet off and fold it while it is flexible.
  6. Repeat with the remaining batter, oiling the surface lightly when a sheet sticks. The sheets should be thin enough to see light through.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • White or yellow cornmeal makes a related thin sheet with a different flavor.
  • Without culinary ash, the sheet still cooks, and it will lack the mineral note of ash-treated piki.

Pairings and uses

  • Snack
  • Accompaniment to a meal
  • A pinch of salt
  • Stew, as a bread on the side
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Dutch baby

Eggy, custardy in the center, and crisp at the lifted rim, with browned butter.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 1 large oven pancake

History

The Dutch baby is an American oven pancake whose name is usually explained as Deutsch, meaning German. It is not a cake from the Netherlands, and it is not a Pennsylvania Dutch recipe. Seattle restaurants are often credited with popularizing it in the early twentieth century, and that popularizer story is the usual account.

Cultural background

The batter is closer to a popover than to a stack of griddle cakes: eggs, milk, and flour baked in a hot buttered skillet. It puffs in the oven and settles as it cools. Seattle is the place on this page.

Why it tastes this way

Steam from the wet batter, trapped by eggs, throws the edges up the sides of a hot pan. Butter browns on the skillet and flavors the crust. The center stays softer because it is thicker. Lemon and sugar at the table cut the richness.

How it is made

Baked in a preheated buttered skillet in a hot oven.

Authentic touch. Bake it in a preheated skillet. A cold pan will not lift the rim. Do not flip it on a griddle and expect the same shape.

How it is used

Straight from the skillet, often with lemon and powdered sugar.

Field notes

  • The usual explanation of the name is Deutsch, meaning German, not a cake from the Netherlands.
  • Seattle restaurants are often credited with popularizing it in the early twentieth century, and that story is the usual account.

Ingredients

  • 3 large eggs
  • 2/3 cup whole milk
  • 2/3 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 3 tablespoons unsalted butter

Steps

  1. Heat the oven to 425°F with a 10-inch ovenproof skillet inside.
  2. Whisk the eggs, milk, flour, sugar, and salt until smooth. A few small lumps will bake out.
  3. Add the butter to the hot skillet and return it to the oven until the butter melts and browns slightly.
  4. Pour the batter into the hot butter. Bake about 18 minutes, until the edges are high and browned and the center is just set.
  5. Do not open the oven early or the puff drops.
  6. Serve at once with lemon and powdered sugar. It settles as it cools.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A savory version leaves out the sugar and adds grated cheese and herbs, which is a different plate.
  • Individual pans make smaller babies and finish a few minutes sooner.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Dessert
  • Lemon wedges
  • Powdered sugar
  • Fruit preserves
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Lowcountry rice griddle cakes

Soft, lightly sweet rice cakes with a crisp buttered edge and separate grains in the crumb.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 10 griddle cakes

History

Lowcountry rice griddle cakes use the long-grain rice of the South Carolina and Georgia coast. Enslaved West Africans brought rice-growing knowledge, and planters built a brutal rice economy on their labor and on the labor of their descendants. The cake is a later griddle use of that grain. It is not a rename of one village dish.

Cultural background

Charleston and the surrounding Lowcountry were the center of that rice system. Carolina-grown long-grain rice is the grain to use when you can get it. Any cooked long-grain white rice will hold the cake.

Why it tastes this way

Cooked rice stays in distinct grains, so the cake tastes steamy and mild instead of like a smooth wheat crumb. Rice flour binds the cakes without gluten. Butter browns the outside and keeps the rice from tasting flat.

How it is made

Cooked rice and rice flour griddled in butter.

Authentic touch. Use leftover cooked rice, not raw rice thrown into a wheat pancake. Name the labor when you tell the story of the grain.

How it is used

Warm, with butter. A little sorghum or cane syrup fits the coast.

Field notes

  • Rice in the Lowcountry is tied to West African knowledge and to enslaved labor, not to a single exported village recipe.
  • Leftover cooked rice is the usual base, which is why these cakes are a breakfast after a rice supper.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups cooked long-grain white rice, cooled
  • 2 large eggs
  • 1/2 cup milk
  • 1/3 cup rice flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the griddle

Steps

  1. Mash the rice with a fork so some grains break and some stay whole.
  2. Stir in the eggs, milk, rice flour, baking powder, sugar, salt, and melted butter.
  3. Heat a buttered griddle over medium heat.
  4. Drop 1/4 cup mounds and flatten them slightly. They will not look like smooth wheat batter.
  5. Cook until the underside is golden and the cake holds together, then flip and finish.
  6. Serve warm with butter.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A savory version leaves out the sugar and adds a sliced scallion.
  • Wheat flour in place of rice flour means the cakes are no longer gluten-free.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Butter
  • Cane syrup
  • Shrimp gravy, for a savory plate, if you eat shellfish
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

New Orleans calas

Fried rice fritters, lightly sweet, with nutmeg and a powdered-sugar coat.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · About 18 fritters

History

New Orleans calas are fried rice fritters sold for generations by Black vendors, many of them women, who carried the cakes through the streets. Rice growing in Louisiana depended on the skill of enslaved West Africans and on the plantation economy built on their labor. This page does not claim that one West African village recipe became calas. The fritter that can be documented is the Creole rice cake sold in the city.

Cultural background

Older calas were yeasted. This recipe uses a short rise and then fries the batter. Cooked rice is the body. Wheat flour and egg bind it. The written recipe has no milk.

Why it tastes this way

Rice stays in soft grains inside a crisp fried shell. Nutmeg is the spice most associated with the fritter, used lightly. Sugar in the batter and powdered sugar afterward make them a sweet street breakfast. Yeast gives a mild ferment flavor if the batter actually rises. If it does not rest, the fritters taste heavy.

How it is made

Yeasted cooked-rice batter fried in oil and dusted with powdered sugar.

Authentic touch. Fry them. A baked rice muffin is not a calas. Dust them with powdered sugar while they are hot, which is how street calas were eaten.

How it is used

Hot, as a breakfast or a snack, with powdered sugar.

Field notes

  • Calas were sold on New Orleans streets by Black vendors, often women.
  • The rice in Louisiana cooking is tied to enslaved West African skill, and this page does not rename one village recipe as calas.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups cooked medium-grain white rice, cooled
  • 1 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/4 teaspoon grated nutmeg
  • 2 large eggs
  • 1/2 cup all-purpose flour
  • 6 cups neutral oil, for frying
  • 1/2 cup powdered sugar, for serving

Steps

  1. Mash the rice with a fork or your hand so some grains break and some remain.
  2. Stir in the yeast, sugar, salt, nutmeg, eggs, and flour. The batter should be thick and spoonable.
  3. Cover and let it rise in a warm place until puffy, about 1 hour. That rise is not added to the active prep time.
  4. Heat 1 1/2 inches of oil in a deep pan to 350°F.
  5. Slip heaping tablespoons of batter into the oil. Fry until deep golden, turning once, about 2 to 3 minutes.
  6. Drain on a rack, dust with powdered sugar, and serve hot.

Fry in enough oil to cover the fritters halfway or more, and do not leave the pot. Keep the heat at a steady 350°F.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Baking powder in place of yeast is a quicker home batter and will taste less fermented.
  • A splash of milk is a later enrichment and would add dairy.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Street food
  • Snack
  • Powdered sugar
  • Coffee
  • A little more nutmeg
  • Vegetarian
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Hudson Valley buckwheat cakes

Sour, earthy buckwheat with a molasses edge and a crisp griddle face.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 cakes

History

Dutch settlers in the Hudson Valley cooked buckwheat cakes, and the grain fits a cool climate and a short season. The same grain shows up in other cold regions for that reason. Nineteenth-century New York breakfasts often included a yeasted buckwheat batter mixed with some wheat flour. These cakes are that Dutch-American breakfast, pinned at Kingston.

Cultural background

The overnight rise is the leavening. A pinch of baking soda in the morning steadies the sour batter and helps it brown. This recipe uses water rather than milk.

Why it tastes this way

Time, not a large dose of baking powder, makes the sour taste. Buckwheat carries an earthy flavor that wheat softens just enough to hold the cake. Molasses feeds the yeast and leaves a dark note. The soda in the morning takes the sharpest edge off.

How it is made

Yeasted overnight buckwheat batter cooked on a griddle.

Authentic touch. Let the batter ferment overnight. A same-morning baking-powder cake can use these flours, but it will not taste like the old Hudson Valley breakfast.

How it is used

Hot, often with butter and maple syrup at a modern table. The batter itself has no dairy.

Field notes

  • Buckwheat suited the short seasons of the Hudson Valley, which is why Dutch-American cooks relied on it.
  • The overnight ferment is the rise. Do not count those hours as hands-on prep.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup buckwheat flour
  • 1/2 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1 tablespoon molasses
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 1/2 cups warm water
  • 1/4 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil, for the griddle

Steps

  1. Stir both flours, the yeast, molasses, salt, and warm water until smooth.
  2. Cover the bowl and leave it at room temperature overnight, about 8 to 12 hours, until the batter is bubbly and smells sour. That wait is not part of the active prep time.
  3. Stir in the baking soda just before cooking.
  4. Film a griddle with oil over medium heat.
  5. Pour small cakes. Cook until the top is full of holes and the underside is brown, then flip and finish.
  6. Serve hot. Butter and maple syrup are table additions.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of wheat flour more makes them less fragile.
  • Skip the morning soda if you want the sourness louder.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Maple syrup
  • Butter, if you eat dairy
  • Smoked fish, for a savory plate
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Pennsylvania Dutch potato pancakes

Shredded potato, onion, and pepper, crisp at the edge and soft in the middle.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 potato cakes

History

Pennsylvania Dutch potato pancakes are shredded-potato cakes griddled by German-speaking communities in Pennsylvania. Pennsylvania Dutch means Deutsch, not a recipe from the Netherlands. The cakes are cooked on a griddle, with crisp edges and a soft center. Lancaster County is the map pin.

Cultural background

Potato cakes traveled with German-speaking cooks and became everyday food, not only a holiday plate. They are related to other shredded-potato cakes and are not the same page as a Jewish latke.

Why it tastes this way

Potato starch that stays in the bowl is part of what holds the cake together. Rinsing the shreds until the water runs clear can make them fall apart. Onion browns at the edge and keeps the potato from tasting flat. The griddle, with only a thin film of oil, fries the surface without deep-frying the center.

How it is made

Shredded potato and onion griddled in a thin film of oil.

Authentic touch. Griddle them, do not deep-fry them. Squeeze excess water from the potatoes and leave the white starch in the bowl.

How it is used

Hot, with applesauce or sour cream if you want dairy at the table. The cakes themselves have no milk or butter.

Field notes

  • Pennsylvania Dutch comes from Deutsch, meaning German, not from the Netherlands.
  • These cakes are griddled. The funnel cake from the same communities is the fried one.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 pounds russet potatoes
  • 1 small yellow onion
  • 2 large eggs
  • 3 tablespoons all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/4 teaspoon black pepper
  • 3 tablespoons neutral oil

Steps

  1. Peel the potatoes and grate them on the large holes of a box grater. Grate the onion the same way.
  2. Squeeze the mixture in a towel over a bowl. Pour off the water that collects and keep the white starch at the bottom of the bowl.
  3. Stir the potato, onion, starch, eggs, flour, salt, and pepper together.
  4. Heat a thin film of oil in a skillet over medium heat.
  5. Drop 1/4 cup mounds, flatten them, and cook until the underside is deep brown, about 3 to 4 minutes. Flip and cook until the center is tender.
  6. Add oil as needed. Serve hot.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Add a spoon of minced parsley for a greener cake.
  • Flour can be reduced if the potatoes are very starchy, but do not rinse the starch away.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • Accompaniment to a meal
  • Applesauce
  • Sour cream, if you eat dairy
  • Chives
  • Vegetarian
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Upper Midwest Swedish pancakes

Thin, eggy, and lightly sweet, rolled around butter and jam.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 10 thin pancakes

History

These are the thin egg pancakes of Swedish-American kitchens in Minneapolis and the Upper Midwest. The batter is close to a crepe and is usually rolled or folded with butter and jam, often lingonberry. This is the United States page, the cake at church breakfasts and home tables. It is not the page for the Swedish original.

Cultural background

Scandinavian settlement put this breakfast on Upper Midwest tables. The cakes did not replace Indigenous food in the region. They are one immigrant griddle among others. Lingonberry jam is the familiar preserve, and raspberry jam is the common stand-in.

Why it tastes this way

More egg and milk than a diner pancake makes the cake thin and custardy. A resting batter spreads evenly and tastes less floury. Butter in the pan is the browning you taste at the edge of the roll.

How it is made

Thin egg batter swirled in a buttered skillet and rolled.

Authentic touch. Cook them thin enough to roll. A thick, baking-powder pancake is the diner stack, not this page. Lingonberry jam is the preserve most tied to the Swedish-American table.

How it is used

Rolled or folded, with butter and lingonberry or raspberry jam.

Field notes

  • This is the Swedish-American pancake of Minneapolis and the Upper Midwest, not the Swedish original.
  • Church-basement breakfasts helped keep the thin pancakes on the regional table.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 3 large eggs
  • 1 1/2 cups whole milk
  • 2 tablespoons unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the pan

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, sugar, salt, eggs, milk, and melted butter into a thin batter. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Heat an 8-inch skillet over medium heat and coat it with butter.
  3. Pour about 1/4 cup batter and swirl the pan so the batter covers the bottom.
  4. Cook until the top looks dry and the underside is golden in spots. Flip if you can, or slide the cake out if it is already set.
  5. Roll each cake with a little butter and jam, or fold them on a plate.
  6. Keep finished cakes warm in a low oven while you cook the rest.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A squeeze of lemon and a dusting of sugar is a plainer finish.
  • Whipped cream is a festive table addition and is still dairy.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Lingonberry jam
  • Butter
  • Raspberry jam
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Alaska sourdough flapjacks

Mildly sour wheat cakes, a little richer from evaporated milk, with a browned edge.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 flapjacks

History

Sourdough flapjacks are the breakfast cooks made from a living starter in Alaska, where gold-rush camps kept a starter going because fresh yeast was hard to replace. The word sourdough became a nickname for an old hand in that country. The cakes are wheat pancakes leavened by the starter and finished with a little soda. Skagway, a Klondike gateway, is the map pin.

Cultural background

In this journal, flapjack means the American pancake, not the British oat bar. Evaporated milk was a practical canned dairy in camps and is used here. The starter has to be active before you start, so feeding it is not counted in the prep time.

Why it tastes this way

A fed starter brings a light sourness and some of the rise. Baking soda reacts with that acid and gives the cakes a quicker lift on the griddle. Evaporated milk tastes slightly cooked and rich. If the starter is sleepy, the cakes taste flat and do not brown as well.

How it is made

Sourdough batter finished with baking soda and cooked on a griddle.

Authentic touch. Use a bubbly wheat starter you have already fed. Stir the soda in just before cooking so the batter does not collapse on the counter.

How it is used

Hot, with butter and syrup.

Field notes

  • Gold-rush cooks in Alaska and the Yukon kept sourdough starters because fresh yeast was scarce.
  • Sourdough also became a nickname for someone who had lasted in that country.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup active sourdough starter, fed ahead of time
  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 large egg
  • 1/2 cup evaporated milk
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil, plus more for the griddle
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda

Steps

  1. Stir the starter, flour, sugar, salt, egg, and evaporated milk until smooth. The starter should already be bubbly from a feeding done before this recipe.
  2. Rest the bowl 15 minutes.
  3. Stir in the oil and baking soda.
  4. Oil a griddle over medium heat. Pour 1/4 cup cakes.
  5. Flip when the surface is full of holes and the underside is brown. Cook until the center is set.
  6. Serve hot with butter and syrup.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A water-based batter without evaporated milk is leaner and less dairy-rich, but then the recipe changes.
  • Fresh milk can replace evaporated milk if that is what you have. Use 1/2 cup.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Butter
  • Maple syrup or berry jam
  • Coffee
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Acadian ployes

Mild buckwheat, a little sour from the grain, with a holey top and a soft underside.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 10 ployes

History

Acadian ployes are buckwheat pancakes of the Madawaska region, on the New Brunswick side of the Saint John River and in nearby Maine. The batter is usually cooked on one side. Buckwheat suited the cool seasons of that border country. The map pin is in Canada, and the same cakes are made across the river in the United States.

Cultural background

A ploye batter is thin, often buckwheat plus some wheat flour, water, and baking powder. Many recipes have no egg and no milk. This one follows that pattern. It is not gluten-free, because of the wheat flour.

Why it tastes this way

Buckwheat tastes earthy, and cooking on one side leaves the top soft and full of holes while the bottom browns. Water keeps the flavor plain. Butter or maple at the table is how many people eat them, and those additions are dairy or sweetness on top of a batter that has neither.

How it is made

Thin buckwheat batter cooked on one side on a hot griddle.

Authentic touch. Cook them on one side only, until the top is dry and bubbled. Flipping them makes a different pancake. Serve them as a bread, not only as a dessert.

How it is used

Hot, often with butter, maple syrup, or baked beans. Cretons, a pork spread, is a local accompaniment for people who eat pork.

Field notes

  • Ployes belong to the Madawaska region of New Brunswick and to nearby Maine.
  • They are traditionally cooked on one side, until the top dries and forms holes.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup buckwheat flour
  • 1/2 cup all-purpose flour
  • 2 teaspoons baking powder
  • 3/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1 1/2 cups cold water
  • 1 teaspoon neutral oil, for the griddle

Steps

  1. Whisk both flours, the baking powder, and the salt.
  2. Whisk in the cold water until the batter is smooth and thin, like cream. Add a spoon of water if it is stiff.
  3. Heat a griddle over medium-high heat and wipe it with a thin film of oil.
  4. Pour about 1/4 cup batter. It should spread.
  5. Cook on one side until the top is dry, the surface is full of holes, and the underside is brown. Do not flip.
  6. Stack them and serve hot.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some families use a higher share of buckwheat. Unless that flour is certified gluten-free, do not call the cakes gluten-free.
  • An egg in the batter is a later enrichment and removes the vegan label.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Accompaniment to a meal
  • Maple syrup
  • Baked beans
  • Butter, if you eat dairy
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Dairy-free

Southeastern corn griddle cakes

Plain corn, salt, and a toasted surface, with no sugar and no wheat.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 cakes

History

Southeastern corn griddle cakes sit in the family of corn breads cooked by Native nations of the Southeast, where corn was the staple grain long before wheat arrived. Those breads are not all the same. This plain cornmeal cake does not belong to one nation, and it is not a ceremonial bread rewritten as a diner recipe. The batter is meal, water, and salt, cooked on a griddle.

Cultural background

The Tennessee River valley is a geographic pin for the wider Southeast, not a claim that one town or one nation owns the cake. Hopi piki is a different food and has its own page. Wheat is left out on purpose.

Why it tastes this way

The flavor is toasted cornmeal and salt. Water instead of milk keeps it plain. A moderate thickness gives a soft center and a firm crust. Sugar, egg, and wheat would each pull it toward a later American pancake.

How it is made

Plain cornmeal batter cooked as medium rounds on a griddle.

Authentic touch. Cook meal, water, and salt. Do not present the result as the ceremonial bread of a nation, and do not say that every Southeastern nation made this exact cake.

How it is used

Hot, as a bread. It needs no syrup.

Field notes

  • Corn was the staple of Southeastern Native nations long before wheat flour arrived.
  • This recipe is a plain griddle cake in that family, and it is not owned by one nation.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup cornmeal
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup warm water
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil, plus more for the griddle

Steps

  1. Stir the cornmeal, salt, water, and oil into a batter that holds a shape and still spreads a little.
  2. Rest 5 minutes. If it stiffened, stir in a spoon of water.
  3. Film a griddle with oil over medium heat.
  4. Drop about 1/4 cup of batter for each cake and nudge it into a round about 1/2 inch thick.
  5. Cook until the underside is brown and the edge looks dry, then flip and cook until the center is set.
  6. Serve hot as a bread.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A thinner batter, cooked crisp, moves toward lacy cornbread.
  • Adding wheat flour, buttermilk, and egg makes a New England-style corn cake and adds those allergens.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Accompaniment to a meal
  • Salt
  • Cooked greens or beans
  • A drizzle of oil
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free

English Shrove pancakes

Thin egg pancakes, lemon and sugar, made to clear the kitchen before Lent.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 thin pancakes

History

These are the thin English pancakes of Shrove Tuesday, the day before Lent. The batter uses the eggs, milk, and fat that a strict Lenten fast would set aside. Shrove Tuesday is one day, not a week, and it is not the French crepe holiday. In England the usual finish is lemon juice and sugar. Butter, jam, or a savory filling exist, but lemon and sugar is the plate this page records.

Cultural background

Pancake races and church bells are local Shrove customs in some English towns. The cake itself is older than those events and does not depend on them. A home cook only needs a pan and a toss they can afford to miss.

Why it tastes this way

The batter is loose with egg and milk, so it sets in a thin sheet instead of a fluffy stack. The surface browns in butter. Lemon cuts the fat, and sugar stays gritty for a moment on the hot cake. There is no baking powder, so the flavor is egg, milk, and browned butter rather than a soda tang.

How it is made

Whisk a thin batter of flour, egg, and milk. Cook one ladle at a time in a hot buttered pan, turning once, until both sides are spotted brown.

Authentic touch. Rest the batter for half an hour if you can. The first pancake is the test piece. Toss it or turn it with a spatula, then eat it while the next one cooks.

How it is used

On Shrove Tuesday, folded or rolled with lemon and sugar. They are a feast-day pudding more than a tall breakfast stack.

Field notes

  • Shrove Tuesday is the single day before Lent, not a butter week.
  • The first pancake is traditionally the cook's test, and it is still eaten.
  • Lemon and sugar, not syrup, is the usual English finish.

Ingredients

  • 100 g plain wheat flour
  • 2 large eggs
  • 300 ml whole milk
  • 1 pinch fine salt
  • 30 g unsalted butter, for the pan and for serving
  • 2 lemons, cut into wedges
  • 3 tbsp sugar

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour and salt. Beat in the eggs, then the milk, until the batter is as thin as light cream. Rest it for 20 minutes if you have the time.
  2. Heat a frying pan over medium-high heat and grease it with a little butter.
  3. Pour in a thin layer of batter and swirl. Cook until the top looks dry and the underside is golden, about 1 minute. Turn and cook the second side for about 30 seconds.
  4. Fold or roll onto a warm plate. Add a squeeze of lemon and a spoon of sugar. Repeat, buttering the pan lightly between pancakes.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Orange juice instead of lemon
  • A savory ham and cheese fold, which is a later meal and not the Shrove plate

Pairings and uses

  • Feast or fast day
  • Dessert
  • Breakfast
  • Lemon wedges and sugar
  • A little jam, if lemon is not on the table
  • Tea
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Scotch pancakes

Small thick drop scones from a Scottish girdle, butter and jam.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 16 small pancakes

History

Scotch pancakes, also called drop scones, are small thick cakes cooked on a girdle. They are not the thin English Shrove pancake and they are not a crepe. A modern batter uses baking powder or buttermilk and soda. Earlier dropped cakes relied more on eggs. This recipe uses baking powder and buttermilk because that is the home cake people mean by the name now. The country pin is the United Kingdom, and the culture is Scottish.

Cultural background

They are a tea-table and breakfast cake across Scotland. A heavy girdle, or a thick frying pan, is the tool. The cakes are eaten the day they are cooked.

Why it tastes this way

Buttermilk and a quick leavener make a soft, slightly tangy crumb. The girdle browns the outside before the inside dries. Sugar in the batter is light. Jam does the rest. Overmixing makes them tough, because the flour is wheat.

How it is made

Flour, egg, buttermilk, and baking powder are mixed briefly and dropped onto a lightly greased girdle.

Authentic touch. Drop the batter from a spoon. Do not spread it into a crepe. Turn when bubbles sit open on the top, and only once.

How it is used

Warm, with butter and jam, at breakfast or tea. They are not rolled.

Field notes

  • Drop scone is the other name. The cake is thick and small.
  • They are a different batter from English Shrove pancakes.
  • A girdle is a griddle. The spelling is the Scottish one.

Ingredients

  • 150 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tsp baking powder
  • 1 tbsp sugar
  • 1 pinch fine salt
  • 1 large egg
  • 180 ml buttermilk
  • 20 g unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the girdle

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, baking powder, sugar, and salt. Beat the egg with the buttermilk and melted butter.
  2. Stir the wet ingredients into the dry until just combined. A few lumps are fine.
  3. Grease a girdle or heavy pan over medium heat. Drop spoonfuls and cook until bubbles open on top.
  4. Turn once and cook until the centers spring back. Butter them and serve with jam.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of raisins in the batter
  • Thick cream instead of butter

Pairings and uses

  • Butter
  • Raspberry jam or another jam
  • Tea

Irish boxty

Pan boxty: raw grated potato and mashed potato, fried as cakes.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 pan cakes

History

Boxty is an Irish potato cake. There are three forms: pan boxty, loaf boxty, and dumpling boxty. This page is only the pan cake. The batter mixes raw grated potato with mashed potato and flour, then the cakes are fried. Potatoes reached Irish kitchens and became a staple. The cake is one of the ways a raw potato was cooked without waiting for a roast. Flour means this recipe is not gluten-free.

Cultural background

Pan boxty is breakfast and supper food. It is associated with the north and the midlands as much as with any single county. This page does not assign it to one village.

Why it tastes this way

Raw potato brings starch and a clean flavor. Mash brings softness. Flour binds the two. Frying browns the starch. If you rinse all the starch off the grated potato, the cake falls apart and tastes watery. Salt is the seasoning. A crisp edge and a soft center is the point.

How it is made

Equal parts raw grated potato and mashed potato are mixed with flour, salt, and a little milk, then fried as flat cakes in butter or oil.

Authentic touch. Grate the raw potato and keep the liquid that settles into starch. Pour off the water and put the starch back. That paste helps the cake hold.

How it is used

Hot, with butter, eggs, or bacon. The cake on this page is vegetarian. Bacon is a serving choice, not part of the batter.

Field notes

  • Pan, loaf, and dumpling boxty are three methods. This is the pan cake.
  • The mix is raw grated potato plus mashed potato, not mash alone.
  • The starch from the grated potato helps bind the cake.

Ingredients

  • 250 g raw potatoes, peeled
  • 250 g cold mashed potato
  • 100 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tsp fine salt
  • 60 ml milk
  • 30 g unsalted butter, for frying

Steps

  1. Grate the raw potatoes onto a clean cloth. Squeeze the liquid into a bowl. Let it sit so the starch settles, pour off the water, and keep the starch.
  2. Mix the squeezed potato, the starch, the mash, flour, salt, and enough milk to make a stiff batter.
  3. Heat the butter in a frying pan. Shape flat cakes and fry over medium heat until both sides are brown and the raw potato is cooked through, about 4 minutes a side.
  4. Rest them a minute and serve with butter or eggs.

The raw potato must cook through. Cut one cake open. The center should be soft and hot, not glassy.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Boxty loaf, baked as a bread, which is a different method
  • Boxty dumpling, boiled, which is also a different method

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • Butter
  • Fried eggs
  • Bacon, if you want a meat breakfast
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Welsh crempog

Welsh buttermilk griddle cakes, stacked with butter between the layers.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 small cakes

History

Crempog, plural crempogau, is the Welsh griddle cake. Buttermilk and bicarbonate are a common home batter, and yeasted overnight batters are also traditional. This page uses buttermilk. The cakes are stacked with butter between them, which is the serving that matters. Some families make a stack for a birthday, and some make crempog when eggs and fat are used up before Lent. Neither occasion is the only one.

Cultural background

The cake is Welsh-language food as well as a recipe. The country on the map is the United Kingdom. The culture is Welsh. A heavy pan or bakestone is the tool.

Why it tastes this way

Buttermilk and soda give a light tang and a soft crumb. Butter between hot cakes melts and salts each layer. The outside is only just browned. They taste less of sugar than a dessert crepe, unless you add jam.

How it is made

Flour, egg, buttermilk, melted butter, and baking soda are mixed to a pourable batter and cooked as small rounds, then stacked with butter.

Authentic touch. Stack them with a smear of butter on each hot cake, not only on the top one. A yeasted batter, left overnight, is the other honest version.

How it is used

In a buttered stack, at breakfast, tea, a birthday, or Shrove. Jam is optional.

Field notes

  • The plural is crempogau. The stack is buttered between the cakes.
  • Buttermilk versions and yeast versions both exist.
  • Some Welsh families stack them for a birthday.

Ingredients

  • 200 g plain wheat flour
  • 1/2 tsp baking soda
  • 1 pinch fine salt
  • 2 large eggs
  • 300 ml buttermilk
  • 25 g unsalted butter, melted, plus more for stacking and the pan

Steps

  2. Grease a bakestone or frying pan over medium heat.
  3. Cook small ladlefuls until the top dulls, then turn and color the other side.
  4. Stack the cakes with butter between them. Serve the stack warm.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A yeasted crempog batter left to rise overnight
  • A little oatmeal in place of part of the wheat flour

Staffordshire oatcakes

Large, soft, yeasted oatmeal pancakes from the Potteries, rolled around a savory filling.

1 hr 20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 large oatcakes

History

Staffordshire oatcakes are yeasted oatmeal pancakes from the Potteries, the towns around Stoke-on-Trent. They are large, thin, and flexible, sold in oatcake shops and rolled around bacon, cheese, or sausage. They are not the dry Scottish oatcake, and they are not the Derbyshire batter on the next page. Both counties use yeast and oats. The Staffordshire cake is oat-heavy and meant to fold.

Cultural background

An oatcake shop batter is a local product. Home cooks keep a jug of it warm so the yeast stays alive. The cake is breakfast and lunch food, eaten hot.

Why it tastes this way

History

Crêpes Suzette are dessert crepes folded in orange butter and an orange sauce, often finished with a warmed orange liqueur. The orange butter is what makes them Suzette. They are a restaurant dessert, not the Chandeleur custom, though the same thin crepe batter can be used on 2 February.

Cultural background

The dish belongs to a dining room more than to a weekday kitchen, but the sauce is not difficult. The theatrics of a flame are optional. The orange and butter are not.

Why it tastes this way

Orange zest oils the butter. Sugar and juice make a syrup that coats a thin, slightly bitter crepe. A little liqueur sharpens the peel. If you burn the butter, the sauce turns harsh. The crepe should stay soft enough to fold, with a few browned spots.

How it is made

Thin crepes are cooked first. Butter, sugar, orange zest, and juice make a sauce in a wide pan, the crepes are turned in it, and a spoon of orange liqueur can be warmed and lit.

Authentic touch. Rub the sugar with the zest before it meets the butter, so the oil from the peel is in the sauce. If you light the liqueur, do it in the pan, never by pouring from the bottle.

How it is used

At once, as a dessert, folded in quarters. They are not a breakfast stack.

Field notes

  • The origin stories for the name Suzette are disputed.

  • These are a dessert service, not the Chandeleur custom itself.

Ingredients

  • 2 large eggs

  • 1 pinch fine salt

Two to three days of fermentation feed lactic acid bacteria and wild yeast, so the bread tastes sour rather than sweet. Teff itself is earthy and slightly nutty. Whisk teff flour and water into a batter like heavy cream and ferment it, covered, for 2 to 3 days until sour and bubbly. Salt it only after that rest. Authentic touch. Cook on one side only and leave the ayen open. Do not flip. If the eyes close under a second side of heat, the surface toughens and stops drinking the stew.

How it is used

Injera lines a platter under meats and vegetable stews, or under a fasting spread of lentils and greens. It is the accompaniment to the meal, not a side dish in a separate basket. Leftover bread is torn into firfir the next day.

Field notes

Baghrir belong to breakfast and to the hour when guests drink tea. They are sold at stalls and cooked at home in a small pan. The first cake of the batch is the test: if the holes do not open, the pan is too cool or the batter is too thick.

Fine semolina gives a faint grain sweetness that white flour alone does not. Yeast opens the crumb and leaves a mild ferment flavor. Butter and honey run into the holes, so each bite is rich and sweet on a bread that was not sugary in the batter. Cooking only one side keeps the holes intact.

Bloom yeast in warm water, then blend it with fine semolina, a little wheat flour, sugar, and salt until the batter is thin and silky. Rest it until foamy. How it is used

Baghrir are breakfast or a sweet snack, often with mint tea. They are eaten with a spoon once the butter and honey have soaked in. Savory toppings are a separate habit and not this recipe.

Field notes

How it is made

Chop a large bunch of herbs finely, squeeze them dry, and stir them into beaten eggs with turmeric, salt, a spoon of flour, chopped walnuts, and barberries. Cook the cake slowly in an oiled skillet, covered, until the center is set. Flip it or finish it under heat if the top is still wet.

Authentic touch. Call it a frittata, not a pancake. Use enough herbs that the batter looks green before it cooks. Walnuts mean this cake is not nut-free. If you cannot find barberries, leave them out rather than substituting a sweet dried fruit.

Plain dosa

Crisp at the edge, faintly sour, with a toasted rice smell and a soft underside.

40 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 dosas

History

Dosa is old in South India. The exact origin century is not settled, so this page does not name one. What the kitchens agree on is the batter: raw rice and skinned black gram, soaked, ground, salted, and fermented, then spread on a hot griddle. Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, and Kerala all keep that ferment and change the sides.

Cultural background

A home batter is judged by smell and bubbles. A warm night may finish it by morning. A cool kitchen needs more time near the stove. The sourness should stay gentle. Harsh smell or visible mold means the bowl is discarded.

Why it tastes this way

Rice supplies the starch that dries against iron and turns crisp. Urad dal brings protein and a soft stickiness so the sheet can spread without tearing. Fermentation adds a mild tang and the gas that opens lacy holes at the rim. A few fenugreek seeds, traditional in many batters, leave a faint bitter aroma under the toast.

How it is made

Soak rice and urad separately, grind them to different textures, ferment until the batter is lively, and cook thin rounds on a lightly oiled griddle.

Authentic touch. Spread from the center while the batter still flows. A pan that is too hot tears the sheet. A pan that is too cool glues it down and never crisps.

How it is used

Eat it at once with coconut chutney and sambar. A covered dosa steams itself and loses the crisp edge.

Field notes

  • Skinned black gram is the urad dal in this batter, sometimes sold as white urad.
  • The same fermented batter can be steamed as idli. The griddle is what makes the dosa.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup idli rice or other short-grain raw rice
  • 1/3 cup skinned urad dal (black gram)
  • 1/4 teaspoon fenugreek seeds
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
  • Water for soaking and grinding
  • Neutral oil for the griddle

Steps

  1. Rinse the rice. Rinse the urad with the fenugreek. Soak them in separate bowls for 4 to 6 hours.
  2. Drain and grind the urad with cold water to a light, fluffy paste. Grind the rice with water to a slightly grainy batter. Stir them together with the salt. The mix should thickly coat a spoon.
  3. Cover the bowl and ferment until it is bubbly and mildly sour, often 8 to 14 hours. In a cool room give it longer. This wait is not part of the active prep time. Discard the batter if it smells putrid or grows mold.
  4. Stir the batter down and loosen it with a spoon or two of water if it is too stiff to spread. Heat a griddle until a drop of water skitters. Grease it lightly.
  5. Pour a ladle in the center and spiral outward. Drizzle a little oil around the edge. Cook until the underside is spotted gold and the top looks dry. Fold and serve immediately.

Ferment in a clean bowl. Discard batter that smells rotten, turns an odd color, or shows mold.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Ferment a rice and urad batter, cook a dry spiced potato, spread a thin dosa, lay the potato in a line, and roll.

History

Rava dosa is a semolina batter, usually stirred and cooked without the long rice-and-urad ferment. Wheat semolina, often called rava or sooji, is the grain that builds the crisp web. Rice flour is there to help the sheet set. It became a restaurant standard because a cook can mix it to order when a fermented batter has run out. It is a different dosa from the fermented rice and urad kind.

Cultural background

The batter is meant to be thin and a little lumpy. Those lumps and the water around them are what leave the holes. A thick batter makes a pancake, which is not the point of this page.

Why it tastes this way

Semolina browns faster than a wet fermented rice batter and tastes toasted and wheat-y. Cumin and crushed black pepper sit on the surface instead of disappearing into a ferment. Curry leaf, dropped into hot oil or onto the batter, adds a sharp green note. The lack of a sour ferment is the flavor clue that this is rava dosa.

How it is made

Whisk semolina, rice flour, and water into a thin batter, season it, and pour it loosely onto a hot griddle so it laces.

Authentic touch. Pour the thin batter from the ladle in separate streams and let the holes stay open. Do not spiral it into a solid disk.

How it is used

Lift it off while it is still brittle. Coconut chutney is the usual partner. It softens if it waits in a stack.

Field notes

  • Rava here is wheat semolina, so this dosa is not gluten-free.
  • The lacy holes are a pouring method. They are not a sign of fermentation.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups cold water, plus more if needed
  • 1/2 teaspoon cumin seeds
  • 1/4 teaspoon crushed black pepper
  • 1 green chile, finely chopped
  • 8 curry leaves, torn
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Neutral oil for the griddle

Steps

  2. Stir in cumin, pepper, green chile, and curry leaves. Thin with a splash of water if it has thickened.
  3. Heat a griddle until very hot. Grease it lightly and pour the batter in separate ribbons rather than one tight spiral.
  4. Fill obvious gaps with a little more batter, then drizzle oil around the lace. Cook until the sheet is golden and rigid.
  5. Loosen the edge and lift the dosa off. Cook the rest, stirring the batter each time so the spices do not sink.

Soak rice, grind it with plenty of water to a watery batter, and cook soft rounds that can be rolled at once.

Appam

A bowl-shaped rice hopper with a lacy crisp rim, a spongy sweet center, and coconut in the batter.

35 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 appams

History

Appam is Kerala's fermented rice hopper, cooked in a curved appam pan so the center stays thick and the sides climb into a lace. Coconut milk is a usual part of the batter. Where toddy is still used, it is the ferment. This home recipe uses a pinch of yeast as the reliable stand-in and says so. Sri Lankan hoppers are a close cousin with their own page. Nei appam, the sweet ghee cake, is not this dish.

Cultural background

Kerala breakfasts pair appam with stew: vegetable, egg, or meat. The hopper itself is rice and coconut. A yeast ferment will not taste identical to a toddy ferment, and the page does not pretend it will. The bowl shape is non-negotiable.

Why it tastes this way

Rice and coconut milk make the center sweet and tender. Fermentation, whether from toddy or from yeast, adds a light sourness that keeps the coconut from tasting flat. The thin batter that climbs the hot wall of the pan dries into lace and picks up a toasted edge. Salt is the only savory note in the cake. The stew around it does the rest.

How it is made

Ferment a rice and coconut-milk batter and swirl it in a covered curved pan so it finishes bowl-shaped.

Authentic touch. Swirl the pan as soon as you pour, so a puddle remains in the center and only a film coats the sides. Then cover the pan. An open flat griddle cannot make an appam.

How it is used

Serve the bowls at once, center side up, with a mild vegetable stew. Meat stew is a common pairing and would add a meat allergen.

Field notes

  • The bowl shape comes from a curved appam pan and a swirl, not from a mold pressed into flat batter.
  • This savory hopper is a different cake from sweet nei appam.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup raw rice
  • 1/2 cup cooked rice
  • 1/2 cup coconut milk, plus more if needed
  • 1/4 teaspoon dry yeast
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Water as needed
  • Neutral oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Soak the raw rice for 3 hours. Drain and grind it with the cooked rice and a little water to a smooth batter.
  2. Stir in coconut milk, yeast, sugar, and salt. The batter should be thinner than dosa batter and thicker than milk.
  3. Cover and ferment until bubbly and lightly sour, often 6 to 10 hours in a warm kitchen. Active prep excludes that wait.
  4. Heat a curved appam pan, grease it, and pour a ladle of batter. Swirl so the sides take a thin coat and the center stays deep.

In a fonda, hotcakes are ordinary city breakfast, not a festival dish. The plate is familiar in Mexico City and in towns across the country, usually sweetened at the table rather than in the bowl.

The crumb tastes of wheat and browned butter, and baking powder keeps it soft if you stop stirring once the flour disappears. Cajeta, goat-milk caramel, is tangy and dark. How it is made

Whisk milk, egg, and melted butter. Stir them into the dry ingredients only until the flour is wet. Cook small rounds in a buttered skillet until the bubbles stay open, then flip once.

History

The corn tortilla is the foundational griddle cake of Mexico, older than wheat hotcakes by a very long stretch. The dough is masa: dried corn simmered with an alkali, rested, rinsed, and ground. That process is nixtamalization. Fresh nixtamal, ground wet, is the older method. Masa harina is dried nixtamalized corn, milled and sold as flour, and it is the practical home shortcut. Neither one is a bowl of plain untreated cornmeal.

Cultural background

A comal, a round griddle, sits on the stove in home kitchens and in tortillerías. Tortillas wrap food, accompany stews, and become the next day's chips or enchiladas. This recipe is the plain cake, just masa and water.

Why it tastes this way

Nixtamal gives the tortilla its corn aroma and a slight mineral taste that plain cornmeal never has. The alkali loosens the skins, changes the flavor, and makes the amino acid niacin more available. Cooked dry, the surface smells toasted. There is no wheat gluten to make it bready, so the cake stays flexible only while it is warm and moist.

How it is made

Stir masa harina with warm water and salt, rest it covered, and press thin rounds. Cook on a hot dry comal until the tortilla releases, flips, and puffs in spots.

Authentic touch. The dough should feel like soft clay that does not crack at the edge and does not glue itself to your palm. If it cracks, add water a teaspoon at a time. If it sticks, knead in a sprinkle of masa harina. Keep the comal dry.

How it is used

Eat them warm, wrapped in a cloth so they steam themselves soft. They are the plate under eggs, beans, salsas, and stews, and they are a meal by themselves with salt.

Field notes

  • A tortilla that puffs has enough moisture and a hot enough comal. The puff is steam, not baking powder.
  • Masa harina is already nixtamalized. Do not add cal again, and do not swap in untreated cornmeal.
  • Corn tortillas in this recipe are masa and water. Lard belongs to some flour tortillas, not to this dough.

  4. Cook on a dry comal over medium-high heat for about 45 seconds, flip, cook the second side, then flip once more until the tortilla smells toasted and puffs in places.
  5. Wrap the tortillas in a clean cloth as they come off the heat so they stay soft.

Precooked cornmeal cooks up dense and corny, with a skin that toasts and a center that stays steamy if the dough was wet enough. In Antioquia the everyday arepa is a thin white-corn round, cooked on a griddle and set beside eggs, beans, or hogao. Cheese may be mixed into the dough. Butter on a hot arepa is a normal breakfast. This is a different plate from the thick split arepa of a Venezuelan counter. Both are corn cakes, and corn cakes in this part of the world are older than wheat hotcakes. The thin Antioquia cake is not a fresh-corn arepa de choclo and not a Mexican tortilla. Precooked cornmeal is the home flour, not nixtamalized masa.

Cultural background

Medellín breakfasts often include an arepa, a small cup of coffee, and eggs. Hogao, scallions and tomato cooked in oil with cumin, is the sauce that shows up next to beans and rice as well. This recipe puts a little cheese in the dough and serves the cake whole, with butter and hogao, rather than splitting it into a sandwich.

Why it tastes this way

A thinner cake dries and toasts more than a thick Caracas arepa, so you taste browned corn at the surface. Cheese in the dough adds salt and a little fat, which helps the round brown. Butter on the hot cake smells milky. Hogao is sweet from cooked tomato, green from scallion, and warm from cumin, without chile heat. The plate is savory and mild.

How it is made

Mix precooked white cornmeal, warm water, salt, and grated cheese. Pat thin rounds, griddle them, and serve with butter and a cooked hogao of scallion, tomato, and cumin.

Authentic touch. Use a bit more water than you would for a thick stuffed arepa, and pat the rounds thinner, under a centimeter. Cook them longer than you think, until the outside is dry and spotted. Hogao should be cooked until the tomato collapses, not served as a raw salsa.

How it is used

Serve the arepas whole at breakfast, with butter melting on top and hogao on the side. Eggs and red beans can share the plate. They are not folded over cheese the way a cachapa is.

Field notes

  • Antioquia's daily arepa is usually thinner than a stuffed Caracas arepa and is often left whole.
  • Hogao is cooked scallion and tomato. It is not a raw Mexican salsa.
  • The flour is precooked cornmeal, not masa harina and not fresh choclo.

Ingredients

  • 200 g precooked white cornmeal
  • 300 ml warm water
  • 3/4 teaspoon salt, divided
  • 80 g grated fresh white cheese
  • 2 scallions, chopped
  • 1 ripe tomato, chopped
  • 1 tablespoon oil, plus butter for the table
  • 1/4 teaspoon ground cumin

Steps

  1. Stir the cornmeal, 1/2 teaspoon salt, cheese, and warm water into a soft dough that can be patted thin. Rest 5 minutes.
  2. Fry the scallions in the oil, add the tomato, cumin, and remaining salt, and cook until the tomato collapses. That is the hogao.
  3. Pat 6 thin rounds. Cook them on a griddle over medium heat until both sides are dry and spotted, several minutes a side.
  4. The cake should be cooked through, not doughy in the center.
  5. Serve whole, with butter and hogao.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Arepa de choclo, on its own page, is fresh corn and a different batter.
  • A coastal arepa de huevo is fried with an egg inside and is not vegetarian.
  • Leave the cheese and butter out, and cook the cake in oil, if you need it dairy-free and vegan.

Brazilian tapioca crepes

A lacy crepe of hydrated tapioca starch, folded over fresh cheese, chewy and mild.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 crepes

History

Street tapioca in Recife and across the Brazilian Northeast is hydrated tapioca starch, called goma, scattered in a skillet until it sets into a crepe and folded over cheese or coconut. The starch is commercial and already processed. Beiju, the plain Indigenous tapioca flatbread, is the older relative and has its own page. This one is the filled street crepe. It is gluten-free if nothing else is added. It is not a wheat panqueca and not a corn arepa.

Cultural background

Corner stands keep moist goma in a bowl, sieve it into a hot pan, and fill the round while it is still flexible. Cheese is the usual breakfast filling. Coconut and condensed milk are the sweet ones. This recipe uses cheese.

Why it tastes this way

Tapioca starch has almost no flavor of its own, so the crepe tastes clean, slightly sweet, and chewy, with a few toasted spots where the pan was hottest. Salt wakes it up. Fresh cheese is the savory part, milky and a little tangy, and it softens against the heat. There is no egg and no wheat, which is why the texture is elastic rather than bready.

How it is made

Sieve moist commercial tapioca starch into a nonstick skillet in a thin even layer, let it set, add cheese, fold, and turn once.

Authentic touch. The goma should feel like damp sand. If you only have dry commercial tapioca starch, sprinkle on just enough water to moisten it, then push it through a sieve into the pan. Do not stir it like a batter. Let the grains lace together.

How it is used

Eat it immediately, folded, at breakfast or from a street stand. It toughens as it cools. Coconut filling is the sweet alternative and is not in this batch.

Field notes

  • Use commercial goma de tapioca. Raw bitter cassava can be poisonous, and this recipe does not teach home processing.
  • The crepe is starch grains that fuse in the pan. There is no wheat batter to stir.
  • Cheese tapioca is a Northeast street breakfast, especially in Recife.

Ingredients

  • 200 g commercial hydrated tapioca starch (goma de tapioca)
  • Up to 80 ml water, only if the starch is dry
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 120 g sliced fresh white cheese

Steps

  1. If the commercial starch is dry, work in just enough water that it feels like damp sand. If it is already hydrated goma, use it as it is. Toss with the salt.
  2. Heat a nonstick skillet over medium heat. Do not add oil.
  3. Sieve a thin layer of starch into the pan, covering the bottom. Let it sit until the grains fuse into a crepe and the underside spots.
  4. Lay on the cheese, fold, and turn once until the cheese softens.
  5. Serve at once and repeat.

Use commercial goma de tapioca or packaged tapioca starch. Raw bitter cassava can be poisonous. This recipe does not teach home processing of raw cassava.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Grated coconut and a little salt, without cheese, can be dairy-free if the starch was moistened with water only.
  • Condensed milk is a sweet street filling and keeps the dairy allergen.
  • Beiju is the unfilled flatbread. Do not confuse the two plates.

Argentine panqueques

Thin milk crepes rolled around dulce de leche, with a browned-butter edge.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 crepes

History

Panqueques in Buenos Aires are thin wheat crepes, a dessert and a Sunday treat, filled most often with dulce de leche. The batter is flour, egg, and milk, the crepe family that spread through the Río de la Plata in the twentieth century along with other European kitchen habits. They are not corn cakes. Corn cakes elsewhere in South America are older than wheat hotcakes, and these are frankly a later wheat dessert. Dulce de leche, milk and sugar cooked until tan, is the filling people expect.

Cultural background

Home cooks in Buenos Aires make a stack of panqueques for dessert or for a late breakfast, then roll them while they are warm. Bakeries sell them already filled. The crepe is a vehicle. The jar of dulce de leche is the flavor most of the table is waiting for.

Why it tastes this way

A Buenos Aires panqueque is a thin egg batter, and the sweetness stays in the filling. Dulce de leche tastes of caramel and cooked cream, thick enough to stay in a ribbon when the crepe is rolled warm. The batter itself should taste lightly of milk and browned butter, not of sugar. A pinch of salt keeps the milk from tasting flat.

How it is made

Whisk flour, egg, milk, melted butter, and salt. Rest the batter, cook thin crepes in a buttered pan, and roll them around dulce de leche.

Authentic touch. Cook the crepes thin enough to see the pan through the batter, and fill them while they are still flexible. Cold crepes crack. Warm the dulce de leche if it is too stiff to spread, without boiling it.

How it is used

Serve them warm, two to a plate, as dessert or a sweet breakfast. A dust of sugar is optional and is not required once the filling is in.

Field notes

  • In Buenos Aires the usual filling is dulce de leche, milk cooked with sugar until it turns tan.
  • The batter is a thin wheat crepe. It is not a corn arepa and not a fritter.
  • Roll the crepes while they are warm so they bend instead of cracking.

Ingredients

  • 100 g wheat flour
  • 1 egg
  • 250 ml milk
  • 1 tablespoon melted butter, plus more for the pan
  • Pinch of salt
  • 200 g dulce de leche

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, egg, milk, melted butter, and salt into a thin batter. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Heat a skillet, film it with butter, and pour in just enough batter to coat the bottom. Swirl.
  3. Cook until the edge lifts and the underside spots, then flip and cook briefly.
  4. Spread dulce de leche on the warm crepe and roll it.
  5. Repeat with the rest of the batter and serve warm.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Dulce de membrillo, quince paste, is another Río de la Plata filling. The Uruguayan page uses it on purpose.
  • A savory ham filling adds meat and is not vegetarian.
  • Orange zest in the batter is a home perfume. It does not replace the dulce de leche.

Ecuador · South America

Llapingachos

Pan-fried potato cakes with cheese inside and a mild peanut sauce over the top.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 cakes

History

Llapingachos are pan-fried potato cakes from the Ecuadorian Andes, especially the central highlands around Ambato. Mashed potato is stuffed with cheese, browned in a skillet, and often served with salsa de maní, a peanut sauce. Peanuts are an Andean crop. This recipe includes the sauce, so the plate contains peanut and is not nut-free. The cheese makes it a dairy dish. A fried egg or a sausage on the side is common and is not in the written steps.

Cultural background

They are breakfast and lunch in highland towns, sold in markets and cooked at home when potatoes are the meal. Annatto colors some batches a soft orange. The sauce is onion, roasted peanut, and milk, not a fiery chile paste. Ají can be served beside it. It is not mixed in here.

Why it tastes this way

Potato and salt are the body, fried so the outside browns and the cheese in the center softens. The peanut sauce tastes toasted and slightly sweet, loosened with milk and onion. Annatto adds color and a faint earthy note, not heat. Because there is no chile in the sauce, the dish stays at no chili heat. The peanut is obvious. Anyone who avoids peanuts should not make this version.

How it is made

Mash boiled potatoes with salt and a little annatto. Seal cubes of cheese inside cakes and pan-fry them. Cook onion and ground roasted peanuts with milk into a sauce and spoon it over.

Authentic touch. Chill the mashed potato before you stuff it, or the cheese leaks before a crust can form. Seal the cakes well. Simmer the peanut sauce until it is thick enough to spoon, not a broth, and salt it.

How it is used

Serve two cakes with sauce for breakfast or lunch. A fried egg on top adds the egg allergen. Chorizo adds meat and is not vegetarian. Neither is in this pan.

Field notes

  • The peanut sauce means this recipe is not nut-free.
  • Cheese inside the potato cake softens. It does not have to stretch like a pizza.
  • Ambato and the central highlands are the landscape people name with llapingachos.

Ingredients

  • 700 g potatoes, peeled
  • 1 teaspoon salt, divided
  • Pinch of ground annatto
  • 120 g fresh white cheese, in 8 cubes
  • 80 g roasted peanuts
  • 1 small onion, chopped
  • 120 ml milk
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil

Steps

  1. Boil the potatoes until tender. Drain, mash, and season with 1/2 teaspoon salt and the annatto. Cool until you can handle the mash.
  2. Divide into 8 portions. Seal a cube of cheese inside each and flatten into a thick cake.
  3. Pan-fry in the oil over medium heat until both sides are browned and the center is hot.
  4. Meanwhile, cook the onion in a spoon of oil, add the peanuts, milk, and remaining salt, and simmer. Mash or blend the sauce until thick.
  5. Spoon the peanut sauce over the cakes and serve.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Skip the peanut sauce and the allergen list loses peanut, and the plate can be nut-free. It is a plainer dish.
  • A fried egg is a usual topping and adds egg.
  • Chorizo on the side adds meat and is not vegetarian.

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Breakfast
  • Street food
  • Ají on the side, which adds heat
  • A small salad of onion and tomato
  • Black coffee
  • Vegetarian

Paraguay · South America

Paraguayan mbeju

A crisp griddled cake of manioc starch, cornmeal, and cheese, salty at the edge and tender in the middle.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 1 large cake

History

Mbeju is a Paraguayan breakfast cake made from manioc starch, corn flour, and cheese, cooked on a griddle until the edge crisps. Manioc, cassava, is an old staple along the Paraguay River. The starch in a modern kitchen is commercial, already processed. This recipe uses that starch and does not teach you to grind raw cassava. Cheese and butter make it a dairy dish. It is often eaten with cocido, a hot yerba mate infusion, which is a drink beside the plate and not an ingredient in the dough.

Cultural background

In Asunción and in the countryside, mbeju is breakfast and an afternoon bite, sometimes cooked in a broad pan so several people can break pieces off. The dough is crumbly when it goes into the pan and sets as the starch heats. Corn flour keeps it from being only elastic tapioca.

Why it tastes this way

Manioc starch goes chewy and then crisp as it dries on the griddle. Cornmeal adds a toasted grain note. Fresh cheese salts the cake and browns in spots. Butter helps the edge fry. There is no sugar and no chile, so the flavor stays savory and mild. Cocido beside it is smoky and bitter, which is why the two share a table.

How it is made

Rub commercial manioc starch with cornmeal, grated fresh cheese, butter, salt, and a splash of milk. Press the crumb into a skillet and cook slowly until the bottom crisps and the center sets. Flip once if it will hold.

Authentic touch. Mix the starch, cornmeal, cheese, and butter to a damp crumb, not a pourable batter. Press it into a hot pan and let it set before you touch it. Turning too soon breaks the cake. The cheese should be fresh and salty, not a hard aged cheese that stays in pebbles.

How it is used

Break it into wedges at breakfast, with cocido or coffee. It is a bread and a cheese dish at the same time.

Field notes

  • Use commercial manioc starch. Raw bitter cassava can be poisonous, and this recipe does not teach home processing.
  • Mbeju is starch, corn, and cheese on a griddle, a Paraguayan breakfast.
  • Cocido is the drink beside it, not a liquid in the dough.

Ingredients

  • 150 g commercial manioc starch (cassava starch)
  • 80 g fine cornmeal
  • 100 g grated fresh cheese
  • 30 g butter
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons milk

Steps

  1. Rub the manioc starch, cornmeal, cheese, butter, and salt together until the mixture looks like damp crumbs. Sprinkle on the milk and mix. It should clump, not pour.
  2. Heat a skillet over medium-low heat and press the crumb in an even layer.
  3. Cook without stirring until the bottom is crisp and the center has set, about 10 minutes.
  4. Flip in one piece if you can, or cut it in the pan and turn the pieces. Cook until the second side spots.
  5. Serve warm, in wedges.

Use commercial manioc (cassava) starch. Raw bitter cassava can be poisonous. This recipe does not teach home processing of raw cassava.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pork fat instead of butter is used in some kitchens. It adds meat and is not vegetarian.
  • More cornmeal makes a mealier cake. More starch makes it chewier.
  • An egg in the dough adds the egg allergen and is not required here.

  • Accompaniment to a meal

Flying Pancakes of the World

Chile · South America

Chilean sopaipillas

Fried pumpkin rounds, crisp and puffed, eaten with pebre or with chancaca syrup.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · 12 sopaipillas

History

Chilean sopaipillas are pumpkin fried flat cakes, the food people make in the central valley when it rains. The dough is mashed zapallo, winter squash, and wheat flour, rolled thin and fried. They are fried. A crepe filled with manjar is a different page. Sopaipillas are eaten two ways on the same day: with pebre, the herb and chile relish, or with chancaca syrup, unrefined cane sugar simmered with orange peel and cinnamon. This recipe includes both finishes. The pebre has a mild chile, so the heat is mild.

Cultural background

Rain in Talca, Santiago, and the towns of the central valley is the usual excuse to fry a batch. They are street food in winter and home food the same afternoon. The pumpkin keeps the dough soft. The fry makes them puff.

Why it tastes this way

Pumpkin sweetens the wheat from the inside, and frying browns that sugar at the edge. The center stays a little bready. Pebre is sharp: cilantro, onion, tomato, vinegar, and a small mild chile. Chancaca syrup is dark and spicy from cinnamon and orange, not from chile. One batch of fritters can go either direction. The cake itself has no dairy and no egg.

How it is made

Mix mashed pumpkin with wheat flour, salt, and a little oil. Roll thin rounds and fry them. Serve with pebre, with chancaca syrup, or with both.

Authentic touch. Mash the pumpkin until it is dryish, not watery, or the dough will drink oil. Roll the rounds thin. Fry in enough oil that they bob and puff. Drain them before the syrup, or the chancaca cannot cling.

How it is used

Eat them hot, the day they are fried, especially when the weather is wet. Pebre makes them a snack with a drink. Chancaca makes them dessert. Both are traditional.

Field notes

  • Sopaipillas are fried. Rainy afternoons in central Chile are when many kitchens make them.
  • Pebre is the savory relish. Chancaca syrup is the sweet one. The fritter can take either.
  • Zapallo, winter squash, is the vegetable in the dough, not a potato.

Ingredients

  • 250 g cooked, well-drained pumpkin puree
  • 250 g wheat flour, plus more for rolling
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil in the dough, plus oil for frying
  • 1 small mild chile, chopped, for the pebre
  • 1/2 cup chopped cilantro, 1/4 onion, 1 tomato, 1 tablespoon vinegar, and a pinch of salt for the pebre
  • 150 g chancaca or panela, 200 ml water, 1 cinnamon stick, and a strip of orange peel for the syrup

Steps

  1. Stir the pumpkin, flour, salt, and 1 tablespoon oil into a soft dough. Rest 10 minutes. Add flour only if it sticks badly.
  2. Chop the chile, cilantro, onion, and tomato. Stir in the vinegar and a pinch of salt. That is the pebre.
  3. Simmer the chancaca, water, cinnamon, and orange peel until the sugar dissolves and the syrup thickens slightly.
  4. Roll the dough thin and cut 12 rounds. Fry in hot oil, turning once, until they puff and turn deep gold.
  5. Drain and serve with pebre, with warm chancaca syrup, or with both.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some cooks add a pinch of baking powder so the rounds puff higher. This dough relies on moisture and a hot fry.
  • Lard in the dough or the fryer adds meat and is not vegetarian. This recipe uses oil.
  • Skip the pebre and the plate has no chile heat. The written recipe includes it, so the heat stays mild.

Pairings and uses

  • Snack
  • Street food
  • A bowl of pebre
  • Warm chancaca syrup
  • Tea on a rainy afternoon

Cuba · Caribbean

Cuban torrejas

Day-old bread soaked in egg and milk, fried, and finished with cinnamon syrup and a splash of wine.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 torrejas

History

Cuban torrejas are a Christmas and New Year bread dish. Stale bread is soaked in egg and milk, fried until the outside sets, and bathed in syrup that may include a splash of wine. They are a relative of French toast. They are not a batter pancake, and they are not masa. The bread was already baked. The holiday work is the soak, the pan, and the syrup.

Cultural background

Families in Havana and across Cuba make torrejas when the bakeries have day-old bread and the table is set for Nochebuena or the days after. The dish uses eggs and milk on purpose, a rich plate for a short season.

Why it tastes this way

Stale bread has lost moisture, so it drinks the custard instead of falling apart. Frying gives the crust a browned egg flavor while the center stays soft. The syrup is cinnamon, citrus peel, and sugar, with wine adding a dry edge so the plate is not only sweet. Milk in the soak tastes plain and creamy against the spice.

How it is made

Whisk eggs, milk, sugar, and cinnamon. Soak stale bread, fry it in a shallow layer of oil, and finish it in a light syrup of sugar, water, cinnamon, lime peel, and a little dry wine.

Authentic touch. Use bread that is dry all the way through. Fresh bread turns to paste in the egg. Soak it long enough to reach the center, then let the extra drip off before it goes into the pan.

How it is used

Serve warm on a holiday dessert plate, with extra syrup. They are not a weekday breakfast batter. They belong to the Christmas table.

Field notes

  • Torrejas are fried soaked bread, a holiday relative of French toast, not a poured batter.
  • Day-old bread is the right start. Very fresh bread collapses in the egg.
  • The wine goes in the syrup. It is not there to ferment a dough.

Ingredients

  • 8 slices day-old white bread
  • 2 eggs
  • 180 ml milk
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1 teaspoon ground cinnamon, divided
  • Neutral oil or butter for the pan
  • 150 g sugar and 160 ml water for the syrup
  • 1 cinnamon stick, a strip of lime peel, and 2 tablespoons dry white wine

Steps

  1. Whisk the eggs, milk, 1 tablespoon sugar, and 1/2 teaspoon ground cinnamon. Lay the bread in the mixture and turn it until the centers are moist. Let the extra drip off.
  2. Simmer 150 g sugar, the water, the cinnamon stick, and the lime peel for 5 minutes. Add the wine and take the syrup off the heat.
  3. Heat a thin layer of oil or butter in a skillet over medium heat.
  4. Fry the soaked bread until both sides are browned and the egg is set. Do not crowd the pan.
  5. Spoon the warm syrup over the torrejas and serve them for dessert.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Skip the wine and keep the syrup to sugar, water, cinnamon, and lime peel.
  • Anise can replace the cinnamon in some family pots.
  • A sweeter enriched bread makes a richer torreja and still starts from bread, not from pancake batter.

Pairings and uses

  • Feast or fast day
  • Dessert
  • Strong coffee after the meal
  • A slice of guava paste, if the table already has it
  • The syrup left in the pan
  • Vegetarian

Jamaican bammy

A dense, mild cassava round, dry and toasted on the outside, then fried so the edge crisps.

15 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 bammies

History

Bammy is a Jamaican cassava flatbread, long associated with St. Elizabeth parish and with roadside plates of fried fish. Cassava bread in the Caribbean is older than the wheat johnny cakes that came later. The root itself has to be processed before it is safe to eat. This recipe starts from commercial cassava flour, which is already processed, or you may pan-fry a packaged bammy. It does not teach you to peel, grate, and press raw cassava at home.

Cultural background

Cooks griddle the dough into thick rounds, and many then dip the cake in salted water or coconut milk and fry it to serve. The plain griddled round is the bread. The fry is the usual plate next to fish. This version leaves the fish off so the recipe stays vegan.

Why it tastes this way

Commercial cassava flour tastes clean, starchy, and slightly sweet, without the toasted corn of a tortilla or the gluten of wheat. Salt is almost the only seasoning in the dough. Frying after a brief soak gives a soft center and a crisp rim. If you stop after the dry griddle, the bammy is firmer and better for soaking up sauce.

How it is made

Mix commercial cassava flour with salt and water into a stiff dough. Flatten thick rounds, griddle them dry, dip briefly in salted water, and pan-fry.

Authentic touch. Press the rounds firmly so they do not crack at the edge, and cook them on a dry skillet until the surface looks dry before you even think about the fry. A wet center will not finish in the oil.

How it is used

Serve hot beside fried fish, callaloo, or a stew. The bammy is the bread on the plate. Packaged bammy can be dipped and fried the same way if you are not making the dough.

Field notes

  • Raw bitter cassava can be poisonous. This page uses commercial cassava flour and does not teach home processing.
  • Bammy is often fried after the first cook, which is why it shows up next to fried fish.
  • There is no wheat in this dough, so it is gluten-free if the flour was not milled with wheat.

Ingredients

  • 280 g commercial cassava flour
  • 1 teaspoon salt, plus more for the dipping water
  • 200 ml water
  • 250 ml water for a brief dip
  • Neutral oil for pan-frying

Steps

  1. Stir the commercial cassava flour, 1 teaspoon salt, and 200 ml water into a stiff dough. If it cracks, add a spoon of water.
  2. Divide into 4 pieces and press each into a round about 1 centimeter thick.
  3. Cook on a dry skillet over medium heat for 6 to 8 minutes a side, until the surface is dry and spotted.
  4. Dip each round for a few seconds in salted water, then drain.
  5. Pan-fry in a thin layer of oil until the outside is crisp. Serve hot.

Raw bitter cassava can be poisonous. This recipe uses commercial cassava flour, or a packaged bammy, and does not teach home processing of raw cassava.

  • Soak the griddled round in coconut milk instead of salted water before frying.
  • Eat it after the dry griddle, without the fry, if you want a firmer bread.
  • A packaged bammy, already cooked and sold in rounds, can be dipped and fried. Read the package if you need to confirm it is only cassava.

Flying Pancakes of the World

Australia · Oceania

Australian pikelets

Small, thick drop pancakes with a soft crumb, eaten with butter and jam at tea.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · About 16 small pikelets

History

Australian pikelets are small thick pancakes dropped by the spoon, a British inheritance on the tea table. Drop scones and pikelets traveled with British settlers and stayed as afternoon-tea food, the kind made for a visit or a church hall. They are not the large thin pancake. That cake has its own page, with a looser batter, a wider pan, and lemon and sugar. Pikelets are the small ones, buttered and jammed.

Cultural background

A pikelet pan is an ordinary frying pan and a tablespoon. The batter is stiff enough that the spoonful keeps a small round and rises thick. Jam and butter are the spread. They are home food more than restaurant food, and they are not an Indigenous Australian dish.

Why it tastes this way

Baking powder lifts a thick batter fast, so the crumb is soft and cakey. Egg and milk taste rich. Sugar in the batter is light, and the jam supplies the fruit. Because the cakes are small, the browned face is a big part of each bite. They do not taste of lemon, and they are not rolled.

How it is made

Stir flour, baking powder, sugar, and salt, then fold in egg, milk, and melted butter to a thick batter. Drop tablespoons onto a moderate griddle and cook until bubbles hold and the underside is brown. Flip once. Serve with butter and jam.

Authentic touch. Drop them small and keep the batter thick. If the cakes spread to the edge of a wide pan, you have left pikelet territory and started the larger pancake. Butter and jam belong on the tea plate.

How it is used

Pikelets are a tea-table snack, not a plated dinner dessert and not a savory meal. Butter and jam are the usual spreads. Cream is a richer extra and still dairy, which the batter already contains.

Field notes

  • Pikelets are small and thick. They are not the large Australian pancake.
  • They come from British drop-scone cooking and sit on the tea table.
  • Butter and jam are the usual spread.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup wheat flour (about 125 g)
  • 2 teaspoons baking powder
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon fine salt
  • 1 egg
  • 3/4 cup milk
  • 1 tablespoon melted unsalted butter, plus more for the pan

Steps

  1. Stir the flour, baking powder, sugar, and salt in a bowl.
  2. Add the egg, milk, and melted butter. Stir to a thick batter with a few small lumps left. Do not thin it into a crepe.
  3. Heat a griddle over medium heat and butter it lightly. Drop tablespoons of batter. They should stay small and thick.
  4. Cook until bubbles sit on the surface and the bottoms are brown, then flip once and cook the second side.
  5. Serve warm with butter and jam. These are the tea pikelets, not the larger lemon-and-sugar pancakes.

The batter contains wheat, egg, and dairy. Do not confuse these with a gluten-free cake.

Pikelets are best the day they are cooked. Wrap leftovers and reheat them briefly in a covered pan. Refrigerate if you are keeping them overnight. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of golden syrup in the batter makes a deeper sweetness. This batch uses sugar.
  • Whole-wheat flour makes a heartier pikelet and still contains wheat.
  • Lemon and sugar is the finish for the larger Australian pancake, not for these.

Pairings and uses

  • Butter
  • Jam
  • Tea
  • Vegetarian

Samoan panikeke

Round fried cakes, lightly sweet, with mashed banana in a soft wheat crumb.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · About 12 fried rounds

History

Panikeke are Samoan round fried cakes, and the name comes from the English word pancake. They are often made with mashed banana, baking powder, and flour, then fried into rounds for breakfast, for church gatherings, and for the shop counter. The borrowed name already places them in the period when English words and wheat flour were part of island cooking. They are not a pre-contact dish. This recipe fries them, which is how they are cooked, and keeps the banana in the dough.

Cultural background

In Apia and in villages, panikeke are a batch food: one bowl, one pan of oil, a plate that empties fast. Ripe banana is the fruit most often mashed in. They are a snack more than a ceremony. The shape is a round fritter, not a thin crepe.

Why it tastes this way

Banana sweetens the dough and keeps it moist in the middle while the oil browns the outside. Wheat and baking powder make a soft, bready crumb rather than a fruit leather. Sugar is light, so the banana remains the sweetener you taste. Salt at the edge of the dough keeps it from eating flat.

How it is made

Mash a ripe banana and stir it with flour, baking powder, sugar, salt, and a little water into a soft dough. Shape rounds and fry them in oil until golden and cooked through. Drain and eat warm.

Authentic touch. Fry rounds, and keep the banana in the dough if you are cooking this version. The name comes from pancake, but the method is a fritter. A thin griddled crepe is not panikeke.

How it is used

Panikeke are a snack and a breakfast cake, at home and at gatherings. They are eaten plain or with a little butter. Butter adds dairy and is not in this dough. They are not a savory stew bread.

Field notes

  • The name panikeke comes from the English word pancake.
  • The cakes are round and fried, often with banana.
  • This recipe is a fritter, not a thin batter cooked on a dry griddle.

Ingredients

  • 1 ripe banana, mashed
  • 1 cup wheat flour (about 125 g)
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon fine salt
  • 1/4 cup water
  • Oil for frying

Steps

  1. Mash the banana until smooth. Stir in the flour, baking powder, sugar, salt, and water to make a soft dough you can shape.
  2. Rest the dough for 10 minutes.
  3. Shape small rounds with floured hands.
  4. Fry the rounds in hot oil, turning once, until they are golden and the center is cooked. Panikeke are fried cakes, and the name comes from pancake.
  5. Drain and serve warm.

The dough contains wheat. Frying oil is hot. There is no egg and no dairy in the dough. Butter on the side would add dairy.

Eat panikeke the day they are fried. Refrigerate leftovers and reheat them in a hot oven. The banana crumb does not stay crisp. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Without banana, the dough is a plainer fried round and less like the usual panikeke.
  • A spoon of mashed pumpkin is another home mash and a different flavor.
  • Egg or milk in the dough changes the allergens. This batch has neither.

Pairings and uses

  • Snack
  • Breakfast
  • Tea or cocoa
  • A ripe banana on the side
  • Butter, which adds dairy
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan

  • Nut-free

Parāoa parai

Fried wheat bread, puffed and crisp at the rim, with a soft, salty interior.

20 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 5 · About 10 fried rounds

History

Parāoa parai is Māori fried bread. Parāoa is the word for flour and for bread, and parai means fried. Wheat flour reached Māori kitchens through trade and the colonial economy, including the long pressure on Māori land. This is not a pre-contact food. It is a fried dough that became ordinary kai, cooked at home and for gatherings. The pin is Whanganui. The bread is cooked in Māori homes well beyond that river. This recipe fries a simple baking-powder dough.

Cultural background

Parāoa parai is often on the table beside other dishes, including food from a hāngī, an earth oven. The bread is the recipe here. The hāngī is not. It is also weeknight food, torn and eaten hot. Nothing about the dough requires a performance or a costume. It is bread, fried.

Why it tastes this way

Hot oil puffs the dough and browns the outside, so the flavor is toasted wheat and salt. Baking powder opens the interior so it is not a hard cracker. There is no egg and no sugar in this version, which keeps it savory. A sweet condiment, if someone adds it, sits on that salt. The colonial pantry is audible in the taste: white flour and frying fat.

How it is made

Mix flour, baking powder, salt, and water into a soft dough. Rest it briefly, pat it into small rounds, and fry in oil until puffed and golden. Drain and serve hot. Milk is a common variant and is not in this dairy-free batch.

Authentic touch. Fry the dough, and describe the flour honestly. It comes from the colonial economy and is not a pre-contact ingredient. Do not decorate the recipe with stereotypes. The method is a plain dough, cut or pulled, and fried.

How it is used

Parāoa parai is a snack, an accompaniment, and a bread at gatherings and at home. It can sit beside a hāngī. It is not a sweet festival cake unless someone adds a sweet condiment. This dough is vegan and contains wheat.

Field notes

  • Parāoa parai means fried bread. The flour is wheat from the colonial economy.
  • It is not a pre-contact food.
  • A hāngī is an earth oven. This recipe is only the fried bread that may be served beside one.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups wheat flour (about 250 g)
  • 2 teaspoons baking powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
  • 3/4 cup water
  • Oil for frying

Steps

  1. Stir the flour, baking powder, and salt. Add the water and mix to a soft dough. There is no milk in this batch.
  2. Knead gently for a minute and rest the dough, covered, for 10 minutes.
  3. Pat it into small rounds about a finger thick. This is fried bread, not a batter pancake.
  4. Heat a few centimeters of oil. Fry the rounds, turning once, until they puff and turn golden and the center is cooked.
  5. Drain and serve hot.

The dough contains wheat. Frying oil is hot and should not be left. This recipe does not use milk. Butter at the table is a separate dairy choice.

Fried bread is best hot. Refrigerate leftovers and reheat them in a hot pan or oven so the surface crisps again. Do not store them in the oil. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Milk in the dough is a softer bread and adds dairy. This recipe uses water.
  • A longer rest makes the dough easier to pat. It does not ferment.
  • Pulling the rounds thinner gives a crisper fry. Thicker rounds stay bready inside.

Pairings and uses

  • Snack
  • Feast or fast day
  • Accompaniment to a meal
  • A savory meal of vegetables or meat, as the table requires
  • Golden syrup, for a sweet bite, which is optional
  • Butter, which adds dairy and is not in the dough
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Fijian babakau

Soft yeasted dough, fried, with a mild sweetness and a pale, airy center.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 5 · About 10 fried pieces

History

Babakau is Fijian fried dough for tea. A yeasted wheat dough is rested, pulled into pieces, and fried. It is shop food and home food, eaten with tea in Suva and far beyond it. Wheat flour, yeast, and tea mark it as a food from the period when those goods were already in Fijian kitchens. It is not an ancient dish, and this page does not call it one. The method is frying, so the base here is a fritter even though the dough is yeasted.

Cultural background

Tea in Fiji can mean a meal, not only a drink, and babakau belongs on that table. Homes fry a batch for guests. Shops sell them through the day. The dough is plain. Flavor comes from the fry and from whatever is served beside it, often butter or a sweet spread.

Why it tastes this way

Yeast gives a light, slightly fermented smell and a soft hole in the center. Frying browns only the outside, so the inside tastes of wheat and a little sugar rather than of crust. Salt keeps the sweetness in check. There is no banana and no coconut in this batch, which sets it apart from other island fried cakes that use fruit.

How it is made

Mix flour, yeast, sugar, salt, and water into a soft dough and let it rise until puffy. Divide it, flatten the pieces lightly, and fry them in oil until golden and cooked through. Drain and serve with tea.

Authentic touch. Fry a yeasted dough and serve it for tea. Do not describe babakau as ancient. A baking-powder dough will fry, and it will not have the same slow rise this recipe uses.

How it is used

Babakau is a snack and a breakfast or tea bread. It is eaten plain, with butter, or with a sweet spread. Butter adds dairy. The dough written here does not contain it. It is not a ceremonial loaf.

Field notes

  • Babakau is fried dough for tea in Fiji.
  • The dough is yeasted, and the cooking method is frying.
  • It belongs to the era of wheat flour and tea. It is not an ancient dish.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups wheat flour (about 250 g)
  • 1 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
  • 3/4 cup warm water
  • Oil for frying

Steps

  1. Stir the flour, yeast, sugar, and salt. Add the warm water and mix to a soft dough. Knead until smooth.
  2. Cover and let it rise until puffy, about 45 to 60 minutes. This is a yeasted dough, and it is not an ancient recipe.
  3. Divide the dough into 10 pieces and flatten each slightly.
  4. Heat the oil. Fry the pieces, turning once, until they are golden and cooked through. Babakau is fried dough for tea.
  5. Drain and serve warm.

The dough contains wheat. Hot oil should not be left alone. There is no dairy in the dough unless you add butter at the table.

Babakau stales quickly. Eat it the day it is fried. Refrigerate leftovers and reheat them in a hot oven so the outside crisps. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A round, bun-like shape and a flatter disk are both sold. This recipe flattens the pieces slightly.
  • Milk in the dough is a richer fry and adds dairy.
  • A pinch of cinnamon is a home choice and not required.

  • Tea
  • Butter, which adds dairy
  • A little sugar or jam
