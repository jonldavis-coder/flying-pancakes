Casabe

A dry, crisp cassava flatbread, more cracker than pancake.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 rounds

History

Casabe is a cassava flatbread of the Caribbean with Taíno roots, still eaten in Cuba, the Dominican Republic, Haiti as kasav, and parts of Puerto Rico. Traditional processing of bitter yuca includes pressing out the juice. This recipe does not teach that process. It uses commercial cassava flour sold for cooking, and it says so. The bread is dry, round, and keeps.

Cultural background

It is a bread for stews and for garlic mojo-style oil, not a sweet cake. Haitian kasav is the sister bread and is not given a second recipe that would only repeat this one.

Why it tastes this way

Cassava starch cooks into a neutral, crisp sheet, which is why it can carry a garlicky sauce or a stew. The Indigenous Caribbean bread was a way to store a root as a dry round. Commercial flour skips the dangerous part of bitter-yuca processing and will not taste identical to a pressed traditional bread. The shape and the use are what this page can honestly teach.

How it is made

Commercial cassava flour and water are cooked as thin rounds on a dry griddle until crisp.

Authentic touch. Spread the dough of flour and water very thin and cook it dry, turning, until it is crisp and pale. It should not be a soft pancake. If it stays gummy, it needs more time and less water.

How it is used

With a savory sauce, garlic oil, or stew. Break it like a cracker.

Field notes

  • This recipe uses commercial cassava flour only.
  • Casabe is a bread, dry and keepable, not a syrup pancake.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup commercial cassava flour
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 cup water, plus more if needed

Steps

  1. Mix flour, salt, and water into a soft dough.
  2. Press or roll very thin rounds between parchment.
  3. Cook on a dry medium griddle, turning, until dry and lightly spotted.
  4. Cool on a rack so they crisp.
  5. Serve with a savory sauce.
  6. If the dough cracks, add a teaspoon of water.

Use commercial cassava flour or yuca sold ready to cook. Do not process bitter cassava at home from these pages.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Haitian kasav is the same family.
  • A soft yuca bread is a different dish. This one is the dry round.

Cuban Torrejas

Bread soaked in spiced milk, fried, and finished in syrup.

20 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 8 · 8 slices

History

Torrejas are Cuba’s Christmas and holiday French toast: stale bread dipped in milk infused with cinnamon and citrus, then egg, fried, and sometimes finished in a light syrup. They are a festive cousin of Spanish torrijas and of pain perdu. They are not an everyday pancake stack.

Cultural background

A sweet bread or a dry loaf both appear. The spice is cinnamon. Families fry them for Nochebuena season.

Why it tastes this way

Cinnamon and citrus peel in the milk are the Cuban perfume, a holiday pantry rather than a plain custard. Stale bread drinks the milk. The fry gives a crust, and a short bath in syrup makes them a sweet, closer to rabanadas than to a diner French toast. The Christmas table is why the spice is generous.

How it is made

Stale bread is dipped in cinnamon milk and egg, fried, and warmed in a light syrup.

Authentic touch. Infuse the milk and let it cool before the bread goes in. Hot milk dissolves the slices. Fry them after a brief soak, not a long drown.

How it is used

Warm or at room temperature, as a holiday sweet.

Field notes

  • Cuban torrejas are a Christmas sweet, not a weekday stack.
  • Cool the infused milk before you soak the bread.

Ingredients

  • 8 slices stale bread
  • 1 cup milk
  • 1 cinnamon stick
  • 2 strips orange peel
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 2 eggs
  • Oil for frying
  • 1/4 cup sugar and 1/4 cup water, for syrup

Steps

  1. Heat milk, cinnamon, orange peel, and 2 tablespoons sugar. Cool, then strain.
  2. Dip the bread, then the beaten eggs.
  3. Fry in a shallow layer of oil until golden.
  4. Simmer the remaining sugar and water 3 minutes and spoon it over the torrejas.
  5. Serve warm.
  6. Do not soak the bread until it collapses.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • An anise seed in the milk is used in some houses.
  • Spanish torrijas are the older cousin, often tied to Lent as well.

Pairings and uses

  • Holiday table
  • Dessert
  • cinnamon
  • syrup

Jamaican Bammy

A dense cassava cake, griddled, then often soaked and fried to finish.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 small rounds

History

Bammy is a Jamaican cassava flatbread, associated with the island’s Taíno heritage and with later Jamaican cooking, especially beside fried fish. Traditional bammy uses grated bitter cassava that has been pressed. This page uses commercial cassava flour or frozen peeled yuca labeled ready to cook, not a home detox of bitter root. The cake is griddled, and a short soak and fry is a common way to reheat it.

Cultural background

It is a bread, round and dense. Fried fish and bammy are a recognized plate. The bread itself is plain.

Why it tastes this way

Cassava tastes mild and starchy, a foil for spicy fish and pickled pepper. Pressing or using a dry flour keeps the cake from being glue. Griddling toasts the outside. A soak in milk or coconut milk before a second fry is a later richness. Coconut milk is written as optional and keeps the base recipe free of dairy if you use water.

How it is made

Cassava flour, salt, and water are shaped into rounds and cooked on a griddle.

Authentic touch. Cook the first side until a crust forms before you flip, or the cake breaks. Use ready-to-cook yuca or commercial flour only.

How it is used

With fried fish or on its own. A pepper sauce on the side is the heat, not the bread.

Field notes

  • Bammy is Jamaica’s cassava bread, often served with fish.
  • This recipe does not process bitter cassava at home.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup commercial cassava flour
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 cup water
  • Oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Mix flour, salt, and water into a firm dough.
  2. Shape four small rounds about 1/2 inch thick.
  3. Cook on a medium oiled griddle, covered, turning once, until both sides are golden and the center is cooked.
  4. Serve warm.
  5. The center should not look wet.
  6. Pepper sauce belongs on the side.

Use commercial cassava flour or yuca sold ready to cook. Do not process bitter cassava at home from these pages.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Soaking a cooked round in coconut milk and frying it again is a common finish.
  • Casabe is the drier Caribbean cracker.

Pairings and uses

  • Bread course
  • Side dish
  • fried fish
  • pepper sauce
  • Vegetarian

Yaniqueques

Crisp fried johnnycakes, salty and blistered.

20 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 12 pieces

History

Yaniqueques are Dominican fried breads in the johnnycake family, the island word sitting close to the New England cake and to other Caribbean journey cakes. A simple flour or cornmeal dough is rolled and fried. Beach vendors sell them. This version uses flour, which is a common vendor dough, and it is fried rather than dry-griddled.

Cultural background

They are a snack with fish or with nothing. The blister is the sign of hot oil. They are not a sweet pancake.

Why it tastes this way

Hot oil blisters a thin dough so it tastes toasted and salty, a portable beach bread. The connection to johnnycake is the journey of a word and a simple dough around the Atlantic, not a claim that a Rhode Island recipe was copied line for line. Salt is the seasoning. The fry is the Dominican street method.

How it is made

A simple dough is rolled thin and fried until blistered.

Authentic touch. Roll them thin and fry hot. Thick yaniqueques stay doughy. Drain them or they go limp.

How it is used

Hot, salted, as a snack.

Field notes

  • Yaniqueque is the Dominican fried johnnycake.
  • Thin and hot is the texture.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • 3/4 cup water
  • Oil for frying

Steps

  1. Mix flour, baking powder, salt, oil, and water into a soft dough. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Roll thin and cut rounds. Slit each one.
  3. Fry in 1/2 inch of hot oil until blistered.
  4. Drain and salt.
  5. Serve hot.
  6. Do not fry them thick.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A cornmeal dough is closer to some johnnycakes and can be gluten-free if it has no wheat. This one is wheat.
  • Baking them makes a different, softer bread.

Pairings and uses

  • Street food
  • Snack
  • fried fish
  • salt

Trinidadian Paratha Roti

A flaky, layered roti, often torn, for wrapping curry.

40 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 rotis

History

Paratha roti in Trinidad, often called buss-up-shut when it is clapped into flaky pieces, is an Indo-Caribbean bread. Indian indentured laborers brought roti techniques in the nineteenth century. The bread is cooked on a tawa with ghee or oil and used to eat curry. Buss-up-shut is the torn style, named for a torn shirt. This recipe is that bread.

Cultural background

It is the utensil for curry goat, channa, or a vegetarian curry. The bread itself is the page. The curry is a separate pot.

Why it tastes this way

Ghee between layers fries the dough into flakes, which is why it can be torn into rags that still taste rich. The Caribbean adaptation of Indian paratha became a national bread in Trinidad. Oil works if you do not have ghee and changes the dairy status. This version uses ghee and says so. The clap at the end is how buss-up-shut is finished.

How it is made

A soft dough is layered with ghee, cooked on a tawa, and clapped into flakes.

Authentic touch. Spread ghee, fold, rest, and cook. Then clap the hot roti between your hands or with two spatulas so the layers shatter. Do that only when it is cooked, or you tear raw dough.

How it is used

With a curry. A simple chickpea curry is a vegetarian partner.

Field notes

  • Buss-up-shut is paratha roti clapped into pieces.
  • Indo-Caribbean history is the reason this bread is in Trinidad.

Ingredients

  • 3 cups flour
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup water
  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • 4 tablespoons ghee, soft

Steps

  1. Mix flour, salt, water, and oil into a soft dough. Rest 20 minutes.
  2. Divide into 6. Roll, spread with ghee, fold, and roll again.
  3. Cook on a hot tawa, turning, until both sides are spotted and the bread is cooked.
  4. Clap each roti between towels or spatulas so it flakes.
  5. Keep them wrapped.
  6. Serve with curry.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Oil instead of ghee makes it dairy-free and less rich.
  • Dhalpuri is the Guyanese split-pea-filled roti, a different bread.

Pairings and uses

  • Bread course
  • Supper
  • chickpea curry
  • ghee
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Guyanese Dhalpuri Roti

A roti stuffed with seasoned split peas, griddled.

40 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 rotis

History

Dhalpuri is a Guyanese roti filled with ground split peas, cooked on a tawa. It is Indo-Caribbean bread, cousin to Trinidadian roti, and a standard partner for curry. The filling is spiced yellow split peas, dry enough to roll inside the dough. It is a staple, not a sweet pancake.

Cultural background

The peas are boiled, dried in the pan, and ground with cumin and pepper. The dough is soft. The roti is cooked with a little oil.

Why it tastes this way

Split peas taste earthy and peppery, a meal inside the bread, which is why dhalpuri is more than a plain wrap. Cumin is the spice that marks the filling. The indenture-era Indian kitchen in Guyana made roti a daily bread and filled it so one round could be lunch. The griddle toasts the outside and steams the pea center.

How it is made

Boiled, seasoned split peas are sealed in dough and cooked on a griddle.

Authentic touch. The pea filling must be dry and fine. Wet peas tear the dough and steam it into a lump. Roll carefully so the filling reaches the edge without bursting.

How it is used

With curry, or on its own if the peas are well seasoned.

Field notes

  • Dhalpuri is defined by the split-pea filling.
  • Dry the peas before you grind or mash them.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups flour
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 3/4 cup water
  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • 1/2 cup yellow split peas, boiled until soft and drained dry
  • 1/2 teaspoon cumin
  • 1/4 teaspoon cayenne
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt, for the peas

Steps

  1. Mash or grind the dry cooked peas with cumin, cayenne, and salt. The filling should not be wet.
  2. Make a soft dough from flour, salt, water, and oil. Rest 15 minutes.
  3. Stuff and seal 6 rounds. Roll them gently.
  4. Cook on a hot oiled tawa until spotted on both sides.
  5. Wrap in a towel.
  6. Serve warm.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Trinidadian paratha is the flaky unfilled cousin.
  • A hotter pepper in the peas is a house choice.

Pairings and uses

  • Bread course
  • Stuffed or filled
  • Supper
  • curry
  • cumin
  • Vegetarian

Mexican Hotcakes

A diner-style pancake, often served with cajeta or condensed milk.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 pancakes

History

Hotcakes are a standard Mexican breakfast, the American-style pancake under a Spanish name, served in fondas and at home with butter, honey, cajeta, or fruit. They are not a pre-Hispanic cake. Corn tortillas are the older griddle bread. Hotcakes are the twentieth-century breakfast beside eggs and coffee.

Cultural background

Cajeta, goat’s-milk caramel, is a beloved pour and is dairy. Honey is the other. The batter is a simple baking-powder batter.

Why it tastes this way

The cake tastes like a mild wheat pancake because that is the café style that spread. Cajeta makes the plate Mexican: goat’s milk cooked with sugar until it is caramel, a colonial-era dairy sweet. Condensed milk is the humbler pour. The hotcake itself is not an ancient recipe, and the page does not pretend it is.

How it is made

A milk batter is cooked as pancakes and served with cajeta or honey.

Authentic touch. Cook them as ordinary pancakes. Warm the cajeta so it pours. Cold cajeta sits in a lump on a hot cake.

How it is used

With butter and cajeta, honey, or fruit.

Field notes

  • Hotcakes are a Mexican breakfast. They are not a pre-Hispanic corn cake.
  • Cajeta, goat’s-milk caramel, is a classic pour.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup flour
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup milk
  • 1 egg
  • 2 tablespoons melted butter
  • Cajeta or honey, to serve

Steps

  1. Whisk a batter and rest 5 minutes.
  2. Cook on a buttered medium griddle until both sides are brown.
  3. Warm the cajeta.
  4. Serve with butter and cajeta or honey.
  5. Do not sweeten the batter like cake.
  6. Eat them hot.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Hotcakes de elote add corn.
  • Crepas de cajeta are the thin version.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • cajeta
  • honey
  • butter
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Corn Tortillas

Soft nixtamal corn flatbreads, toasted on a comal.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 tortillas

History

Corn tortillas are the foundational griddle bread of Mexico, made from masa of nixtamalized maize. Nixtamal, cooking dried corn with calcium hydroxide, is an Indigenous technique that makes the grain nutritious and able to form a dough. Tortillas are cooked on a comal. They are not pancakes, and they are in this journal because they are the corn cake that explains a great deal of Mexican food. This recipe uses masa harina, the flour of that process, not a raw-corn shortcut.

Cultural background

They are the daily bread: tacos, enchiladas, a meal beside beans. A tortilla press or a flat plate and a bag help you flatten them.

Why it tastes this way

Nixtamalized corn tastes toasted and a little mineral, different from sweet cornmeal, because the alkali changes the grain. A hot comal dries the surface and keeps the center flexible. Wheat never had to be the daily bread where masa was the technology. The flavor is corn, salt, and the comal.

How it is made

Masa harina and water are pressed into thin rounds and cooked on a hot dry comal.

Authentic touch. Keep the dough as soft as clay, not crumbly. If it cracks at the edges, knead in a teaspoon of water. Cook about a minute a side, until they puff a little. Stack them in a towel.

How it is used

Warm, as a bread, the same day if you can.

Field notes

  • Masa harina is nixtamalized corn. It is not the same as cornmeal.
  • A soft dough that cracks needs a spoon more water.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups masa harina
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 1/2 cups warm water, plus more if needed

Steps

  1. Mix masa, salt, and water to a soft clay-like dough. Rest 10 minutes.
  2. Press thin rounds between plastic.
  3. Cook on a hot dry comal about a minute a side, until they speckle and puff a little.
  4. Stack in a towel.
  5. Serve warm.
  6. Add water if the edges crack.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Blue corn masa makes blue tortillas and is the same method.
  • Flour tortillas are the northern wheat bread.

Pairings and uses

  • Bread course
  • Supper
  • beans
  • salsa
  • eggs
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Tlayudas

A large, partly dried corn tortilla, toasted and topped with beans and cheese.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 tlayudas

History

Tlayudas are large Oaxacan corn tortillas, often cooked until they are partly dry and crisp, then topped with refried beans, quesillo or asiento, cabbage, and salsa. They are a night-market food of Oaxaca. The tortilla is the base. This recipe uses large corn tortillas you cook further on a comal, with beans and cheese. Asiento, unrefined pork fat, is traditional and is not in this version.

Cultural background

They are a meal. The tortilla should be toasted, not soft like a fresh taco tortilla. Quesillo is the stringy Oaxacan cheese. Mozzarella is only a stand-in and should be named as such if you use it.

Why it tastes this way

A large masa tortilla toasted on a comal tastes nuttier and crisper than a fresh soft tortilla, which is why it can hold beans without falling apart. Oaxaca’s corn diversity is in the masa. The toppings are the market meal. Cheese adds salt and stretch. Leaving out asiento keeps the plate free of pork and changes it from some street versions. That change is stated.

How it is made

Large corn tortillas are toasted on a comal, spread with beans and cheese, and heated until the cheese softens.

Authentic touch. Toast the tortilla until it is dry and blistered but not burnt to carbon. Spread beans thinly. Too much weight cracks it.

How it is used

Folded or open, with salsa and shredded cabbage.

Field notes

  • Tlayudas are the large toasted tortillas of Oaxaca.
  • This version leaves out pork asiento and says so.

Ingredients

  • 4 large corn tortillas
  • 1 cup refried beans
  • 1 cup shredded quesillo or mozzarella
  • 1 cup shredded cabbage
  • Salsa
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Toast the tortillas on a comal until dry and blistered but not burnt.
  2. Spread a thin layer of beans.
  3. Add cheese and heat until it softens.
  4. Top with cabbage and salsa.
  5. Serve immediately.
  6. Do not overload them.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Asiento is the traditional pork fat and adds meat.
  • A fully soft tortilla is a different, taco-like bite.

Pairings and uses

  • Supper
  • Street food
  • refried beans
  • quesillo

Gorditas

Thick corn cakes, griddled, split, and filled.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 gorditas

History

Gorditas are thick masa cakes, cooked on a comal and often split and filled with beans, cheese, or stewed meat. The name means little fat ones. They are street and home food across central and northern Mexico, with many local fillings. This recipe is the masa cake and a bean filling, so the bread itself stays vegan.

Cultural background

They should puff a little. The pocket is pinched open after cooking. Fillings are salty and savory against the plain corn.

Why it tastes this way

Thicker masa stays soft inside and toasted outside, a container rather than a flexible tortilla. Nixtamal is still the corn flavor. A filling of beans makes a meal from the same staples. Regional gorditas differ. The thick comal cake is the shared idea.

How it is made

Masa is patted into thick rounds, cooked on a comal, and filled with beans.

Authentic touch. Keep the masa soft and the cakes about a half inch thick. Cook them until they are no longer raw in the center and the outside is spotted. Split them carefully while warm.

How it is used

Hot, filled.

Field notes

  • Gorditas are thick enough to split and fill.
  • The written filling is beans, so the cake can stay meatless.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups masa harina
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 1/2 cups warm water
  • 1 cup refried beans

Steps

  1. Mix a soft masa dough.
  2. Shape 6 thick disks.
  3. Cook on a medium comal until spotted and cooked through, turning.
  4. Split and fill with beans.
  5. Serve hot.
  6. The center should not be raw masa.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A potato and cheese filling is another meatless option and adds dairy.
  • Fried gorditas exist and are a different, heavier cake.

Pairings and uses

  • Supper
  • Stuffed or filled

Crepas de Cajeta

Thin crepes in goat’s-milk caramel.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 crepes

History

Sweet crepes with cajeta are a Mexican dessert-café standard. Cajeta is a caramel of goat’s milk, famously associated with Celaya. The crepe is the French technique. The sauce is Mexican. Together they are a real menu item, not a home invention of this guide.

Cultural background

The crepes are thin. The cajeta is warmed and thinned with a spoon of milk so it can coat. Chopped nuts are optional and are a tree nut if you add them.

Why it tastes this way

Goat’s milk caramel tastes tangier than cow’s-milk dulce de leche, which is the point of cajeta. A thin crepe folds into the sauce and tastes of browned butter and caramel. The pairing is a café dessert of the twentieth century, French technique and Mexican dairy sweet on one plate.

How it is made

Thin crepes are folded with warmed cajeta.

Authentic touch. Warm the cajeta with a splash of milk until it flows. Thick cajeta tears a crepe. Fill and fold, then give them a moment in the pan.

How it is used

Warm, as dessert.

Field notes

  • Cajeta is goat’s-milk caramel, tangier than cow’s-milk dulce de leche.
  • This dessert does not ask you to flame the sauce.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup flour
  • 2 eggs
  • 1 1/4 cups milk
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • Pinch of salt
  • 1 tablespoon melted butter
  • 3/4 cup cajeta
  • 2 tablespoons milk, to thin the cajeta

Steps

  1. Make thin crepes and stack them.
  2. Warm the cajeta with the milk until it flows.
  3. Spread and fold the crepes.
  4. Warm them briefly in the pan.
  5. Serve immediately.
  6. Do not boil the cajeta.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pecans on top are common and add tree nuts.
  • Cajeta from a jar is the practical ingredient. A from-scratch caramel is a longer project.

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • cajeta
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Cachapas

A sweet-corn pancake, soft and irregular, often folded with cheese.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 6 cachapas

History

Cachapas are Venezuelan pancakes of fresh ground sweet corn, cooked on a griddle and often folded around cheese, queso de mano or similar. They are street and home food. The batter is the corn itself, sometimes loosened with a little milk or water. This recipe keeps the batter dairy-free and serves cheese as an optional fold, so the cake can be vegan and the cheese is a choice.

Cultural background

The corn must be sweet and juicy. Canned corn, drained and blended, is a workable off-season substitute and tastes less alive. Say which you used.

Why it tastes this way

Fresh corn is sweet because the kernels are immature and full of sugar, which is why a cachapa tastes like corn and dessert at once even when the filling is salty cheese. The griddle browns the natural sugar. No wheat is required. The cheese, when used, is salty and squeaky, the contrast. Street griddles make them irregular. That rustic edge is normal.

How it is made

Fresh corn is blended with a little salt and cooked as thick pancakes.

Authentic touch. Blend the corn until it is a coarse batter, not a juice. Cook on medium and cover briefly so the center sets. It will not look like a smooth wheat pancake.

How it is used

Folded, with cheese if you eat dairy, or plain.

Field notes

  • Cachapas are fresh-corn pancakes. Cheese is optional here.
  • Canned corn works and tastes less alive than fresh.

Ingredients

  • 4 ears sweet corn, kernels cut off
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon cornmeal, if needed
  • Oil
  • Cheese, optional

Steps

  1. Blend the corn and salt to a coarse batter. Add cornmeal if it is watery.
  2. Cook thick rounds on a medium oiled griddle, covered briefly.
  3. Flip when the underside is spotted.
  4. Fold with cheese if you eat dairy.
  5. Serve hot.
  6. They will look rustic.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of cornmeal helps a watery blend hold.
  • Costa Rican chorreadas are the close cousin.

Venezuelan Arepas

Thick corn cakes of precooked maize flour, griddled, then split.

15 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 arepas

History

Arepas are a daily bread of Venezuela and Colombia, with roots in Indigenous maize cakes of the region. The modern Venezuelan arepa is usually made from precooked corn flour, masarepa, shaped into thick disks, griddled, and often finished in the oven, then split and filled. Fillings are a whole cuisine. This page is the corn cake. Reina pepiada and other meat fillings are variations, not the dough.

Cultural background

The dough should feel like clay. A griddle browns the face. A few minutes in a hot oven, if you have one, sets the center. They are split while warm.

Why it tastes this way

Precooked corn flour is a twentieth-century product that made a much older maize cake fast to cook at home. The taste is toasted corn, mild and a little sweet, a container for salty fillings. The thickness is why an arepa is not a tortilla. Venezuela’s habit of stuffing them turned the bread into breakfast and dinner.

How it is made

Masarepa and water are shaped into thick disks and cooked on a griddle, then split.

Authentic touch. Use masarepa, not masa harina for tortillas and not cornmeal. The dough should be moist and not crack. Cook the crust, then finish the center. A raw white line in the middle means they need more time.

How it is used

Split, with cheese, beans, or eggs. Meat fillings are a separate choice.

Field notes

  • Use masarepa, the precooked corn flour, not tortilla masa.
  • Stuffing them is the Venezuelan habit.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups masarepa
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 1/2 cups warm water
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Stir masarepa, salt, and water. Rest 5 minutes. The dough should be moist and not crack.
  2. Shape 6 thick disks.
  3. Cook on a medium griddle until a crust forms, then bake at 400°F for 10 minutes or continue on the griddle until the center is set.
  4. Split.
  5. Fill with beans, cheese, or eggs.
  6. Do not use tortilla masa harina.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Colombian arepas are often left unsplit and served beside the meal.
  • Andean arepas use wheat and are a different bread.

Pupusas

Thick stuffed corn cakes, griddled, served with curtido.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 pupusas

History

Pupusas are El Salvador’s stuffed corn cakes, a national dish also loved in Honduras. Masa is filled with cheese, beans, or chicharrón, sealed, and cooked on a comal. Curtido, a lightly fermented cabbage slaw, and a tomato salsa are the usual sides. This recipe is the cheese pupusa. Beans are the vegan variation if you omit cheese. Chicharrón adds pork.

Cultural background

They are supper and street food. The masa is closed around the filling so nothing leaks. Curtido’s tang is part of the plate.

Why it tastes this way

A thick masa cake steamed on a comal tastes of toasted corn and melted cheese, salty against the sour cabbage. Nixtamal is the Indigenous maize technique under a later national dish. Curtido is quick-pickled, which is why it tastes bright and cuts the fat of the cheese. The comal, not a fryer, is the traditional heat.

How it is made

Masa is stuffed with cheese, flattened, and cooked on a comal.

Authentic touch. Keep a little masa in your palm, cup it, add a small amount of cheese, and close it. Flatten gently. If the cheese leaks, the cake was too thin or the heat too low to set the crust.

How it is used

Hot, with curtido and salsa.

Field notes

  • Pupusas are El Salvador’s stuffed corn cakes.
  • Curtido is the cabbage relish that belongs on the plate.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups masa harina
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 1/2 cups warm water
  • 1 cup grated quesillo or mozzarella

Steps

  1. Mix a soft masa.
  2. Cup a ball of masa, add a little cheese, close, and flatten gently.
  3. Cook on a medium comal until both sides are spotted and the center is hot.
  4. Serve with curtido and salsa.
  5. Do not overfill.
  6. Keep cooked pupusas covered.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Bean pupusas can be vegan if the beans are.
  • Loroco, an edible flower bud, is a traditional addition with the cheese when you can get it.

Pairings and uses

  • Street food
  • Stuffed or filled
  • curtido
  • tomato salsa
  • Vegetarian

Chorreadas

Sweet fresh-corn pancakes, cousins of cachapas.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 cakes

History

Chorreadas are Costa Rican fresh-corn pancakes, cooked on a griddle and served with sour cream or a crumbly cheese. They are the local sister of Venezuelan cachapas and Nicaraguan güirilas. The batter is corn. This written cake is vegan. Sour cream is the usual partner and adds dairy at the table.

Cultural background

They are a bit irregular. Sweet corn is the requirement. They are breakfast or a snack, not a stuffed dinner.

Why it tastes this way

Fresh corn’s sugar browns on a griddle and tastes like the season. A Costa Rican table often meets that sweetness with sour cream, the same sweet-salt idea as a cheese cachapa. No wheat is needed. The cake is a Central American corn pancake with its own name.

How it is made

Fresh corn is blended with a pinch of salt and cooked as small pancakes.

Authentic touch. Blend to a coarse batter and cook on medium. Cover for a minute if the center is slow. They should be spotted, not burnt.

How it is used

With sour cream or cheese if you eat dairy, or plain.

Field notes

  • Chorreadas are Costa Rica’s fresh-corn pancakes.
  • Sour cream is a table partner, not required in the batter.

Ingredients

  • 4 ears sweet corn
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon masa harina, if needed
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Blend corn and salt to a coarse batter.
  2. Cook small cakes on a medium oiled griddle until spotted and set.
  3. Serve plain or with sour cream.
  4. They should taste of corn.
  5. Cover briefly if the center is slow.
  6. Eat them the same day.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of milk in the blend adds dairy and richness.
  • Cachapas and güirilas are the neighboring cakes.

Güirilas

Soft fresh-corn cakes, traditionally eaten with cuajada cheese.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 cakes

History

Güirilas are Nicaraguan fresh-corn cakes, close to chorreadas and cachapas, traditionally paired with cuajada, a fresh cheese. They are breakfast and a side. The corn cake is vegan here. The cheese is the traditional partner and is dairy.

Cultural background

The texture is soft. The corn is sweet. They are cooked on a griddle and eaten the same morning.

Why it tastes this way

Young corn tastes sweet and moist, and a fresh salty cheese is the Nicaraguan contrast, the same logic as the other Central American corn pancakes with different names. Keeping the name güirila matters so Nicaragua is not only a footnote on the cachapa page. The griddle toasts the milk sugars in the corn.

How it is made

Fresh corn is blended and cooked as thick soft cakes.

Authentic touch. Do not add much liquid. The kernels supply it. Cook until the center is set and the face is spotted gold.

How it is used

With cuajada or another fresh cheese, if you eat dairy.

Field notes

  • Güirilas are Nicaragua’s fresh-corn cakes.
  • Cuajada, a fresh cheese, is the traditional partner.

Ingredients

  • 4 ears sweet corn
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Oil
  • Fresh cheese, to serve

Steps

  1. Blend the corn and salt.
  2. Cook thick soft cakes on a medium griddle until spotted and set.
  3. Serve with cuajada or another fresh cheese.
  4. Do not add much water.
  5. The cakes should stay soft.
  6. Eat them warm.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Chorreadas are the Costa Rican name in the same family.
  • A savory herb in the batter is a home change.

Brazilian Tapioca Crepes

A crisp-edged crepe of hydrated tapioca starch, filled with cheese or coconut.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 crepes

History

Tapioca crepes are a Brazilian street and home breakfast made from gummed tapioca starch, not a wheat batter. The starch is moistened until it clumps, then scattered in a pan, where it binds into a crepe. Cheese or coconut and condensed milk are fillings. The starch itself is cassava, a crop with deep Indigenous roots in Brazil. Use commercial tapioca starch.

Cultural background

A good tapioca has a lacy edge and a flexible center. Queijo coalho is a traditional salty cheese. This filling uses a firm cheese and notes coconut as the vegan option.

Why it tastes this way

Tapioca starch has almost no flavor and a lot of chew, so the filling is the taste: salty cheese or sweet coconut. The pan hydrates the starch into a sheet, which is a different chemistry from wheat gluten. Cassava is the Indigenous and later national starch. The crepe format is a modern street breakfast. Both can be true.

How it is made

Moistened tapioca starch is cooked as a crepe and filled.

Authentic touch. Moisten the starch until it holds when squeezed and still crumbles. Scatter it in an even layer in a moderate pan. Do not stir. It will knit itself. Flip once, quickly.

How it is used

Folded, hot. Cheese is the dairy filling. Coconut and a little sugar are the vegan one.

Field notes

  • The crepe is tapioca starch, not wheat.
  • Cheese is one filling. Coconut and sugar are the vegan one.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups tapioca starch
  • About 3/4 cup water
  • 1 cup grated firm cheese, or 1 cup grated coconut plus 2 tablespoons sugar

Steps

  1. Sprinkle water over the starch and toss until it clumps when squeezed and still crumbles.
  2. Scatter a thin even layer in a medium pan. Do not stir.
  3. When the sheet knits, flip it.
  4. Add cheese, or coconut and sugar, and fold.
  5. Serve immediately.
  6. Use commercial tapioca starch.

Use commercial cassava flour or yuca sold ready to cook. Do not process bitter cassava at home from these pages.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Coconut and sugar instead of cheese keep it vegan.
  • Beiju is the plainer cassava bread.

  • coconut

Beiju

A plain cassava-starch bread, softer and less filled than a street tapioca.

10 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 rounds

History

Beiju is a cassava-starch bread of Brazil, with Indigenous origins, cooked from moistened tapioca starch on a hot surface. It is plainer than the filled tapioca crepe of a city stall. Some beiju are thick. Some are thin. This is a thin griddled round of commercial tapioca starch.

Cultural background

It is a bread, eaten with butter, coconut, or nothing. The stall crepe is the dressed-up descendant.

Why it tastes this way

Cassava starch toasts into a neutral, slightly sweet sheet. Indigenous cooking in Brazil used the root as a staple bread. Commercial starch is the safe home ingredient. Butter or coconut on top is flavor. The bread itself is almost plain, which is the point of a staple.

How it is made

Moistened tapioca starch is cooked on a dry pan into a round.

Authentic touch. Scatter an even layer and let it knit. Do not add a filling until it has become a sheet. Keep the heat moderate so it does not scorch before it binds.

How it is used

Warm, plain or with coconut.

Field notes

  • Beiju is the plainer cassava-starch bread.
  • The filled street tapioca is the dressed-up relative.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups tapioca starch
  • About 3/4 cup water
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Moisten the starch and salt until it clumps.
  2. Scatter thin rounds in a dry medium pan and let them knit.
  3. Flip once.
  4. Serve plain or with coconut.
  5. Do not brown them black.
  6. Use commercial tapioca starch.

Use commercial cassava flour or yuca sold ready to cook. Do not process bitter cassava at home from these pages.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • The tapioca crepe page is the filled street version.
  • A thicker beiju needs a mold and more starch.

Mbeju

A crisp cassava-starch and cheese cake, rich and salty.

15 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · one 9-inch cake

History

Mbeju, or mbeyú, is a Paraguayan cake of cassava starch and cheese, cooked on a griddle or in a pan until the outside is crisp. It is a traditional breakfast and snack, part of a cuisine that also includes chipa and sopa paraguaya. Cheese and starch are the body. An egg helps it hold.

Cultural background

It should be thin enough to crisp and rich enough to taste of cheese. It is eaten with coffee or cocido. Fresh farmer cheese or a dry queso is better than a wet mozzarella.

Why it tastes this way

Cassava starch crisps, and a salty cheese melts into it, which is the Paraguayan combination of a local starch and a dairy farm. The flavor is salty, toasted, and rich, not sweet. An egg binds. The griddle is the tool. It is a national breakfast cake, not a sweet pancake.

How it is made

Cassava starch, cheese, egg, and milk or cream are cooked as a flat cake in a skillet.

Authentic touch. Grate the cheese dry. Pat the dough thin in a buttered pan and cook it patiently until the bottom is a real crust. Wet cheese makes it steam.

How it is used

Warm, in wedges, with coffee.

Field notes

  • Mbeju is cassava starch and cheese, crisp on the griddle.
  • Use a dry cheese or the cake steams.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup cassava starch
  • 1 cup finely grated dry cheese
  • 1 egg
  • 1/4 cup milk
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon butter

Steps

  1. Mix starch, cheese, egg, milk, and salt into a soft dough.
  2. Pat it thin in a buttered skillet.
  3. Cook covered over medium-low until the bottom is a real crust.
  4. Brown the top.
  5. Rest 5 minutes and cut.
  6. The cheese should be dry.

Use commercial cassava flour or yuca sold ready to cook. Do not process bitter cassava at home from these pages.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Anise is sometimes in the dough.
  • Beiju is the plainer starch bread without cheese.

Pairings and uses

  • coffee

Argentine Panqueques

Thin crepes filled with dulce de leche.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 crepes

History

Panqueques in Argentina are thin crepes, a standard dessert when filled with dulce de leche. Dulce de leche is milk cooked with sugar until it is caramel, a Río de la Plata sweet shared with Uruguay. The crepe is the wrapper. The filling is the national flavor.

Cultural background

They are rolled and sometimes flambéed in restaurants. This recipe does not flame them. It warms the dulce de leche and rolls the crepes.

Why it tastes this way

Dulce de leche tastes of cooked milk sugar, richer and less tangy than goat’s-milk cajeta. A thin egg crepe is mild enough to carry it. The dessert is a café and home sweet of the twentieth century, French technique and a local caramel. Lemon and sugar would be a different country’s crepe.

How it is made

Thin crepes are spread with dulce de leche and rolled.

Authentic touch. Warm the dulce de leche so it spreads. Cold caramel tears the crepe. Do not boil it.

How it is used

Warm, as dessert.

Field notes

  • Dulce de leche is the filling that makes the crepe Argentine.
  • This recipe warms the caramel and does not flame it.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup flour
  • 2 eggs
  • 1 1/4 cups milk
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • Pinch of salt
  • 1 tablespoon melted butter
  • 3/4 cup dulce de leche

Steps

  1. Cook thin crepes.
  2. Warm the dulce de leche until it spreads.
  3. Fill and roll.
  4. Serve warm.
  5. Do not boil the caramel.
  6. A thin ribbon of filling is enough.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A sprinkle of coconut is a home finish.
  • Chilean panqueques are a close relative.

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • dulce de leche
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Llapingachos

Pan-fried potato cakes stuffed with cheese, served with peanut sauce.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 6 cakes

History

Llapingachos are Ecuadorian potato cakes, pan-fried, often stuffed with cheese and served with a peanut sauce and salad or sausage. They are highland food. The potato cake is the page. Peanut sauce is traditional and is a peanut allergen, so this written plate serves the cakes with cheese inside and puts the peanut sauce as a named variation you add only if you eat peanuts.

Cultural background

The cakes are thick. The cheese melts inside. A pickled onion salad is a common side and can be made without peanuts.

Why it tastes this way

Highland potatoes fry into a crisp cake with a soft center, a staple turned into a meal. Cheese in the middle salts them. Peanut sauce, when served, is the richer, sweeter note of the classic plate and is also a serious allergen, which is why it is not silently included. The cake without it is still a llapingacho. The sauce is the choice.

How it is made

Mashed potato is stuffed with cheese, shaped into thick cakes, and pan-fried.

Authentic touch. Season the mash, enclose a small piece of cheese, and chill the cakes before frying so they hold. Fry in a little oil until both sides are crusty.

How it is used

With a small salad. Add peanut sauce only if everyone at the table can eat peanuts.

Field notes

  • Llapingachos are Ecuadorian stuffed potato cakes.
  • Peanut sauce is traditional and optional here because it is a major allergen.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 pounds potatoes, boiled and mashed
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 4 ounces cheese, cut in 6 pieces
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Season the mash and divide into 6.
  2. Enclose a piece of cheese in each and shape thick cakes. Chill 15 minutes.
  3. Fry in a little oil until both sides are crusty and the center is hot.
  4. Serve with salad.
  5. Add peanut sauce only if everyone can eat peanuts.
  6. Do not use a wet mash.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Peanut sauce is the classic partner and adds a peanut allergen.
  • A sausage on the side is a common fuller plate and adds meat.

Pairings and uses

  • Side dish
