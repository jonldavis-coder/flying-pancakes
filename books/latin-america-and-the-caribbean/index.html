Mexican hotcakes

Soft wheat cakes with browned butter, finished with cajeta, honey, or condensed milk.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 hotcakes

History

Wheat hotcakes spread through Mexican breakfasts in the twentieth century, when baking powder, packaged flour, and diner griddles showed up on fonda menus. A morning cook still turns them out beside coffee and eggs. Corn tortillas and other masa cakes are much older in Mexico. These hotcakes are a later wheat batter. They are not an ancient recipe, and they are not a corn cake under a new name.

Cultural background

In a fonda, hotcakes are ordinary city breakfast, not a festival dish. The plate is familiar in Mexico City and in towns across the country, usually sweetened at the table rather than in the bowl.

Why it tastes this way

The crumb tastes of wheat and browned butter, and baking powder keeps it soft if you stop stirring once the flour disappears. Cajeta, goat-milk caramel, is tangy and dark. Sweetened condensed milk is plain and very sweet. Honey stays floral. The topping does as much work as the batter, which is why a stack without one of them tastes unfinished.

How it is made

Whisk milk, egg, and melted butter. Stir them into the dry ingredients only until the flour is wet. Cook small rounds in a buttered skillet until the bubbles stay open, then flip once.

Authentic touch. Rest the batter for five minutes, then leave the lumps alone. A smooth, overworked batter makes a tough cake. The griddle is ready when a drop of water skitters.

How it is used

Serve straight from the pan at breakfast, with cajeta, honey, or condensed milk, and coffee. This is a fonda plate, not a street antojito.

Field notes

  • Baking powder is a nineteenth-century tool, and this Mexican breakfast cake follows that later wheat style.
  • Cajeta is goat milk cooked with sugar. It is not the same jar as cow-milk dulce de leche.
  • Corn cakes were already the daily griddle food of Mexico long before these hotcakes arrived.

Ingredients

  • 150 g wheat flour
  • 2 teaspoons baking powder
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 240 ml milk
  • 1 egg
  • 2 tablespoons melted butter, plus more for the skillet
  • Cajeta, for serving

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, baking powder, sugar, and salt in a bowl.
  2. Whisk the milk, egg, and melted butter in a second bowl, then pour them into the flour and stir only until the dry streaks disappear.
  3. Rest the batter for 5 minutes. Heat a skillet over medium heat and film it with butter.
  4. Pour about 60 ml of batter for each cake. Cook until the bubbles stay open and the underside is browned, then flip and cook the second side.
  5. Serve at once with cajeta. Honey or sweetened condensed milk can go on at the table instead.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Honey or sweetened condensed milk can replace the cajeta.
  • A spoon of orange zest in the batter is a home variation.
  • Whole wheat flour makes a heartier cake and still contains wheat.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Café de olla or black coffee
  • Eggs on the same breakfast plate
  • Sliced fruit beside the stack
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Corn tortillas

Soft, toasted corn with a faint mineral note from nixtamal, cooked dry on a hot comal.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 tortillas

History

The corn tortilla is the foundational griddle cake of Mexico, older than wheat hotcakes by a very long stretch. The dough is masa: dried corn simmered with an alkali, rested, rinsed, and ground. That process is nixtamalization. Fresh nixtamal, ground wet, is the older method. Masa harina is dried nixtamalized corn, milled and sold as flour, and it is the practical home shortcut. Neither one is a bowl of plain untreated cornmeal.

Cultural background

A comal, a round griddle, sits on the stove in home kitchens and in tortillerías. Tortillas wrap food, accompany stews, and become the next day's chips or enchiladas. This recipe is the plain cake, just masa and water.

Why it tastes this way

Nixtamal gives the tortilla its corn aroma and a slight mineral taste that plain cornmeal never has. The alkali loosens the skins, changes the flavor, and makes the amino acid niacin more available. Cooked dry, the surface smells toasted. There is no wheat gluten to make it bready, so the cake stays flexible only while it is warm and moist.

How it is made

Stir masa harina with warm water and salt, rest it covered, and press thin rounds. Cook on a hot dry comal until the tortilla releases, flips, and puffs in spots.

Authentic touch. The dough should feel like soft clay that does not crack at the edge and does not glue itself to your palm. If it cracks, add water a teaspoon at a time. If it sticks, knead in a sprinkle of masa harina. Keep the comal dry.

How it is used

Eat them warm, wrapped in a cloth so they steam themselves soft. They are the plate under eggs, beans, salsas, and stews, and they are a meal by themselves with salt.

Field notes

  • A tortilla that puffs has enough moisture and a hot enough comal. The puff is steam, not baking powder.
  • Masa harina is already nixtamalized. Do not add cal again, and do not swap in untreated cornmeal.
  • Corn tortillas in this recipe are masa and water. Lard belongs to some flour tortillas, not to this dough.

Ingredients

  • 250 g masa harina
  • 360 ml warm water, plus more if the dough cracks
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Extra masa harina for dusting if the dough sticks

Steps

  1. Stir the masa harina, salt, and warm water until a dough forms. Knead for a minute. It should feel like soft clay.
  2. Cover and rest for 20 minutes so the flour hydrates. Adjust with a spoon of water or a dust of masa harina.
  3. Divide into 12 pieces. Press each between plastic sheets in a tortilla press, or under a heavy skillet, into a thin round.
  4. Cook on a dry comal over medium-high heat for about 45 seconds, flip, cook the second side, then flip once more until the tortilla smells toasted and puffs in places.
  5. Wrap the tortillas in a clean cloth as they come off the heat so they stay soft.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Blue or red masa harina changes the color and keeps the same method.
  • Fresh nixtamal from a mill can replace masa harina. Use less water, because the dough is already wet.
  • A flour tortilla is a different bread, made with wheat and fat in the north.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • Accompaniment to a meal
  • Frijoles de la olla and a salsa
  • Scrambled eggs and avocado
  • A stew that needs a soft scoop
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan

Flour tortillas

Soft, wheaty, and a little rich from fat, with browned spots and a flexible crumb.

40 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 tortillas

History

Flour tortillas belong to northern Mexico, especially Sonora, where wheat became a staple crop. The dough is wheat flour, fat, salt, and water, rolled thin and cooked on a comal. Corn tortillas are older. Wheat hotcakes are later still. This northern flatbread is its own thing: a pliable wheat wrap for machaca, beans, and burros, not a corn tortilla made with different flour.

Cultural background

In Sonora and neighboring northern states, the flour tortilla is daily bread. Home cooks roll them larger than the corn tortillas of the center and south. This version uses vegetable shortening so the recipe stays vegetarian. Many family recipes use lard instead.

Why it tastes this way

Wheat gluten, handled lightly, stretches into a soft sheet instead of a tough cracker. Fat shortens that gluten, which is why the tortilla bends without snapping while it is warm. The comal adds toasted spots. There is no egg and no baking powder in this dough, so the flavor stays plain and wheaty, a carrier for the filling.

How it is made

Cut shortening into flour and salt, add warm water, knead briefly, and rest. Roll thin rounds and cook them on a hot comal until they blister.

Authentic touch. Rest the dough at least 30 minutes before rolling. The flour hydrates, and the rounds relax instead of springing back. Cook them dry or with only a trace of fat. Stack them in a cloth at once.

How it is used

Use them warm as the wrap for beans, eggs, or stews, or fold one around cheese while it is still hot. They are a bread, not a dessert.

Field notes

  • Sonoran flour tortillas are often much larger than a central Mexican corn tortilla.
  • This dough uses vegetable shortening. Swap in lard and the tortilla is no longer vegetarian.
  • Corn cakes in Mexico are older than this wheat flatbread and older than wheat hotcakes.

Ingredients

  • 250 g wheat flour, plus more for rolling
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 40 g vegetable shortening
  • 150 ml warm water

Steps

  1. Stir the flour and salt. Rub in the shortening until the mixture looks like coarse crumbs.
  2. Add the warm water and knead just until a smooth, soft dough forms. Cover and rest for 30 minutes.
  3. Divide into 8 pieces. Roll each into a thin round on a floured board.
  4. Cook on a hot dry comal for about 30 to 45 seconds a side, until brown spots appear and the surface blisters.
  5. Stack the tortillas in a cloth so they steam and stay flexible.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Lard in place of vegetable shortening is common in northern kitchens. That version contains meat and is not vegetarian.
  • A spoon of wheat flour more or less sets the size. Keep the dough soft, not wet.
  • Some cooks add a pinch of baking powder for a softer puff. This recipe leaves it out.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • Accompaniment to a meal
  • Mashed beans and a mild salsa
  • Scrambled eggs and roasted chile
  • Carne asada, on a plate that is no longer vegetarian
  • Vegetarian

Gorditas

Thick toasted masa, split and filled with mashed beans, fresh cheese, and a mild salsa.

30 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 gorditas

History

Gorditas are thick masa cakes, griddled until the outside is set, then split and filled. The corn dough is older than the habit of stuffing it, and corn cakes in Mexico are older than wheat hotcakes. A gordita is not a sope. A sope keeps a pinched rim and an open top. A gordita is fatter and is opened like a pocket after cooking. This recipe fills the pocket with beans and cheese. Chicharrón is a common filling in many towns and is not vegetarian.

Cultural background

Gorditas are antojitos, the category of small cooked snacks sold from comals and market stalls. León and many other cities have their own fillings. The masa base is the shared part.

Why it tastes this way

Because the cake is thick, the center stays steamy and corny while the outside dries and toasts. Nixtamalized masa tastes fuller than raw cornmeal. Salt in the dough, salt in the beans, and a mild chile salsa keep the plate savory. Cheese adds a milky salt note. The split lets the filling heat through against the hot masa.

How it is made

Mix masa harina with water and salt, rest it, and pat thick rounds. Griddle them dry, split, and fill with warm mashed beans, cheese, and salsa.

Authentic touch. Cook the cakes long enough that the crust is dry before you split them. A wet cake tears instead of opening into a pocket. Use a thin knife and turn the gordita as you cut.

How it is used

Eat them hot, standing or at the table, with the salsa on the filling rather than cooked into the dough. They are a meal or a heavy snack.

Field notes

  • The name means little fat ones, which describes the thickness, not a dessert.
  • Split the gordita after it cooks. Filling it raw is the method for a tlacoyo, a different shape.
  • Masa harina in this recipe is nixtamalized corn, not plain cornmeal from a baking aisle.

Ingredients

  • 250 g masa harina
  • 320 ml warm water
  • 1 teaspoon salt, divided
  • 300 g cooked beans, mashed and warmed with a spoon of their broth
  • 80 g crumbled fresh white cheese
  • 2 ripe tomatoes, 1 jalapeño, and a pinch of salt for a quick salsa

Steps

  1. Stir masa harina with 1/2 teaspoon salt and the warm water. Knead, cover, and rest 20 minutes. The dough should be soft and not cracked.
  2. Blend or finely chop the tomatoes and jalapeño with a pinch of salt for a mild salsa.
  3. Divide the dough into 8 pieces and pat each into a thick round, about 1 centimeter.
  4. Cook on a dry comal over medium heat for 4 to 5 minutes a side, until the outside is dry and spotted and the center is set.
  5. Split each cake along the edge with a knife, leaving one side attached. Fill with warm beans and cheese, then spoon on salsa.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Chicharrón and potatoes are a classic filling. That version contains meat and is not vegetarian.
  • Nopales and beans, with the cheese left out, can be made dairy-free and vegan.
  • Blue masa harina changes the color and not the shape.

Pairings and uses

  • Street food
  • Savory meal
  • Snack
  • A thin guajillo or tomato salsa
  • Shredded lettuce and onion, if you want crunch
  • Agua fresca or black coffee
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Tlacoyos

An oval masa cake with a seam of mashed beans, topped with nopales, onion, and salsa.

35 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 tlacoyos

History

Tlacoyos are oval masa cakes from the Valley of Mexico. The filling, often mashed beans or habas, which are fava beans, goes inside the raw dough, and the cake is sealed before it meets the comal. That is different from a gordita, which is split after cooking, and from a sope, which is an open round with a pinched rim. The masa is nixtamalized corn, older in this region than wheat hotcakes. This recipe uses habas and keeps cheese off the cake.

Cultural background

Street cooks around Xochimilco and across Mexico City pat tlacoyos to order and set them on a clay or metal comal. Toppings are added after the cake is cooked. Queso fresco and crema are common. They are not in this version.

Why it tastes this way

The sealed bean filling steams inside the masa, so each bite is toasted corn and then a soft, salty center. Fava beans taste earthier and a little sweeter than black beans. A raw onion and a cooked nopal on top add bite and a green, tart note. The mild salsa brings acid and a small amount of chile without covering the corn.

How it is made

Season mashed cooked favas. Tuck them into oval masa cakes, seal, and griddle dry. Top with nopales, onion, and salsa.

Authentic touch. Shape a log, press a groove, lay in a thin rope of beans, and close the dough so the filling does not leak. The finished cake is oval and flatter than a gordita, with pointed or rounded ends.

How it is used

Eat them hot from the comal as a street snack or a light meal. Because this batch has no cheese or crema, it stays vegan. Add cheese only if you mean to change that.

Field notes

  • Habas, fava beans, are a classic tlacoyo filling along with black beans and requesón.
  • The oval shape and the sealed raw filling are what separate a tlacoyo from a gordita or a sope.
  • This batch is masa, beans, nopales, and salsa, so it stays vegan if you skip cheese and crema.

Ingredients

  • 250 g masa harina
  • 320 ml warm water
  • 1 teaspoon salt, divided
  • 200 g cooked fava beans, mashed with a spoon of their cooking liquid
  • 1 cup cooked nopales, drained
  • 1 small white onion, finely chopped
  • 2 tomatoes and 1 jalapeño for salsa

Steps

  1. Mix the masa harina, 1/2 teaspoon salt, and warm water. Knead, cover, and rest 20 minutes.
  2. Season the mashed favas with the remaining salt. They should be thick, not soupy. Chop the tomatoes and jalapeño with a pinch of salt for a mild salsa.
  3. Divide the dough into 8 pieces. Form each into an oval, press a groove down the center, add a spoon of favas, and seal the dough around them. Pat into a flat oval.
  4. Cook on a dry comal over medium heat for about 4 minutes a side, until the masa is spotted and cooked through.
  5. Top with nopales, raw onion, and salsa. Serve hot.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Mashed black beans can replace the favas. Keep the filling dry enough to seal.
  • Crumbled fresh cheese and crema are a common topping. They add dairy, so the plate is no longer vegan or dairy-free.
  • A hotter salsa is a table choice. The cake itself is the same.

Pairings and uses

  • Street food
  • Savory meal
  • Snack
  • Sliced radishes and lime
  • A cup of hot corn atole, if you want a drink beside them
  • Black coffee
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Sopes

A small thick masa base with a raised rim, topped with beans, cheese, onion, and salsa.

30 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 sopes

History

Sopes are small, thick masa cakes. While the dough is still soft from the first side on the comal, the cook pinches a rim so the toppings stay on. They are not gorditas. A gordita is split into a pocket. A sope is an open boat. They are not tlacoyos either, which are oval and filled before cooking. The dough is nixtamalized masa, part of the corn cooking that is older in Mexico than wheat hotcakes.

Cultural background

Sopes are market and home antojitos. Toluca and Mexico City both sell them with beans, salsa, onion, and cheese. The rim is the defining move, and it has to be pinched before the dough dries out.

Why it tastes this way

The base is thick enough to taste of steamed corn in the center and toasted masa at the bottom. Refried beans add salt and body. Fresh cheese is milky and a little sour. Raw onion and a mild tomato salsa cut the starch. Because the toppings stay on the surface, you taste them separately from the corn instead of as a hidden filling.

How it is made

Pat small thick disks of masa, griddle one side, pinch a rim, cook through, and top with warm beans, cheese, onion, and salsa.

Authentic touch. After the first side sets, flip the sope and pinch the rim quickly while the masa is flexible. If you wait until it is crisp, the edge cracks instead of rising into a wall.

How it is used

Serve two or three sopes as a snack or a light meal. Eat them with your hands once the cheese softens from the heat of the masa.

Field notes

  • The pinched rim is made with your fingers, not with a mold, while the dough is half cooked.
  • A sope is open on top. A gordita is split. They start from similar masa and become different cakes.
  • Plain cornmeal will not hold a rim the way nixtamalized masa does.

Ingredients

  • 250 g masa harina
  • 300 ml warm water
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 250 g refried beans, warmed
  • 80 g crumbled fresh white cheese
  • 1 small onion, chopped
  • 1 cup mild tomato and jalapeño salsa

Steps

  1. Mix masa harina, salt, and warm water. Knead, cover, and rest 20 minutes.
  2. Divide into 8 pieces. Pat each into a thick disk about 8 centimeters across.
  3. Cook on a dry comal over medium heat for 2 minutes. Flip. While the top is soft, pinch up a rim all the way around.
  4. Cook another 3 to 4 minutes, turning once, until the base is spotted and the center is set.
  5. Spread on warm refried beans. Add cheese, onion, and salsa. Serve hot.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Potatoes and chorizo are a common topping. Chorizo adds meat and is not vegetarian.
  • Leave the cheese off and the sopes can be vegan and dairy-free.
  • A wider sope is sometimes called a picadita or a huarache when the shape changes. This recipe stays small and round.

Pairings and uses

  • Street food
  • Savory meal
  • Snack
  • Shredded lettuce for a colder crunch
  • Radishes and lime
  • Agua fresca
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Pupusas

Griddled masa stuffed with beans and melting cheese, served with tangy curtido and a light tomato salsa.

40 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 pupusas

History

Pupusas are the stuffed masa cakes of El Salvador, cooked on a griddle and served with curtido, a quick pickled cabbage, and a thin tomato salsa. The corn dough belongs to the older masa cooking of the isthmus, which predates wheat hotcakes in the region. Stuffing the round with cheese, beans, or both is the form people eat now. Chicharrón, fried pork, is another everyday filling. It is not in this recipe, and a pupusa made with it is not vegetarian.

Cultural background

Pupusas are dinner, a night-market meal, and Sunday food. In San Salvador they are cooked to order, patted by hand, and slid onto a hot comal. Curtido is made ahead so it has time to sour slightly.

Why it tastes this way

Nixtamalized masa toasts on the outside and stays moist where it meets the filling. Quesillo melts into the beans, salty and stretchy. The curtido is sour from vinegar, sweet from carrot, and bitter-green from cabbage, which cuts the starch. A mild salsa adds acid and a little chile. The combination is the dish, not the masa alone.

How it is made

Make a soft masa dough, seal beans and cheese inside thick rounds, and cook them in a lightly oiled comal until spotted and melted inside. Serve with curtido and salsa.

Authentic touch. Cup the dough in one hand, press a hole, add a small amount of filling, and seal it completely before you pat the round flat. Leaking cheese welds itself to the comal. Oil the comal lightly, unlike a dry tortilla griddle.

How it is used

Serve two pupusas on a plate with a pile of curtido and a spoon of salsa. They are eaten hot, often for dinner or a late snack.

Field notes

  • Curtido is cabbage, carrot, onion, oregano, and vinegar. It is a relish, not a cooked slaw.
  • A pupusa is sealed before it is cooked. A sope is left open and pinched at the rim.
  • This recipe uses cheese and beans. Add pork only if you accept that the pupusa is no longer vegetarian.

Ingredients

  • 250 g masa harina
  • 320 ml warm water
  • 1 teaspoon salt, divided
  • 200 g cooked red beans, mashed and cooled
  • 150 g grated quesillo or another fresh melting cow-milk cheese
  • 2 cups shredded cabbage, 1 carrot, 1/2 onion, 1 teaspoon dried oregano, and 80 ml vinegar for curtido
  • 2 tomatoes, 1 small green chile, and a pinch of salt for salsa
  • 1 tablespoon oil for the comal

Steps

  1. Stir cabbage, grated carrot, sliced onion, oregano, vinegar, and 1/2 teaspoon salt. Set the curtido aside while you cook.
  2. Mix masa harina, the remaining salt, and warm water. Rest the dough, covered, for 15 minutes. Chop the tomatoes and chile into a thin salsa.
  3. Mix the mashed beans with the cheese. Divide the dough into 8 pieces. Cup each piece, add a spoon of filling, seal, and pat into a round about 1 centimeter thick.
  4. Cook on a lightly oiled comal over medium heat for 4 to 5 minutes a side, until brown spots form and the center feels set.
  5. Serve hot with curtido and salsa.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Chicharrón, or beans mixed with chicharrón, is a standard filling. It contains meat and is not vegetarian.
  • Cheese alone, without beans, is the other common vegetarian filling.
  • Squash and cheese is a seasonal filling in some kitchens. The cheese keeps it a dairy dish.

Pairings and uses

  • Street food
  • Savory meal
  • Feast or fast day
  • Curtido heaped on the side
  • A thin tomato salsa
  • Horchata de morro, the Salvadoran seed drink
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Cuban torrejas

Day-old bread soaked in egg and milk, fried, and finished with cinnamon syrup and a splash of wine.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 torrejas

History

Cuban torrejas are a Christmas and New Year bread dish. Stale bread is soaked in egg and milk, fried until the outside sets, and bathed in syrup that may include a splash of wine. They are a relative of French toast. They are not a batter pancake, and they are not masa. The bread was already baked. The holiday work is the soak, the pan, and the syrup.

Cultural background

Families in Havana and across Cuba make torrejas when the bakeries have day-old bread and the table is set for Nochebuena or the days after. The dish uses eggs and milk on purpose, a rich plate for a short season.

Why it tastes this way

Stale bread has lost moisture, so it drinks the custard instead of falling apart. Frying gives the crust a browned egg flavor while the center stays soft. The syrup is cinnamon, citrus peel, and sugar, with wine adding a dry edge so the plate is not only sweet. Milk in the soak tastes plain and creamy against the spice.

How it is made

Whisk eggs, milk, sugar, and cinnamon. Soak stale bread, fry it in a shallow layer of oil, and finish it in a light syrup of sugar, water, cinnamon, lime peel, and a little dry wine.

Authentic touch. Use bread that is dry all the way through. Fresh bread turns to paste in the egg. Soak it long enough to reach the center, then let the extra drip off before it goes into the pan.

How it is used

Serve warm on a holiday dessert plate, with extra syrup. They are not a weekday breakfast batter. They belong to the Christmas table.

Field notes

  • Torrejas are fried soaked bread, a holiday relative of French toast, not a poured batter.
  • Day-old bread is the right start. Very fresh bread collapses in the egg.
  • The wine goes in the syrup. It is not there to ferment a dough.

Ingredients

  • 8 slices day-old white bread
  • 2 eggs
  • 180 ml milk
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1 teaspoon ground cinnamon, divided
  • Neutral oil or butter for the pan

Trinidadian dhalpuri roti

A soft wheat roti with a layer of seasoned ground split peas that flakes when the bread is clapped.

History

After emancipation in the British Caribbean, planters recruited indentured workers from India to keep the cane fields going. Indenture was a fixed contract, often five years, and it was not free passage. The first ships from India reached Trinidad in 1845. Guyana received workers on the same system. Cultural background

Dhalpuri is the wrap around curries at roti shops in Port of Spain and in Guyanese lunchrooms. The filling is called dhal, but it is ground and dried, not a soup. Families also cook it for Sunday lunch. The recipe uses oil, not ghee, so it stays vegan.

Why it tastes this way

The split-pea filling tastes of cumin, garlic, and black pepper, toasted by the griddle through the wheat skin. Because the peas are ground and dry, they flake inside the bread instead of steaming it into paste. The roti itself is plain wheat and salt, a little rich from the oil you wipe on while it cooks. Clapping the hot bread breaks the pea layer into a loose crumble.

How it is made

Boil yellow split peas, drain them very well, and grind them with garlic, cumin, salt, and pepper. Seal a spoonful inside a wheat dough, roll it out, and cook it on a hot tawa with a little oil.

Authentic touch. The pea paste has to be dry and fully cooled before it goes into the dough. Wet filling bursts and soaks the roti. Roll gently so the peas spread in a thin sheet without tearing the surface.

How it is used

Use the roti to wrap a curry, or eat it beside a stew. In a shop it is often the edible plate under goat, chicken, or a potato and chickpea curry. Those fillings are separate from this bread.

  • The first indenture ships from India arrived in Trinidad in 1845, after emancipation.

Why it tastes this way

Precooked cornmeal cooks up dense and corny, with a skin that toasts and a center that stays steamy if the dough was wet enough. Salt is the only seasoning in the cake, so the flavor is plain corn, not chile and not wheat. Fresh cheese melts at the edges and salts the interior once the cake is split. The contrast is the point of the modern sandwich: a bland, toasted shell and a rich filling.

Stir precooked white cornmeal into warm salted water, rest it, and form thick cakes. Griddle them until a crust forms and the inside is set, then split and fill with cheese.

History

Tacu-tacu is a cake of leftover rice and beans, fried in a skillet until the outside is crisp. It is Lima criollo cooking. Afro-Peruvian cooks helped build that coastal cuisine in the colonial city and afterward, working with Indigenous ingredients and Spanish techniques in kitchens shaped by slavery and, later, by free Black communities. The dish itself is practical: the previous day's rice and beans, garlic, onion, and ají amarillo, pressed into a hot pan. A fried egg or a breaded steak is a common addition. Those are not in this recipe.

Cultural background

Criollo cooking is the coastal home cooking of Lima and other Peruvian port cities. Tacu-tacu is what a cook does with rice and beans that are already seasoned and a little dry. It is lunch or a heavy breakfast. This version stays with the rice and beans so the plate can be vegan.

Why it tastes this way

Day-old rice fries harder and crisper than fresh rice, which is why leftovers are the right start. Beans break into the grains and salt the cake. Garlic and onion, cooked first, perfume the oil. Ají amarillo is fruity and mildly hot, enough to set the heat at mild without burning. There is no cheese and no egg in the cake, so the flavor stays with the beans, the rice, and the chile.

How it is made

Fry onion and garlic, add ají amarillo, cooked beans, and cooked rice. Mash part of the mixture, press it into a cake in the skillet, and fry until a crust forms on both sides.

Authentic touch. Use rice and beans that are dry enough to mash and hold a shape. Wet rice steams and never crusts. Press the cake into the pan and leave it alone until the bottom is brown. Then flip once. Stirring makes fried rice, not tacu-tacu.

How it is used

Slide the cake onto a plate for lunch or breakfast. A fried egg on top is a different plate and adds egg. Breaded steak adds meat. The cake here is the rice and beans alone.

Field notes

  • Tacu-tacu is leftover rice and beans fried into a cake, a Lima criollo dish.
  • Afro-Peruvian cooks were part of building that coastal kitchen. The page stays with the dish and that history.
  • Let the cake sit in the pan long enough to crust. Stirring it turns it into fried rice.

  • 3 cups cooked white rice, cold
  • 2 cups cooked canary or black beans, drained

  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 tablespoon ají amarillo paste
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 3 tablespoons neutral oil

Steps

  1. Heat 1 tablespoon oil and cook the onion and garlic until soft. Stir in the ají amarillo paste.
  2. Add the beans and mash about half of them into the onion. Fold in the rice and salt. The mixture should hold together.
  3. Heat the remaining oil in a skillet over medium heat. Add half the mixture and press it into a thick cake.
  4. Cook without stirring until the bottom is brown, then flip and brown the other side. Repeat with the rest.
  5. Serve hot.

  • A breaded steak beside the cake is a restaurant plate and adds meat.
  • Leave out the ají and the heat drops to none. The written recipe includes a tablespoon.

Paraguayan mbeju

A crisp griddled cake of manioc starch, cornmeal, and cheese, salty at the edge and tender in the middle.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 1 large cake

History

Mbeju is a Paraguayan breakfast cake made from manioc starch, corn flour, and cheese, cooked on a griddle until the edge crisps. Manioc, cassava, is an old staple along the Paraguay River. The starch in a modern kitchen is commercial, already processed. This recipe uses that starch and does not teach you to grind raw cassava. Cheese and butter make it a dairy dish. It is often eaten with cocido, a hot yerba mate infusion, which is a drink beside the plate and not an ingredient in the dough.

Cultural background

In Asunción and in the countryside, mbeju is breakfast and an afternoon bite, sometimes cooked in a broad pan so several people can break pieces off. The dough is crumbly when it goes into the pan and sets as the starch heats. Corn flour keeps it from being only elastic tapioca.

Why it tastes this way

Manioc starch goes chewy and then crisp as it dries on the griddle. Cornmeal adds a toasted grain note. Fresh cheese salts the cake and browns in spots. Butter helps the edge fry. There is no sugar and no chile, so the flavor stays savory and mild. Cocido beside it is smoky and bitter, which is why the two share a table.

How it is made

Rub commercial manioc starch with cornmeal, grated fresh cheese, butter, salt, and a splash of milk. Press the crumb into a skillet and cook slowly until the bottom crisps and the center sets. Flip once if it will hold.

Authentic touch. Mix the starch, cornmeal, cheese, and butter to a damp crumb, not a pourable batter. Press it into a hot pan and let it set before you touch it. Turning too soon breaks the cake. The cheese should be fresh and salty, not a hard aged cheese that stays in pebbles.

How it is used

Break it into wedges at breakfast, with cocido or coffee. It is a bread and a cheese dish at the same time.

Field notes

  • Use commercial manioc starch. Raw bitter cassava can be poisonous, and this recipe does not teach home processing.
  • Mbeju is starch, corn, and cheese on a griddle, a Paraguayan breakfast.
  • Cocido is the drink beside it, not a liquid in the dough.

Ingredients

  • 150 g commercial manioc starch (cassava starch)
  • 80 g fine cornmeal
  • 100 g grated fresh cheese
  • 30 g butter
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons milk

Steps

  1. Rub the manioc starch, cornmeal, cheese, butter, and salt together until the mixture looks like damp crumbs. Sprinkle on the milk and mix. It should clump, not pour.
  2. Heat a skillet over medium-low heat and press the crumb in an even layer.
  3. Cook without stirring until the bottom is crisp and the center has set, about 10 minutes.
  4. Flip in one piece if you can, or cut it in the pan and turn the pieces. Cook until the second side spots.
  5. Serve warm, in wedges.

Use commercial manioc (cassava) starch. Raw bitter cassava can be poisonous. This recipe does not teach home processing of raw cassava.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pork fat instead of butter is used in some kitchens. It adds meat and is not vegetarian.
  • More cornmeal makes a mealier cake. More starch makes it chewier.
  • An egg in the dough adds the egg allergen and is not required here.
