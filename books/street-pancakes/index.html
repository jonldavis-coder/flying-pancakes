Apam Balik

A thick turnover pancake filled with crushed peanuts and sugar.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 turnovers

History

Apam balik is a Malaysian and Singaporean turnover pancake, cooked thick, filled with peanuts, sugar, and sometimes sweet corn or creamed corn, then folded. It is a night-market snack. Peanuts are in the classic filling, so this recipe includes them and is not nut-free. A peanut-free sugar-and-corn filling is the variation for anyone who cannot eat peanuts.

Cultural background

The crumb is soft and cakey. The filling is sweet and crunchy. Vendors fold it while hot.

Why it tastes this way

A thick batter on a covered griddle tastes like a soft cake, and the peanut-sugar filling melts into it. Peanuts are the crunch of a market snack. Sweet corn, when used, adds moisture. The fold is why it is called balik, turned over. It is a street pancake, not a breakfast crepe.

How it is made

A thick sweet batter is cooked, filled with peanut and sugar, and folded.

Authentic touch. Cook covered so the thick batter sets, add the filling, and fold before the top dries. Chop the peanuts fine so the fold seals.

How it is used

Warm, in wedges.

Field notes

  • Peanuts are the classic filling and a serious allergen.
  • Apam balik is folded, which is the balik.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1 egg
  • 3/4 cup milk
  • 1/2 cup finely chopped roasted peanuts
  • 2 tablespoons brown sugar
  • Butter

Steps

  1. Mix a thick batter from flour, baking powder, sugar, egg, and milk.
  2. Cook a thick round covered on medium-low until the center sets.
  3. Sprinkle peanuts and brown sugar on half and fold.
  4. Cook 1 minute more.
  5. Cut and serve warm.
  6. Peanuts are part of this classic filling.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Skip the peanuts and use sugar and a spoon of creamed corn if you need a peanut-free cake. It will not be the classic.
  • A banana inside is a home addition.

Arepa de Choclo

A sweet fresh-corn arepa, often with cheese, folded or topped.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 6 cakes

History

Arepa de choclo is a Colombian fresh-corn cake, especially associated with places that sell it with cheese as a sweet-salty breakfast. Choclo is fresh corn. It is closer to a cachapa than to a masarepa arepa. Cheese is part of the usual plate, so this recipe includes it and is not vegan.

Cultural background

Street vendors cook them on a griddle. The corn is sweet. The cheese melts. They are eaten with the hands.

Why it tastes this way

Fresh corn’s sugar browns on the griddle, and a salty fresh cheese is the contrast Colombians expect. That sweet-salt pair is the dish. It is a different corn from the dried masarepa of a plain arepa: juicy, seasonal, and closer to a pancake. The griddle, not an oven, is the tool.

How it is made

Fresh corn is blended, cooked as thick cakes, and topped with cheese.

Authentic touch. Blend the corn coarse. If the batter is watery, a spoon of masarepa or cornmeal helps. Put the cheese on while the cake is hot so it softens.

How it is used

Folded or open, hot.

Field notes

  • Choclo means fresh corn, not dried masarepa.
  • Cheese on top is the usual sweet-salt bite.

Ingredients

  • 4 ears sweet corn
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon masarepa, if needed
  • 1 cup crumbled fresh cheese
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Blend the corn and salt. Add masarepa if watery.
  2. Cook thick cakes on a medium griddle.
  3. Top with cheese while hot.
  4. Fold or serve open.
  5. Eat immediately.
  6. The corn should taste sweet.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Without cheese it is closer to a cachapa and can be vegan.
  • Cachapas are the Venezuelan sister.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Street food
  • queso fresco
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Arepa de Huevo

A fried corn cake with an egg inside, a coastal street food.

20 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 arepas

History

Arepa de huevo is a street food of Colombia’s Caribbean coast: a corn dough disk, slit, filled with an egg, sealed, and fried. It is associated with cities such as Cartagena and Barranquilla. It is a fried arepa, not a griddled one, and the egg is the point.

Cultural background

The dough is masarepa, often colored with achiote. The egg should set inside. It is eaten hot, standing up.

Why it tastes this way

Frying a sealed corn cake cooks the egg in a pocket, so you get a crisp shell and a set egg in one bite. Achiote, if you use it, adds color and a mild earthiness common on the coast. The fry is heavier than a comal arepa, which is why it eats like street food. Corn is still the structure. The egg is the filling.

How it is made

Masarepa disks are filled with an egg, sealed, and fried until the egg is cooked.

Authentic touch. Seal the edge well and fry at a steady medium heat. Too hot, and the dough browns before the egg sets. Too cool, and the cake drinks oil.

How it is used

Hot, as a snack.

Field notes

  • The egg is sealed inside and fried on the Caribbean coast.
  • The egg should be cooked through, not liquid.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups masarepa
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 cups warm water
  • 4 eggs
  • Oil for frying

Steps

  1. Mix a soft dough and shape 4 disks. Make a pocket in each and slip in an egg. Seal well.
  2. Flatten gently.
  3. Fry in hot oil over medium until the dough is golden and the egg is cooked through.
  4. Drain.
  5. Serve hot.
  6. If the egg is still liquid, fry longer at a lower heat.

The egg must be cooked through. Fry over steady heat.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A griddled arepa with a fried egg on top is easier and is not this street food.
  • Achiote in the dough is traditional color.

Pairings and uses

  • Street food
  • Breakfast
  • aji sauce
  • Vegetarian

Baleadas

A soft flour tortilla folded around refried beans and cheese.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 baleadas

History

Baleadas are a Honduran breakfast and street food: a large flour tortilla folded over refried beans, crema, and cheese. The north coast is especially associated with them. The tortilla is wheat. Beans and cheese are the simple filling. Eggs or meat make a baleada especial and are variations.

Cultural background

The tortilla should be soft and a bit blistered. Beans are mashed and salted. Crema is tangy. This recipe uses beans and cheese. Crema adds more dairy.

Why it tastes this way

A soft wheat tortilla folds without cracking, which is why the dish works as a portable breakfast. Refried beans are savory and filling. Crema and cheese are the dairy richness of the Honduran plate. It is a flour-tortilla dish, not a corn pupusa, and the wheat is the northern Central American choice for this particular wrap.

How it is made

Flour tortillas are cooked on a comal and folded around refried beans and cheese.

Authentic touch. Cook the tortilla until it puffs in spots and keep it soft. Spread a thin layer of beans. A thick layer tears the bread when you fold it.

How it is used

Warm, folded.

Field notes

  • A baleada is a flour tortilla folded over beans and cheese.
  • A thin layer of beans is what lets it fold.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups flour
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 3/4 cup warm water
  • 1 1/2 cups refried beans
  • 1 cup crumbled cheese

Steps

  1. Mix a soft dough and rest 15 minutes.
  2. Roll 6 thin rounds and cook on a comal until spotted and soft.
  3. Spread beans and cheese and fold.
  4. Serve warm.
  5. Keep the tortillas covered.
  6. A thin layer of beans folds. A thick one tears.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • An egg or avocado is a common addition. Avocado adds no allergen.
  • Flour tortillas of northern Mexico are the bread technique.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Street food
  • Stuffed or filled
  • refried beans

Bánh Khọt

Tiny crisp rice cakes cooked in a well pan, topped with shrimp.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 16 cakes

History

Bánh khọt are small southern Vietnamese rice cakes cooked in a special well pan, crisp at the rim, often topped with a single shrimp. They are a street snack, eaten with herbs and nước chấm. The batter is rice flour and turmeric, kin to bánh xèo in miniature.

Cultural background

An appe or paniyaram pan is a fair tool. Each well gets a little oil, batter, and a piece of shrimp. They are eaten immediately.

Why it tastes this way

A small well fries the sides of a rice batter so the rim shatters and the center stays tender. Shrimp on each cake is the savory bite. Turmeric colors the batter. Herbs and a salty-sour dip are the freshness. The format is street food: many small cakes, not one large crepe.

How it is made

A thin rice batter is cooked in oiled wells with a piece of shrimp on each.

Authentic touch. Oil each well and get the pan hot. Do not fill the wells to the brim. The shrimp should be cooked through when the cake is crisp.

How it is used

Hot, with herbs and a lime-fish sauce dip, or a soy-lime dip if you are avoiding fish sauce.

Field notes

  • Bánh khọt are the small well-pan cakes.
  • A shrimp on each one is the usual topping.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup rice flour
  • 1 cup coconut milk
  • 1/4 teaspoon turmeric
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 16 small shrimp
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Whisk a thin batter.
  2. Oil a hot well pan.
  3. Add a spoon of batter and one shrimp to each well.
  4. Cook until the rims are crisp and the shrimp are opaque. Turn if the pan allows.
  5. Drain briefly.
  6. Serve at once with herbs.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Bánh xèo is the large crepe.
  • Without shrimp the cakes are a different, milder snack.

Pairings and uses

  • Street food
  • Snack

Bánh Xèo

A crisp turmeric rice crepe filled with shrimp, bean sprouts, and herbs.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 crepes

History

Bánh xèo is the sizzling rice-flour crepe of Vietnam, named for the sound of batter on a hot pan. Turmeric colors it. Shrimp and pork are usual fillings, with bean sprouts and a pile of herbs, eaten wrapped in lettuce with nước chấm. This version uses shrimp and leaves out pork. Cambodian bánh xèo is a cousin. The crepe is rice flour and coconut milk.

Cultural background

It should be thin and shatter at the edge. The herbs are not a garnish. They are half the bite. The filling is savory and the wrapper is crisp.

Why it tastes this way

Rice flour and coconut milk fry into a lacy, rich crepe that wheat would make tough and pale. Turmeric is color and a mild earthiness. Shrimp is the sweet saline filling of a coastal crepe. Herbs and lettuce make it fresh. The high heat is why it crackles, the xèo of the name.

How it is made

A thin rice and coconut batter is cooked crisp, filled with shrimp and sprouts, and folded.

Authentic touch. Use a hot pan and a thin batter. Add the filling as soon as the batter sets, cover briefly so the shrimp cook, and fold. Do not stack the crepes or they steam soft.

How it is used

With lettuce, mint, and a dipping sauce of lime, fish sauce, and chile. Fish sauce adds fish. A soy-lime dip is the fish-free option and should be called that.

Field notes

  • The name refers to the sizzle.
  • This written filling is shrimp, not pork.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup rice flour
  • 1 cup coconut milk
  • 1/2 teaspoon turmeric
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 8 ounces shrimp
  • 2 cups bean sprouts
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Whisk rice flour, coconut milk, turmeric, salt, and enough water to make a thin batter.
  2. Oil a hot skillet, pour a thin crepe, and lay on shrimp and sprouts.
  3. Cover until the shrimp are opaque and the edge is crisp.
  4. Fold and serve.
  5. Do not stack.
  6. Herbs and lettuce belong on the plate.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pork alongside the shrimp is common.
  • A vegetarian filling of mushrooms is a different crepe.

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Street food
  • Supper

  • mint
  • lime

Basque Talo

A toasted corn flatbread, often wrapped around chistorra or chocolate.

20 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 talo

History

Talo is a Basque corn flatbread, cooked on a griddle, traditionally wrapped around chistorra sausage or, for a sweet, around chocolate. Corn arrived in the Basque Country and became talo flour, a coarser meal. The bread is festival and cider-house food as well as home food. This recipe is the bread. The sausage is a separate meat.

Cultural background

It should be flexible enough to wrap and toasted enough to taste corn. It is thicker than a crepe and thinner than a loaf.

Why it tastes this way

Coarse cornmeal tastes toasted and slightly sweet, a wrap that does not fight a salty sausage or a piece of chocolate. The griddle is the Basque tool for this bread. Corn replaced earlier grains in this particular cake after maize spread in Europe. The two fillings, sausage and chocolate, show how the same bread works as street food and as a treat.

How it is made

Cornmeal, salt, and water are patted into rounds and cooked on a griddle.

Authentic touch. Rest the dough so the meal hydrates. Cook it on a dry or lightly oiled griddle until it spots and is cooked through, still pliable.

How it is used

Warm, wrapped around a cooked sausage or a piece of chocolate, or simply buttered.

Field notes

  • Talo is a Basque corn flatbread for sausage or chocolate.
  • The sausage is a filling, not part of the dough.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups cornmeal
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 1/4 cups boiling water

Steps

  1. Stir cornmeal, salt, and boiling water. Rest 10 minutes.
  2. Pat thin rounds.
  3. Cook on a medium dry griddle until spotted and cooked through, still flexible.
  4. Wrap and serve.
  5. A sausage or chocolate is a filling, not part of the dough.
  6. Add a spoon of water if the dough cracks.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A wheat flour addition makes them easier and removes the gluten-free label.
  • They are a bread, not a syrup pancake.

Benne Dosa

A crisp fermented dosa finished with a notable amount of butter.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 dosas

History

Benne dosa is the butter dosa of Davangere in Karnataka, a tiffin-room specialty. Benne means butter. The dosa batter is the fermented one, and the butter on the tawa is generous. A potato filling is common. The butter is the defining point, so this recipe is not vegan and not dairy-free.

Cultural background

It is a restaurant breakfast more than a temple recipe. People go to Davangere, and to restaurants that copy it, for the butter.

Why it tastes this way

Butter on a hot tawa browns and flavors the rice batter in a way oil does not, which is the entire reason to name the dosa after butter. The ferment still supplies tang. The contrast of crisp edge and rich fat is the plate. It is a city specialty built on an older batter, and the dairy is honest.

How it is made

Fermented dosa batter is cooked on a buttered tawa until crisp.

Authentic touch. Use butter, not only oil, and let it foam on the tawa before the batter goes down. Too little butter and you have made a plain dosa.

How it is used

With chutney and, if you like, a potato filling.

Field notes

  • Benne means butter. The fat is the specialty.
  • Davangere is the city most tied to the name.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups fermented dosa batter
  • 4 tablespoons butter
  • A pinch of salt if the batter needs it

Steps

  1. Heat a tawa and add a spoon of butter.
  2. Spread a ladle of batter thin.
  3. Cook until crisp, adding a little more butter at the edge.
  4. Fold and serve.
  5. Repeat, refreshing the butter.
  6. Chutney on the side.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Oil instead of butter is a different, vegan dosa. Do not call it benne.
  • A potato filling makes it a meal.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Street food
  • coconut chutney
  • potato masala
  • Vegetarian

Bindaetteok

A crisp mung-bean pancake with cabbage and kimchi.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 pancakes

History

Bindaetteok, also nokdujeon, are mung-bean pancakes. Soaked mung beans are ground, mixed with kimchi or vegetables, and fried. They are a popular snack and drinking food, and an older grain cake than the wheat pajeon. This version keeps them free of wheat and meat.

Cultural background

The edge should be lacy. The bean flavor is earthy. Kimchi adds acid. A dipping sauce is optional because the cake is already seasoned.

Why it tastes this way

Mung beans taste earthy and fry crisp when the batter is coarse, not a smooth milk. Kimchi juice seasons the batter without a long spice list. The cake is a bean pancake in a wheat-pancake culture, which is why it has its own name. Oil and a hot pan make the lace. A blender that purees it too fine makes a dense cake.

How it is made

Soaked mung beans are ground coarse, mixed with kimchi and cabbage, and fried as thick pancakes.

Authentic touch. Grind the beans until they are gritty, not baby-food smooth. Squeeze a little of the water off if the batter is a puddle. Fry in enough oil that the edge frills.

How it is used

Hot, in wedges. A vinegar dip is optional.

Field notes

  • Bindaetteok are mung-bean pancakes, also called nokdujeon.
  • A coarse grind fries lacier than a smooth puree.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup dried mung beans, soaked 8 hours and drained
  • 1/2 cup chopped kimchi
  • 1/2 cup chopped napa cabbage
  • 1 clove garlic
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Grind the beans with garlic and a splash of water until coarse and spreadable.
  2. Stir in kimchi, cabbage, and salt.
  3. Fry four thick cakes in a filmed skillet until both sides are crisp and the center is cooked.
  4. Drain.
  5. Serve hot.
  6. Add water only if the batter will not spread.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pork in the batter is a common version and adds meat.
  • A smooth grind is a different, heavier texture.

Brazilian Tapioca Crepes

A crisp-edged crepe of hydrated tapioca starch, filled with cheese or coconut.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 crepes

History

Tapioca crepes are a Brazilian street and home breakfast made from gummed tapioca starch, not a wheat batter. The starch is moistened until it clumps, then scattered in a pan, where it binds into a crepe. Cheese or coconut and condensed milk are fillings. The starch itself is cassava, a crop with deep Indigenous roots in Brazil. Use commercial tapioca starch.

Cultural background

A good tapioca has a lacy edge and a flexible center. Queijo coalho is a traditional salty cheese. This filling uses a firm cheese and notes coconut as the vegan option.

Why it tastes this way

Tapioca starch has almost no flavor and a lot of chew, so the filling is the taste: salty cheese or sweet coconut. The pan hydrates the starch into a sheet, which is a different chemistry from wheat gluten. Cassava is the Indigenous and later national starch. The crepe format is a modern street breakfast. Both can be true.

How it is made

Moistened tapioca starch is cooked as a crepe and filled.

Authentic touch. Moisten the starch until it holds when squeezed and still crumbles. Scatter it in an even layer in a moderate pan. Do not stir. It will knit itself. Flip once, quickly.

How it is used

Folded, hot. Cheese is the dairy filling. Coconut and a little sugar are the vegan one.

Field notes

  • The crepe is tapioca starch, not wheat.
  • Cheese is one filling. Coconut and sugar are the vegan one.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups tapioca starch
  • About 3/4 cup water
  • 1 cup grated firm cheese, or 1 cup grated coconut plus 2 tablespoons sugar

Steps

  1. Sprinkle water over the starch and toss until it clumps when squeezed and still crumbles.
  2. Scatter a thin even layer in a medium pan. Do not stir.
  3. When the sheet knits, flip it.
  4. Add cheese, or coconut and sugar, and fold.
  5. Serve immediately.
  6. Use commercial tapioca starch.

Use commercial cassava flour or yuca sold ready to cook. Do not process bitter cassava at home from these pages.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Coconut and sugar instead of cheese keep it vegan.
  • Beiju is the plainer cassava bread.

Venezuela · South America

Cachapas

A sweet-corn pancake, soft and irregular, often folded with cheese.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 6 cachapas

History

Cachapas are Venezuelan pancakes of fresh ground sweet corn, cooked on a griddle and often folded around cheese, queso de mano or similar. They are street and home food. The batter is the corn itself, sometimes loosened with a little milk or water. This recipe keeps the batter dairy-free and serves cheese as an optional fold, so the cake can be vegan and the cheese is a choice.

Cultural background

The corn must be sweet and juicy. Canned corn, drained and blended, is a workable off-season substitute and tastes less alive. Say which you used.

Why it tastes this way

Fresh corn is sweet because the kernels are immature and full of sugar, which is why a cachapa tastes like corn and dessert at once even when the filling is salty cheese. The griddle browns the natural sugar. No wheat is required. The cheese, when used, is salty and squeaky, the contrast. Street griddles make them irregular. That rustic edge is normal.

How it is made

Fresh corn is blended with a little salt and cooked as thick pancakes.

Authentic touch. Blend the corn until it is a coarse batter, not a juice. Cook on medium and cover briefly so the center sets. It will not look like a smooth wheat pancake.

How it is used

Folded, with cheese if you eat dairy, or plain.

Field notes

  • Cachapas are fresh-corn pancakes. Cheese is optional here.
  • Canned corn works and tastes less alive than fresh.

Ingredients

  • 4 ears sweet corn, kernels cut off
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon cornmeal, if needed
  • Oil
  • Cheese, optional

Steps

  1. Blend the corn and salt to a coarse batter. Add cornmeal if it is watery.
  2. Cook thick rounds on a medium oiled griddle, covered briefly.
  3. Flip when the underside is spotted.
  4. Fold with cheese if you eat dairy.
  5. Serve hot.
  6. They will look rustic.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

History

Crêpes are thin pancakes cooked across France. In Brittany they are a regional pride, and February 2, Chandeleur, is the candle-day associated with eating them. That date is Candlemas, not Shrove Tuesday, though both belong to the late-winter Christian calendar. Sweet crêpes are usually wheat. Savory buckwheat galettes are the other Breton cake and have their own page.

Cultural background

Street stands and home kitchens both cook them. Sugar, jam, chocolate, or a squeeze of lemon are ordinary finishes. Crêpes Suzette is a restaurant dish, not the everyday cake.

Why it tastes this way

A rested batter of flour, egg, milk, and butter spreads paper-thin and tastes of browned butter rather than of baking powder. Wheat, not buckwheat, is what makes a sweet crêpe pale and mild enough for sugar. Chandeleur explains a spike in home cooking. The rest of the year, the cake is simply a fast dessert.

How it is made

A thin wheat batter is swirled in a hot, lightly buttered pan and cooked briefly on both sides.

Authentic touch. Rest the batter at least 30 minutes, or the gluten snaps back and the crêpe tears. The first one seasons the pan and is rarely the prettiest.

How it is used

Sugar, jam, lemon, or a chocolate spread. Fold or roll.

Field notes

  • Chandeleur, February 2, is the French crêpe day most often named. It is not the same day as Shrove Tuesday.
  • Sweet crêpes are wheat. Galettes are buckwheat.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 2 large eggs
  • 1 1/4 cups milk
  • 2 tablespoons melted butter, plus more for the pan
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • Pinch of salt

Steps

  1. Blend or whisk into a smooth batter. Rest 30 minutes.
  2. Heat a crêpe pan and film it with butter.
  3. Swirl a thin layer and cook until the edge browns.
  4. Flip for a few seconds.
  5. Fill or sugar, and fold.
  6. Keep finished crêpes warm under a plate.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Buckwheat galettes are the savory Breton cousin.
  • A splash of beer or cider in the batter is a Breton home habit and changes the flavor.

Taiwanese Danbing

A thin breakfast crepe with egg, scallion, and a salty-sweet sauce.

10 min prep · 10 min cook · Serves 2 · 2 rolls

History

Danbing is a Taiwanese breakfast crepe: a thin batter cooked on a griddle with egg, often a cruller or youtiao inside, and a savory sauce. Breakfast shops cook them to order. It is related to northern jianbing and is its own breakfast-shop food. This version uses a wheat crepe, egg, and sauce, with the cruller as an optional variation.

Cultural background

It is wrapped and eaten with soy milk at a breakfast shop. The crepe is soft-crisp. The egg is fully cooked.

Why it tastes this way

Egg on a thin crepe is the protein of a fast breakfast, and the sauce, often a sweet soy or a miso-like bean paste, makes it salty-sweet. Scallion is the fresh note. Taiwanese breakfast shops standardized the wrap. Wheat batter is what those shops usually pour. The speed of the griddle is why the edge crisps before the egg dries out.

How it is made

A thin batter is cooked with egg and scallion, sauced, and rolled.

Authentic touch. Spread the batter thin, add the egg at once, and roll the crepe as soon as the egg sets. Overcooking makes a stiff tube.

How it is used

Hot, as breakfast.

Field notes

  • Danbing is a Taiwanese breakfast-shop crepe.
  • The egg should be cooked through in the roll.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup flour
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 2 eggs
  • 2 scallions, sliced
  • 1 tablespoon soy sauce
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Whisk flour and water. Stir soy and sugar together.
  2. Cook a thin crepe in an oiled pan.
  3. Pour a beaten egg on it with scallion and cook until the egg sets.
  4. Brush with the soy-sugar mix and roll.
  5. Repeat.
  6. Serve immediately.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A fried cruller inside is the fuller shop version.
  • Jianbing is the northern cousin, often with a cracker and cilantro.

Pairings and uses

Tlayudas

A large, partly dried corn tortilla, toasted and topped with beans and cheese.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 tlayudas

History

Tlayudas are large Oaxacan corn tortillas, often cooked until they are partly dry and crisp, then topped with refried beans, quesillo or asiento, cabbage, and salsa. They are a night-market food of Oaxaca. The tortilla is the base. This recipe uses large corn tortillas you cook further on a comal, with beans and cheese. Asiento, unrefined pork fat, is traditional and is not in this version.

Cultural background

They are a meal. The tortilla should be toasted, not soft like a fresh taco tortilla. Quesillo is the stringy Oaxacan cheese. Mozzarella is only a stand-in and should be named as such if you use it.

Why it tastes this way

A large masa tortilla toasted on a comal tastes nuttier and crisper than a fresh soft tortilla, which is why it can hold beans without falling apart. Oaxaca’s corn diversity is in the masa. The toppings are the market meal. Cheese adds salt and stretch. Leaving out asiento keeps the plate free of pork and changes it from some street versions. That change is stated.

How it is made

Large corn tortillas are toasted on a comal, spread with beans and cheese, and heated until the cheese softens.

Authentic touch. Toast the tortilla until it is dry and blistered but not burnt to carbon. Spread beans thinly. Too much weight cracks it.

How it is used

Folded or open, with salsa and shredded cabbage.

Field notes

  • Tlayudas are the large toasted tortillas of Oaxaca.
  • This version leaves out pork asiento and says so.

Ingredients

  • 4 large corn tortillas
  • 1 cup refried beans
  • 1 cup shredded quesillo or mozzarella
  • 1 cup shredded cabbage
  • Salsa
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Toast the tortillas on a comal until dry and blistered but not burnt.
  2. Spread a thin layer of beans.
  3. Add cheese and heat until it softens.
  4. Top with cabbage and salsa.
  5. Serve immediately.
  6. Do not overload them.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Asiento is the traditional pork fat and adds meat.
  • A fully soft tortilla is a different, taco-like bite.

Tortillitas de Camarones

Lacy chickpea-and-shrimp fritters from Cádiz.

15 min prep · 10 min cook · Serves 4 · about 12 fritters

History

Tortillitas de camarones are a Cádiz tapa: tiny shrimp in a thin chickpea-flour batter, fried into lacy cakes. They are a specialty of the Bay of Cádiz, where small shrimp and chickpea flour meet. They are fritters, and they are the shrimp pancake of southern Spain.

Cultural background

They should be thin and crisp, eaten immediately with a squeeze of lemon. The shrimp are small. If you use larger shrimp, chop them.

Why it tastes this way

Chickpea flour fries into a nutty lace, and the shrimp taste sweet and saline because they are the local catch, not a garnish. A very thin batter is why you can see the shrimp. Olive oil is the frying fat of Andalusia. Lemon is the acid. The tapa is a port food.

How it is made

Chickpea flour, water, parsley, and chopped shrimp are fried as thin lacy cakes.

Authentic touch. The batter should be as thin as cream. Fry spoonfuls in hot oil and do not let them brown black. Drain well. Eat at once.

How it is used

Immediately, with lemon.

Field notes

  • These are a Cádiz specialty.
  • Thin batter and hot oil make the lace.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup chickpea flour
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 4 ounces small shrimp, chopped
  • 2 tablespoons chopped parsley
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Olive oil for frying
  • Lemon

Steps

  1. Whisk chickpea flour, water, parsley, and salt into a thin batter. Stir in the shrimp.
  2. Fry spoonfuls in hot oil until lacy and golden.
  3. Drain.
  4. Serve at once with lemon.
  5. Stir the batter so the flour does not settle.
  6. The shrimp should be cooked through.

Fry over steady heat. Shrimp should be cooked through.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Without shrimp they are a different, vegan chickpea fritter and should not keep the name.
  • Onion in the batter is used by some cooks.

Vitumbua

Coconut rice cakes, sweet and cardamom-scented, crisp outside.

1 hr 10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · about 12 cakes

History

Vitumbua are Swahili coast rice-and-coconut cakes, cooked in a special pan with round wells, related in shape to appam pans and to æbleskiver pans. They are a Zanzibar and coastal Tanzanian breakfast and snack. Rice, coconut milk, sugar, and cardamom are the flavor. Yeast lifts them.

Cultural background

The coconut is the coast. Cardamom is the Indian Ocean trade. They are sweet, not savory, and they are eaten with tea.

Why it tastes this way

Coconut milk makes the crumb rich without dairy, and rice keeps it soft. Cardamom is a traded spice that became local in coastal sweets. The well pan fries the sides so the cake is crisp outside and tender inside, a shape that suits street selling. Sugar is moderate. The coconut is the sweetness people remember.

How it is made

A yeasted batter of rice flour and coconut milk is cooked in round wells.

Authentic touch. Use full-fat coconut milk. Light coconut milk makes them dry. Turn the cakes in the wells if your pan is deep, so both ends brown. An æbleskiver or appe pan is a fair tool.

How it is used

Warm, with tea. They are already sweet.

Field notes

  • Vitumbua are a Swahili coast coconut rice cake.
  • Cardamom points at Indian Ocean trade, not at a random spice.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup rice flour
  • 1 cup coconut milk
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1/2 teaspoon cardamom
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • Oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Stir the batter and rest 1 hour until slightly risen.
  2. Oil an æbleskiver, appe, or similar well pan over medium.
  3. Cook the batter in the wells, turning, until golden and set.
  4. Serve warm.
  5. Do not use low-fat coconut milk if you can help it.
  6. They are best the day they are cooked.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A little mashed banana in the batter is a home variation.
  • Plain rice flour pancakes in a flat pan are not quite vitumbua. The wells matter.

Yaniqueques

Crisp fried johnnycakes, salty and blistered.

20 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 12 pieces

History

Yaniqueques are Dominican fried breads in the johnnycake family, the island word sitting close to the New England cake and to other Caribbean journey cakes. A simple flour or cornmeal dough is rolled and fried. Beach vendors sell them. This version uses flour, which is a common vendor dough, and it is fried rather than dry-griddled.

Cultural background

They are a snack with fish or with nothing. The blister is the sign of hot oil. They are not a sweet pancake.

Why it tastes this way

Hot oil blisters a thin dough so it tastes toasted and salty, a portable beach bread. The connection to johnnycake is the journey of a word and a simple dough around the Atlantic, not a claim that a Rhode Island recipe was copied line for line. Salt is the seasoning. The fry is the Dominican street method.

How it is made

A simple dough is rolled thin and fried until blistered.

Authentic touch. Roll them thin and fry hot. Thick yaniqueques stay doughy. Drain them or they go limp.

How it is used

Hot, salted, as a snack.

Field notes

  • Yaniqueque is the Dominican fried johnnycake.
  • Thin and hot is the texture.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • 3/4 cup water
  • Oil for frying

Steps

  1. Mix flour, baking powder, salt, oil, and water into a soft dough. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Roll thin and cut rounds. Slit each one.
  3. Fry in 1/2 inch of hot oil until blistered.
  4. Drain and salt.
  5. Serve hot.
  6. Do not fry them thick.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A cornmeal dough is closer to some johnnycakes and can be gluten-free if it has no wheat. This one is wheat.
  • Baking them makes a different, softer bread.
