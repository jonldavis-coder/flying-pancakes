Southern buttermilk pancakes

Tangy buttermilk, a little sugar, and a soft crumb with a browned rim.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 pancakes

History

Southern buttermilk pancakes are thick wheat cakes that became a regional breakfast after chemical leaveners and readily available dairy were both in the kitchen. The acid in buttermilk reacts with baking soda and keeps the crumb tender. Atlanta is only the map pin: the same stack is a home and diner breakfast across the South. Butter and a pour of cane syrup or sorghum usually finish the plate.

Cultural background

This is the later wheat stack, separate from cornmeal hoecakes and from rice cakes. Baking powder and soda are nineteenth-century tools, and they are what make this cake rise as high as it does.

Why it tastes this way

Buttermilk tastes slightly sour, and that sourness is the point, not a flaw. Melted butter in the batter and on the griddle browns at the edge. Stop stirring once the flour disappears: extra stirring tightens gluten and turns the crumb tough.

How it is made

Griddled in butter over medium heat until the center is set.

Authentic touch. Use real buttermilk, not regular milk with a splash of lemon. The griddle is ready when a drop of water skitters.

How it is used

Stacked hot, with butter and cane syrup or sorghum.

Field notes

  • Chemical leaveners, not yeast, are what lift this particular Southern stack.
  • A watery batter makes them spread thin, and a stiff batter makes them heavy.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups all-purpose flour
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 2 teaspoons baking powder
  • 1 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 cups buttermilk
  • 2 large eggs
  • 3 tablespoons unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the griddle

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, sugar, baking powder, baking soda, and salt in a bowl.
  2. In a second bowl, whisk the buttermilk, eggs, and melted butter.
  3. Pour the wet ingredients into the dry and stir just until no dry flour remains. A few lumps are fine.
  4. Heat a griddle over medium heat and film it with butter. A drop of water should skitter.
  5. Pour about 1/4 cup batter for each cake. Cook until the bubbles on top pop and stay open, then flip and cook until the center is set.
  6. Serve at once with butter and cane syrup or sorghum.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Leave the sugar out when the plate is savory.
  • Replace two tablespoons of the flour with cornmeal for a coarser crumb.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Butter
  • Cane syrup
  • Sorghum syrup
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Southern hoecakes

Plain toasted corn, salt, and a fried edge, denser than a wheat pancake.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 hoecakes

History

Southern hoecakes are round cornmeal cakes cooked on a griddle. The story that they were baked on the blade of a hoe is widely repeated and disputed, and this page does not treat that story as fact. Hoecakes were ordinary food in the South, including at eighteenth-century Virginia tables such as Mount Vernon. This version is meal, salt, and boiling water, with no wheat.

Cultural background

Corn was the everyday grain. A hoecake was a way to cook a quick bread on a flat iron when there was no oven loaf. The name survives even though the hoe-blade explanation is not settled.

Why it tastes this way

Scalding the meal softens the coarse parts and lets the cake hold together without egg or flour. The surface against the fat tastes toasted. The inside stays moist and distinctly of corn.

How it is made

Shaped cornmeal rounds griddled in a thin film of oil.

Authentic touch. Make them thicker than a Rhode Island johnnycake, about a finger thick, and cook them through. Ignore the hoe story and use a griddle.

How it is used

Hot, as a bread beside beans or with syrup if you want them sweet.

Field notes

  • The hoe-blade baking story is famous and disputed.
  • George Washington's household at Mount Vernon ate hoecakes, which is well documented.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups cornmeal
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 1/4 cups boiling water
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil, plus more for the griddle

Steps

  1. Stir the cornmeal, salt, and oil in a bowl.
  2. Pour in the boiling water and stir to a dough stiff enough to hold a round. Rest 10 minutes.
  3. With wet hands, shape 8 rounds about 1/2 inch thick.
  4. Heat a film of oil on a griddle over medium heat.
  5. Cook the cakes until the underside is browned and the side looks set, about 4 minutes, then flip and cook until the center is no longer wet.
  6. Serve hot. They are bread, not a tall diner stack.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Grease the griddle with bacon drippings if you eat pork.
  • A spoon of wheat flour in the batter is a later, softer version and is no longer gluten-free.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Accompaniment to a meal
  • Potlikker or beans
  • Sorghum syrup
  • Butter, if you eat dairy
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Lacy cornbread

Crisp, salty corn with a brittle lace of holes and a tender spot in the middle.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 skillet cakes

History

Lacy cornbread is a very thin cornmeal batter cooked in a hot skillet until the surface opens into crisp holes. It belongs to Appalachian corn cooking, especially in West Virginia and southwestern Virginia. It is a griddle bread, closer to a crisp cake than to a deep pan of cornbread. The batter is meal, water, and salt.

Cultural background

Mountain cooks poured this batter into a hot greased skillet when they wanted cornbread in a hurry. The lace is what the heat does to a thin batter, not a separate ingredient.

Why it tastes this way

Extra water lets the batter spider across the pan instead of sitting in a mound. Hot fat fries the strands as they spread, so the cake tastes toasted and crisp. Coarse meal keeps a corn flavor that wheat cornbread would soften.

How it is made

Poured thin into a very hot oiled skillet so the batter laces.

Authentic touch. The batter should look thin, almost like cream. If it sits in a thick puddle, add water. The lace is the test that you got it right.

How it is used

Whole from the skillet, broken at the table, often with beans or greens.

Field notes

  • The holes are the batter frying as it spreads, which is why cooks call it lacy.
  • It is closer to a crisp griddle cake than to a square of baked cornbread.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup cornmeal
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 3/4 cups water
  • 3 tablespoons neutral oil, divided

Steps

  1. Whisk the cornmeal, salt, water, and 1 tablespoon of the oil into a thin batter. It should run off the spoon.
  2. Rest the batter 5 minutes and whisk again.
  3. Heat a small skillet over medium-high heat. Add 1 1/2 teaspoons of oil and let it get hot.
  4. Pour in about 1/3 cup batter and tilt the pan. The batter should spread and break into holes.
  5. Cook until the top looks dry and the underside is brown and crisp, then flip carefully and brown the other side.
  6. Repeat with the remaining oil and batter. Serve hot.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Bacon fat in place of oil is common in kitchens that cook pork.
  • A spoon of wheat flour stops the lace and makes a different bread.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Accompaniment to a meal
  • Pot beans
  • Cooked greens
  • Butter, if you eat dairy
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Sweet potato pancakes

Orange-fleshed sweet potato, a little cinnamon, and a moist wheat crumb.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 pancakes

History

Sweet potato pancakes mash an orange-fleshed sweet potato into a wheat batter. Sweet potatoes have been a Southern field crop for a long time, in Native gardens and later in the kitchens of enslaved and free Black cooks and of white farm families. The griddle cake is a breakfast use of the mashed root. Wilson, North Carolina, is a modern market town for the crop and the map pin, not a claim that the pancake was invented there.

Cultural background

The root and the pancake are different ages. This page is the wheat breakfast cake. It does not assign an older sweet-potato bread to one community.

Why it tastes this way

Mashed sweet potato adds moisture and a flavor closer to roasted squash than to white potato. Cinnamon sits on top of that sweetness. Because the mash is wet, the flour has to be enough to keep the cakes from slumping.

How it is made

Griddled wheat batter enriched with mashed sweet potato.

Authentic touch. Use a cooked, mashed sweet potato with a drier texture, not a can of syrup-packed pieces. Orange-fleshed varieties are what most Southern cooks mean.

How it is used

Hot, with butter. They are sweet enough that syrup can be light.

Field notes

  • Wilson, North Carolina, is a long-running market center for Southern sweet potatoes.
  • The mashed root makes the batter wetter than a plain buttermilk pancake, so the flour amount matters.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup mashed cooked sweet potato, cooled
  • 1 1/2 cups all-purpose flour
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 2 teaspoons baking powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground cinnamon
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup buttermilk
  • 2 large eggs
  • 2 tablespoons unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the griddle

Steps

  1. Mash the sweet potato until smooth.
  2. Whisk the flour, sugar, baking powder, baking soda, cinnamon, and salt.
  3. Whisk the sweet potato with the buttermilk, eggs, and melted butter. Stir that into the flour just until combined.
  4. Butter a griddle over medium-low heat. The sugar and sweet potato brown quickly.
  5. Pour 1/4 cup cakes. Flip when the underside is deep gold and the top has set.
  6. Cook the second side until the center is no longer wet. Serve with butter.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Roast the sweet potato instead of boiling it for a deeper flavor.
  • Skip the cinnamon for a plainer cake.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Butter
  • A thin drizzle of sorghum
  • Pecans only if you want a tree-nut topping
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Lowcountry rice griddle cakes

Soft, lightly sweet rice cakes with a crisp buttered edge and separate grains in the crumb.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 10 griddle cakes

History

Lowcountry rice griddle cakes use the long-grain rice of the South Carolina and Georgia coast. Enslaved West Africans brought rice-growing knowledge, and planters built a brutal rice economy on their labor and on the labor of their descendants. The cake is a later griddle use of that grain. It is not a rename of one village dish.

Cultural background

Charleston and the surrounding Lowcountry were the center of that rice system. Carolina-grown long-grain rice is the grain to use when you can get it. Any cooked long-grain white rice will hold the cake.

Why it tastes this way

Cooked rice stays in distinct grains, so the cake tastes steamy and mild instead of like a smooth wheat crumb. Rice flour binds the cakes without gluten. Butter browns the outside and keeps the rice from tasting flat.

How it is made

Cooked rice and rice flour griddled in butter.

Authentic touch. Use leftover cooked rice, not raw rice thrown into a wheat pancake. Name the labor when you tell the story of the grain.

How it is used

Warm, with butter. A little sorghum or cane syrup fits the coast.

Field notes

  • Rice in the Lowcountry is tied to West African knowledge and to enslaved labor, not to a single exported village recipe.
  • Leftover cooked rice is the usual base, which is why these cakes are a breakfast after a rice supper.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups cooked long-grain white rice, cooled
  • 2 large eggs
  • 1/2 cup milk
  • 1/3 cup rice flour
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the griddle

Steps

  1. Mash the rice with a fork so some grains break and some stay whole.
  2. Stir in the eggs, milk, rice flour, baking powder, sugar, salt, and melted butter.
  3. Heat a buttered griddle over medium heat.
  4. Drop 1/4 cup mounds and flatten them slightly. They will not look like smooth wheat batter.
  5. Cook until the underside is golden and the cake holds together, then flip and finish.
  6. Serve warm with butter.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A savory version leaves out the sugar and adds a sliced scallion.
  • Wheat flour in place of rice flour means the cakes are no longer gluten-free.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Butter
  • Cane syrup
  • Shrimp gravy, for a savory plate, if you eat shellfish
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Georgia peach pancakes

Juicy ripe peach against a tangy buttermilk crumb, sweet but not candied.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 pancakes

History

Georgia peach pancakes are a seasonal wheat cake made when ripe peaches are in the market. The state's commercial peach industry grew in the late nineteenth century. The pancake is a summer breakfast. Fort Valley sits in the peach-growing belt and is the map pin, not the birthplace of a plantation recipe.

Cultural background

Fresh peaches are wet. Dicing them small and folding them in at the end keeps the batter from turning pink and soggy. Frozen peaches, drained, are the off-season substitute.

Why it tastes this way

A ripe peach tastes floral and tart, and heat concentrates that juice at the edge of the piece. Buttermilk keeps the crumb from tasting only of sugar. Too much syrup hides the fruit, which is why the batter sugar stays low.

How it is made

Griddled buttermilk batter with fresh peach pieces folded in.

Authentic touch. Use ripe peaches in season and cut them yourself. A peach-flavored syrup on a plain pancake is a different dish.

How it is used

Straight off the griddle, with butter and the smallest pour of syrup.

Field notes

  • Georgia's commercial peach crop expanded in the late nineteenth century.
  • Fort Valley is a real center of that growing belt, which is why the map pin sits there.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup diced peeled ripe peaches
  • 1 1/2 cups all-purpose flour
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 2 teaspoons baking powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 1/2 cups buttermilk
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the griddle

Steps

  1. Pat the diced peaches dry with a towel so they do not water down the batter.
  2. Whisk the flour, sugar, baking powder, baking soda, and salt.
  3. Stir in the buttermilk, egg, and melted butter until just mixed. Fold in the peaches.
  4. Butter a griddle over medium heat. Pour 1/4 cup cakes.
  5. Flip when the bubbles set. Peek underneath so the peach juice does not scorch.
  6. Serve as soon as the center is set, with butter.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Drain thawed frozen peaches and pat them dry when fresh fruit is gone.
  • A pinch of nutmeg is enough spice. They do not need a heavy spice mix.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Butter
  • A few extra peach slices
  • Hot tea
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Texas pecan pancakes

Buttered wheat cakes with toasted pecan, sweet and slightly bitter at the brown edge of the nut.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 pancakes

History

Texas pecan pancakes put a native nut into a wheat breakfast cake. Pecan trees grow wild and in orchards across Texas, and Indigenous peoples of the region used the nut long before a diner pancake did. The cake itself is a twentieth-century breakfast. San Saba is a pecan-growing town and the map pin.

Cultural background

Toasting the nuts first is what makes them taste like pecans rather than pale crumbs. The wheat batter is the usual buttermilk stack with nuts folded through.

Why it tastes this way

Pecans are oily. Heat releases that oil and turns the flavor round and toasted. If the nuts go in raw, the cakes taste flatter. A bitter note from the brown skin is part of a real pecan, so do not expect a bland crunch.

How it is made

Griddled buttermilk batter with toasted pecans in and on the cakes.

Authentic touch. Toast the pecans in a dry skillet until they smell nutty, then chop them. Scatter a few more on the batter after you pour it.

How it is used

Hot, with butter and syrup, and extra pecans on the plate.

Field notes

  • The pecan is native to Texas and the wider South.
  • San Saba built a town identity on pecan growing, which is why this pin sits there.

Ingredients

  • 3/4 cup chopped pecans
  • 1 1/2 cups all-purpose flour
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 2 teaspoons baking powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 1/2 cups buttermilk
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the griddle

Steps

  1. Toast the pecans in a dry skillet over medium heat, stirring, until they smell fragrant. Cool them, then chop if they are still in large pieces.
  2. Whisk the flour, sugar, baking powder, baking soda, and salt. Stir in half of the pecans.
  3. Add the buttermilk, egg, and melted butter. Stir until just combined.
  4. Butter a griddle over medium heat and pour 1/4 cup cakes. Scatter a few of the remaining pecans on each cake.
  5. Flip when the tops set. Cook until the nuts on the bottom are brown, not black.
  6. Serve with butter, syrup, and the rest of the pecans.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Leave the nuts on top only if you need to cook a nut-free cake for someone else, and use a clean pan.
  • A darker sorghum syrup stands up to the nut better than a very light syrup.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Butter
  • Maple syrup or sorghum
  • More toasted pecans
  • Vegetarian

Southern banana pancakes

Ripe banana mashed into a tangy buttermilk pancake, soft and sweet.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 pancakes

History

Southern banana pancakes are buttermilk cakes with ripe banana in the batter. Bananas arrived in Gulf ports as an imported fruit and became an ordinary grocery item, and the cake is a home breakfast built on that fruit. It is separate from the New Orleans banana-sauce dessert and from Hawaiian diner hotcakes. Mobile is the map pin because it is a Gulf port.

Cultural background

A very ripe banana, with a spotted peel, mashes smooth and tastes sweet enough that the batter needs little sugar. Green bananas stay starchy and dull.

Why it tastes this way

Mashed banana adds sugar and a floral flavor and makes the crumb moist. Buttermilk keeps it from cloying. Slices on top brown in the butter. If the banana is underripe, the cakes taste flat and dry.

How it is made

Griddled buttermilk batter with mashed banana and banana slices.

Authentic touch. Mash a ripe banana into the batter and put two or three slices on each cake as it cooks. This is not the rum sauce of Bananas Foster.

How it is used

Hot, with butter. Syrup is optional.

Field notes

  • Bananas became a Southern grocery item through the fruit trade at Gulf ports.
  • This cake is separate from Bananas Foster and from Hawaiian macadamia hotcakes.

Ingredients

  • 1 very ripe banana, mashed, plus 1 ripe banana, sliced
  • 1 1/2 cups all-purpose flour
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 2 teaspoons baking powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 1/4 cups buttermilk
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the griddle

Steps

  1. Mash one banana until mostly smooth.
  2. Whisk the flour, sugar, baking powder, baking soda, and salt.
  3. Stir in the mashed banana, buttermilk, egg, and melted butter until just combined.
  4. Butter a griddle over medium-low heat. Pour 1/4 cup cakes and press two or three banana slices onto each.
  5. Flip when the underside is golden. The slices brown quickly, so watch the heat.
  6. Serve when the center is set.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Leave the slices off and keep the banana only in the batter.
  • A pinch of cinnamon is optional and not required.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Butter
  • A few extra banana slices
  • Coffee
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

New Orleans calas

Fried rice fritters, lightly sweet, with nutmeg and a powdered-sugar coat.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · About 18 fritters

History

New Orleans calas are fried rice fritters sold for generations by Black vendors, many of them women, who carried the cakes through the streets. Rice growing in Louisiana depended on the skill of enslaved West Africans and on the plantation economy built on their labor. This page does not claim that one West African village recipe became calas. The fritter that can be documented is the Creole rice cake sold in the city.

Cultural background

Older calas were yeasted. This recipe uses a short rise and then fries the batter. Cooked rice is the body. Wheat flour and egg bind it. The written recipe has no milk.

Why it tastes this way

Rice stays in soft grains inside a crisp fried shell. Nutmeg is the spice most associated with the fritter, used lightly. Sugar in the batter and powdered sugar afterward make them a sweet street breakfast. Yeast gives a mild ferment flavor if the batter actually rises. If it does not rest, the fritters taste heavy.

How it is made

Yeasted cooked-rice batter fried in oil and dusted with powdered sugar.

Authentic touch. Fry them. A baked rice muffin is not a calas. Dust them with powdered sugar while they are hot, which is how street calas were eaten.

How it is used

Hot, as a breakfast or a snack, with powdered sugar.

Field notes

  • Calas were sold on New Orleans streets by Black vendors, often women.
  • The rice in Louisiana cooking is tied to enslaved West African skill, and this page does not rename one village recipe as calas.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups cooked medium-grain white rice, cooled
  • 1 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/4 teaspoon grated nutmeg
  • 2 large eggs
  • 1/2 cup all-purpose flour
  • 6 cups neutral oil, for frying
  • 1/2 cup powdered sugar, for serving

Steps

  1. Mash the rice with a fork or your hand so some grains break and some remain.
  2. Stir in the yeast, sugar, salt, nutmeg, eggs, and flour. The batter should be thick and spoonable.
  3. Cover and let it rise in a warm place until puffy, about 1 hour. That rise is not added to the active prep time.
  4. Heat 1 1/2 inches of oil in a deep pan to 350°F.
  5. Slip heaping tablespoons of batter into the oil. Fry until deep golden, turning once, about 2 to 3 minutes.
  6. Drain on a rack, dust with powdered sugar, and serve hot.

Fry in enough oil to cover the fritters halfway or more, and do not leave the pot. Keep the heat at a steady 350°F.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Baking powder in place of yeast is a quicker home batter and will taste less fermented.
  • A splash of milk is a later enrichment and would add dairy.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Street food
  • Snack
  • Powdered sugar
  • Coffee
  • A little more nutmeg
  • Vegetarian
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Bananas Foster pancakes

A plain buttermilk pancake under bananas in brown sugar, butter, cinnamon, and rum.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 pancakes with sauce

History

Bananas Foster is a 1951 dessert created at Brennan's in New Orleans, bananas cooked with butter, brown sugar, cinnamon, and rum, and named Bananas Foster. Pancakes with that sauce are a later adaptation, not the original restaurant dessert. The cakes here are a wheat stack with the banana sauce spooned over them. The restaurant dessert is flambéed, and this home sauce cooks the rum in the pan.

Cultural background

New Orleans already had bananas through the fruit trade. The 1951 dessert is a restaurant showpiece. Putting the sauce on pancakes came afterward, on brunch menus and in home kitchens.

Why it tastes this way

Brown sugar and butter make a caramel that sticks to the banana. Rum perfumes the sauce once the raw alcohol cooks off. Cinnamon is the spice. The pancake underneath is plain so the sauce stays the loud flavor. A very thick stack will not warm through under a spoon of sauce.

How it is made

Griddled buttermilk pancakes with a stovetop banana, brown sugar, and rum sauce.

Authentic touch. Make the sauce and the pancakes. A bottled banana syrup on a stack is not Bananas Foster. Simmer the rum. You do not need to light the pan.

How it is used

Pancakes on plates, sauce and bananas spooned over while hot.

Field notes

  • Bananas Foster was created in 1951 at Brennan's in New Orleans.
  • The pancake version is a later adaptation of that dessert.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup buttermilk
  • 1 large egg
  • 1 tablespoon unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the griddle
  • 4 tablespoons unsalted butter, for the sauce
  • 1/2 cup packed brown sugar
  • 2 ripe bananas, sliced
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground cinnamon
  • 2 tablespoons dark rum

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, sugar, baking powder, and salt. Stir in the buttermilk, egg, and melted tablespoon of butter.
  2. Cook 8 small pancakes on a buttered griddle over medium heat, flipping once, until the centers are set. Keep them warm.
  3. For the sauce, melt 4 tablespoons of butter in a skillet. Stir in the brown sugar and cinnamon until the sugar dissolves.
  4. Add the bananas and cook about 1 minute, until they soften at the edges.
  5. Add the rum and simmer 1 minute so the sauce thickens slightly. You do not need to set it on fire.
  6. Spoon the bananas and sauce over the pancakes and serve at once.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Skip the rum for an alcohol-free butter and brown-sugar banana sauce.
  • The original dessert was served over ice cream, which is the 1951 plate, not this pancake.

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Breakfast
  • The banana sauce
  • A little extra rum left out of the pan if you want it at the table
  • Coffee
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free
