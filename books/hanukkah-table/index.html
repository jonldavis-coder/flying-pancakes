Potato latkes

Ashkenazi potato pancakes for Hanukkah, fried in oil, with the older cheese custom named beside them.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 latkes

History

Potato latkes are the Ashkenazi Hanukkah pancake of grated potato, onion, egg, and flour or matzo meal, fried in oil. Potatoes became common in Eastern Europe in the 19th century. Cheese pancakes are the older Hanukkah dairy custom, tied in Ashkenazi teaching to Judith, who in the Book of Judith fed an enemy general salty cheese and wine. That cheese cake has its own page. This one is the potato cake, and it is not gluten-free, because flour or matzo meal is wheat. The pin is Poland. The same cakes are cooked in Ukraine, the United States, and Israel.

Cultural background

Hanukkah food is fried, a reminder of oil in the Temple story. A dairy-free latke fried in oil can sit on a meat meal. Sour cream is a dairy topping and belongs on a dairy meal, not beside meat. Applesauce keeps the plate neutral.

Why it tastes this way

Oil frying makes the crust crisp and savory. Onion sweetens it. The inside stays soft if the shreds were not ground to a paste. Salt and pepper are the seasoning. Applesauce is tart and cool. Because there is no baking powder, the flavor is potato, onion, and browned oil, not a diner pancake.

How it is made

Potato and onion are grated, squeezed, mixed with egg, flour, and salt, and fried in oil until crisp.

Authentic touch. Use flour or matzo meal and say so: this is not gluten-free. Fry in oil, not butter, if the latkes need to stay dairy-free for a meat meal. Keep the starch that settles from the grated potato.

How it is used

Hot at Hanukkah, with applesauce, or with sour cream when the meal is dairy. They are a feast food.

Field notes

  • Cheese latkes are the older Ashkenazi Hanukkah dairy custom. Potato latkes spread after potatoes did.
  • Potatoes became common in Eastern Europe in the 19th century.
  • Flour or matzo meal means these latkes are not gluten-free.

Ingredients

  • 700 g starchy potatoes, peeled
  • 1 onion
  • 1 large egg
  • 3 tbsp plain wheat flour or matzo meal
  • 1 tsp fine salt
  • 1/4 tsp black pepper
  • Neutral oil, for frying
  • Applesauce, for serving

Steps

  1. Grate the potatoes and onion. Collect the liquid, let the starch settle, pour off the water, and return the starch to the potato.
  2. Stir in the egg, flour or matzo meal, salt, and pepper.
  3. Fry flattened spoonfuls in hot oil until both sides are deep golden and the centers are cooked.
  4. Drain on a rack and serve with applesauce, or with sour cream if the meal is dairy.

Fry in enough oil to brown the cakes and do not leave the pan. Drain them so they stay crisp.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • The older farmer-cheese latke, on its own page
  • Matzo meal instead of flour, still wheat

Pairings and uses

  • Feast or fast day
  • Savory meal
  • Applesauce
  • Sour cream, on a dairy meal
  • Salt
  • Vegetarian
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Farmer cheese latkes

The older Ashkenazi Hanukkah pancake, made of farmer cheese rather than potato.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 cakes

History

These are cheese latkes, the older Ashkenazi Hanukkah dairy pancake. The customary teaching connects Hanukkah dairy foods with Judith, who served cheese to an enemy general. Potato latkes became common later, after potatoes spread in Eastern Europe in the 19th century. This page is written from a Lithuanian Jewish table and does not claim that only Lithuania fried cheese. The potato page is pinned to Poland. Cheese, egg, and flour make a cake that is vegetarian and not vegan, not dairy-free, and not gluten-free.

Cultural background

They are Hanukkah food and a breakfast cake when cheese is in the house. Sweet and lightly salty versions both exist. This one is slightly sweet, with sour cream or a little jam.

Why it tastes this way

Farmer cheese is tangy and rich. Sugar is restrained so it still tastes like cheese. Egg sets the center. Flour makes a thin crust in the butter. They brown slowly. A high flame dries them out and loses the creamy middle that distinguishes them from a potato latke.

How it is made

Farmer cheese is mixed with egg, sugar, salt, and flour, shaped into small cakes, and fried in butter.

Authentic touch. Use a dry farmer cheese or well-drained tvorog. This is a dairy Hanukkah dish, so do not serve it as the potato latke and do not call it gluten-free.

How it is used

Warm at a dairy Hanukkah meal, with sour cream or jam. They are vegetarian.

Field notes

  • Cheese pancakes are the older Ashkenazi Hanukkah custom.
  • The Judith story is the customary explanation for dairy at that holiday.
  • Potato latkes came after potatoes were common, and they have their own page.

Ingredients

  • 400 g farmer cheese or drained tvorog
  • 2 large eggs
  • 3 tbsp plain wheat flour
  • 1 tbsp sugar
  • 1/4 tsp fine salt
  • 30 g unsalted butter, for frying
  • Sour cream, for serving

Steps

  1. Mash the cheese with the eggs, flour, sugar, and salt until it holds a shape.
  2. Form eight small cakes.
  3. Fry in butter over medium heat until both sides are golden and the center is hot, not dry.
  4. Serve with sour cream or jam.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A savory cheese latke with salt and dill and no sugar
  • Potato latkes, the later Hanukkah cake

Pairings and uses

  • Feast or fast day
  • Breakfast
  • Sour cream
  • Jam
  • A dusting of sugar
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Cheese blintzes

A thin, lightly sweet pancake wrapped around tangy soft cheese and fried in butter.

35 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 blintzes

History

Cheese blintzes are thin pancakes wrapped around a sweet cheese filling, an Ashkenazi dish from Eastern Europe that became Lower East Side and wider New York home cooking. Dairy blintzes are a Shavuot food and also an ordinary breakfast. The wrapper is a thin egg batter, and the cake is fried again after it is filled.

Cultural background

Farmer cheese or dry cottage cheese is the usual filling. Wet ricotta needs to be drained or the blintz leaks. The Eastern European origin and the New York life of the dish are both part of the story.

Why it tastes this way

The wrapper tastes of egg and butter, and the second frying makes it crisp at the seam. Farmer cheese is tangy and slightly dry, so the filling stays in a soft curd instead of running. A little sugar and lemon zest keep it from tasting like a plain cheese omelet.

How it is made

Thin pancakes filled with sweet cheese, folded, and fried in butter.

Authentic touch. Cook the wrappers on one side, fill the pale side, and fry the filled packets in butter until both faces are golden. Boiling them is not the dish.

How it is used

Hot, with sour cream or a little jam. Applesauce is a common side.

Field notes

  • Blintzes traveled from Eastern European Ashkenazi cooking to New York, including the Lower East Side.
  • Cheese blintzes are a dairy dish associated with Shavuot and also eaten as breakfast.

Ingredients

  • 3/4 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 cup whole milk
  • 2 large eggs
  • 1 tablespoon unsalted butter, melted, plus 2 tablespoons for frying
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup farmer cheese
  • 1 large egg yolk
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1 teaspoon finely grated lemon zest

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, milk, whole eggs, melted tablespoon of butter, and salt into a thin batter. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Stir the farmer cheese, egg yolk, sugar, and lemon zest.
  3. Butter an 8-inch skillet lightly and heat it over medium. Pour about 1/4 cup batter and swirl it into a thin round.
  4. Cook until the top is set and the underside is spotted. Slide it out cooked side up. Repeat, stacking the wrappers.
  5. Spoon cheese onto the pale side of each wrapper. Fold the sides in and roll the wrapper closed.
  6. Fry the rolls in the remaining butter over medium heat until golden on both sides. Serve hot.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A potato filling makes a savory blintz and is a different recipe.
  • Dry cottage cheese can stand in for farmer cheese if it is drained well.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Dessert
  • Feast or fast day
  • Sour cream
  • Fruit jam
  • Applesauce
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free
