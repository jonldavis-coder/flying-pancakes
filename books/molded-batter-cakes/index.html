Aebleskiver

Round Danish pancakes from a molded pan, cardamom, jam, and powdered sugar.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · about 28 aebleskiver

History

Aebleskiver are round pancakes cooked in a special pan with hemispherical cups. The name means apple slices. Older versions tuck in a bit of apple. Many Danish kitchens now cook them plain, with cardamom, and serve jam and powdered sugar. They are especially common in December, and they are also eaten at other times. Church breakfasts in the American Upper Midwest are a later home of the pan, not the origin of the cake. This recipe is the Danish one.

Cultural background

The pan is the recipe's tool, as with Dutch poffertjes, and the batters are not the same. Aebleskiver are turned with a knitting needle or a skewer so a ball forms. They are Advent and Christmas food in many families now.

Why it tastes this way

Whipped egg whites and baking powder make them light. Cardamom perfumes the crumb. The crust browns in butter while the inside stays like a small popover. Powdered sugar and a tart jam are the contrast. Apple, if you add it, steams inside and tastes like the name.

How it is made

A batter of flour, buttermilk, cardamom, and separated eggs is cooked in a buttered aebleskiver pan, turned once into rounds, and served with jam and powdered sugar.

Authentic touch. Turn each cake before the center is dry, so the unset batter runs into the cup and makes a ball. A flat pan cannot do this. A piece of apple in the cup is the older filling. Leaving it out is the common modern cake.

How it is used

Hot, with jam and powdered sugar, at breakfast, as a snack, or through December. The American church breakfast is a later table, not the source.

Field notes

  • The name means apple slices, and many current recipes leave the apple out.
  • Upper Midwest church breakfasts are a later home, not the origin.
  • The ball shape comes from turning the cake in a molded pan.

Ingredients

  • 180 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tsp baking powder
  • 1/2 tsp ground cardamom
  • 2 tbsp sugar
  • 1/4 tsp fine salt
  • 2 large eggs, separated
  • 250 ml buttermilk
  • 40 g unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the pan
  • Jam and powdered sugar, for serving

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, baking powder, cardamom, sugar, and salt. Stir in the yolks, buttermilk, and melted butter.
  2. Beat the whites to soft peaks and fold them in.
  3. Heat an aebleskiver pan, butter the cups, and fill them almost full. Cook until the bottoms are brown.
  4. Turn each cake with a skewer and cook until the ball is set and browned. Serve with jam and powdered sugar.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A cube of apple in each cup
  • A yeasted batter, which is an older style than baking powder

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Dessert
  • Feast or fast day
  • Snack
  • Jam
  • Powdered sugar
  • A small piece of apple hidden in the batter
  • Vegetarian

Banh khot

Tiny crisp-edged rice cakes from a dimpled pan, each topped with a shrimp and a soft coconut center.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 24 mini cakes

History

Banh khot are mini savory pancakes of southern Vietnam, cooked in a dimpled pan. Vung Tau is especially known for them. The batter is rice flour, turmeric, and coconut milk, close to a small banh xeo but shaped by the mold and usually topped with one shrimp rather than folded around pork. They are street food, served with herbs and a fish-sauce dip. This recipe has shrimp and fish sauce, so it is not vegetarian. It is a mold cake, not the large crepe.

Cultural background

The pans look like a cluster of small cups. A crisp rim and a slightly custardy center are the textures to chase. Quail eggs or pork appear in some stalls. Shrimp is the topping this page writes. The cakes are eaten as soon as they leave the mold.

Why it tastes this way

Coconut milk and turmeric give the same yellow, lightly bitter sweetness as a larger rice crepe, concentrated in a bite. The shrimp on top tastes briny and sweet. Fish sauce in the dip adds salt and acid. Because the cake is small and molded, the rim fries harder than the center. Herbs make each bite fresher than the batter alone.

How it is made

Pour turmeric-coconut rice batter into a hot dimpled pan, set a shrimp in each cup, and cook until the rims are crisp.

Authentic touch. Heat the molds until oil shimmers before you pour. A cool cup sticks and never crisps. One shrimp, pressed in while the batter is still wet, stays attached.

How it is used

Lift them out and eat them at once with herbs and nuoc cham. They are a snack or a street meal.

Field notes

  • Banh khot are the small molded cakes of southern Vietnam, especially Vung Tau.
  • They share a turmeric rice batter with banh xeo and use a dimpled pan instead of a wide crepe.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup rice flour, with no wheat added
  • 3/4 cup coconut milk
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 1/2 teaspoon turmeric
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 24 small shrimp, peeled
  • 1 tablespoon fish sauce
  • Neutral oil
  • Scallion greens

Steps

  1. Whisk rice flour, coconut milk, water, turmeric, and salt into a batter thinner than pancake batter. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Toss the shrimp with a teaspoon of fish sauce. Keep the rest for a dip with lime, sugar, and water.
  3. Oil a hot dimpled pan. Fill each cup about halfway.
  4. Press one shrimp and a few scallion rings into each cup. Cook until the rims are golden and the centers are set.
  5. Loosen the cakes and serve them with herbs and the dip.

Cook the shrimp until they are opaque and pink.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A quail egg in a few cups
  • Finely sliced scallion in the batter

Pairings and uses

  • Street food
  • Savory meal
  • Snack
  • Nuoc cham
  • Lettuce and mint
  • Pickled carrot
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Bangladesh · South Asia

Chitoi pitha

Small, soft rice cakes with a dimpled top, mild and a little sweet if date molasses is poured on.

15 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 small cakes

History

Chitoi pitha are Bangladeshi rice cakes cooked in a dimpled chitoi pan during winter pitha season. Rice flour and water, sometimes a little salt, set over the pits of a special pan into small cushioned cakes. They belong to the same winter weeks as other pithas, when date-palm molasses is in season. They are a mold cake, not a rolled patishapta and not a fried malpua.

Cultural background

Dhaka homes and village kitchens both bring the pan out in the cool months. The cakes are often eaten with khejur gur, date molasses, or with a savory bhorta. This recipe keeps the cake plain so either path stays open. The written ingredients have no wheat.

Why it tastes this way

Rice flour and water taste clean and slightly sweet once they steam in the molds. Browning is light, so the flavor stays soft. Date molasses, if you use it, adds mineral caramel. A savory side such as mashed dried fish would add fish and a salty contrast. The dimples of the pan brown a pattern into a cake that would otherwise be plain.

How it is made

Mix a smooth rice-flour batter and cook it in a covered dimpled chitoi pan until the cakes set.

Authentic touch. Wipe a tiny film of oil on hot molds, then pour. Too much oil fries the cakes hard. Too little, and they weld to the pan. Covering the pan helps the tops set.

How it is used

Serve warm with date molasses, or with grated coconut and a pinch of salt. Winter is the season.

Field notes

  • Chitoi pitha are winter cakes, cooked when Bangladesh is in pitha season.
  • The dimpled pan gives the cakes their shape and their spotted crust.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup rice flour, with no wheat added
  • 1 1/4 cups water
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • Neutral oil for the molds

Steps

  1. Whisk rice flour, salt, and water into a smooth batter about as thick as crepe batter. Rest 10 minutes.
  2. Heat a chitoi pan over medium heat. Brush the molds lightly with oil.
  3. Fill each mold about two-thirds full. Cover the pan.
  4. Cook until the tops look set and the undersides are pale gold, then turn the cakes if the pan allows, or cover longer until the centers are cooked.
  5. Unmold and repeat. Serve warm.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of soaked rice ground into the batter for a softer crumb
  • A savory top of onion and green chile

Pairings and uses

  • Snack
  • Feast or fast day
  • Breakfast
  • Date-palm molasses
  • Grated coconut with salt
  • A vegetable bhorta
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free

Sri Lanka · South Asia

Egg hoppers

A coconut rice hopper with a soft egg set in the center, crisp at the rim.

30 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 egg hoppers

History

An egg hopper is a Sri Lankan hopper with an egg broken into the thick center so the white sets against the rice and the yolk stays soft. The batter is the same fermented rice and coconut milk used for plain hoppers. The egg is the addition that gives this page its own name. It is a breakfast from hopper stands in Colombo and elsewhere on the island. It is not a string hopper, and it is not a wheat omelet.

Cultural background

The cook pours and swirls the batter, then cracks the egg at once so it cooks under the lid with the rice. A firm yolk is a missed hopper. Chile sambol is served beside it, not baked inside, in the usual plate.

Why it tastes this way

The rice bowl is lightly sour and sweet from coconut. The egg adds richness the plain hopper does not have, and a soft yolk sauces the spongy center. The crisp rim stays free of egg if the crack is careful, so you still get the cracker edge. Salt and the heat of a sambol finish the plate.

How it is made

Swirl fermented rice batter in a hopper pan, crack an egg into the center, and cover until the white sets.

Authentic touch. Crack the egg into the center puddle, not onto the thin wall. An egg on the lace burns and welds the hopper to the pan.

How it is used

Eat at once with a spicy onion sambol. The yolk should still move. Dried-fish sambols add a fish allergen on the side.

Field notes

  • The egg goes into the center of a rice hopper. It does not replace the rice batter.
  • String hoppers are pressed rice noodles, a different dish from this pan.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups fermented rice and coconut-milk hopper batter
  • 4 eggs
  • Salt and pepper
  • Neutral oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Use hopper batter that has fermented until bubbly, as on the plain hopper page.
  2. Heat and grease a hopper pan. Pour a ladle and swirl so the center remains deep.
  3. Immediately crack one egg into that center. Sprinkle a little salt and pepper.
  4. Cover and cook on medium-low until the rim is crisp, the egg white is set, and the yolk is still soft.
  5. Loosen the rim and slide the hopper out. Repeat with the remaining eggs.

Cook the egg white until it is set. People who need fully cooked yolks should leave the lid on longer.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A pinch of salt and pepper on the yolk
  • A firmer yolk if you cover the pan longer

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • Onion sambol
  • Seeni sambol
  • Banana and a second plain hopper
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Japan · No chili heat · 8 filled cakes

Flying Pancakes of the World

Japan · East Asia

Imagawayaki

A round filled cake cooked in a hinged iron, with sweet red bean paste sealed inside a soft wheat shell.

15 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 8 · 8 filled cakes

History

Imagawayaki are round filled cakes cooked in a molded iron. Batter is poured, sweet red bean paste is set in the center, and a second skin of batter closes the cake before the iron is turned. The names vary by city. Imagawayaki is one name, and obanyaki is another, heard often in Kansai. This page does not pick a founding shop or a year. The method is the mold, which makes it a different sweet from dorayaki's two separate pancakes.

Cultural background

Street stalls still use heavy hinged pans over a gas flame. The cake should be brown, not pale, and the paste should reach neither the hinge nor the open air. A leak of anko welds the cake to the iron. Home cooks can use a small filled-cake pan or an electric mold with the same batter.

Why it tastes this way

The iron browns the batter on both sides at once, so the crust tastes more toasted than a dorayaki cooked in an open pan. Egg and milk keep the interior tender. Anko is sweet and beany, a filling rather than a sandwich layer you can see. Because the paste steams inside the mold, it stays moist against the crumb.

How it is made

Cook a wheat batter in a round mold, bury a spoon of anko, cover it with more batter, and turn the iron until both faces brown.

Authentic touch. Add the bean paste only after the first batter has skinned over. If you drop the paste into raw batter, it sinks and breaks the seal.

How it is used

Cool them until the center is no longer scorching. Eat them as a street snack with tea. They are a dessert, not a meal.

Field notes

  • Imagawayaki and obanyaki are two city names for this molded filled cake.
  • The iron cooks both sides. Dorayaki is two pancakes cooked in the open and then joined.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose wheat flour
  • 1 egg
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/2 cup milk
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1 cup sweet red bean paste
  • Neutral oil for the molds

Steps

  1. Whisk flour, egg, sugar, milk, and baking powder into a thick batter. Rest it 10 minutes.
  2. Oil a hot imagawayaki iron or a round filled-cake pan.
  3. Pour a little batter into each mold, add a spoon of anko, and cover with more batter so the paste is sealed.
  4. Cook, turning the iron or flipping the cakes, until both sides are deep brown and the batter is set.
  5. Cool briefly and serve.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Custard in place of anko
  • Obanyaki, the same mold cake under another city's name

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Street food
  • Snack
  • Green tea
  • Roasted barley tea
  • Nothing sweet beside them
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Kenya · East Africa

Kenyan vitumbua

Yeasted rice cakes scented with coconut and cardamom, crisp on the curve and soft inside.

25 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 4 · About 12 molded cakes

History

Vitumbua are coconut rice pancakes of the Swahili coast, cooked in Kenya and along the Tanzanian coast as well. Rice flour, coconut milk, sugar, and yeast make a batter that is poured into a rounded pan, sometimes a pan with deep cups, and cooked until the shell browns. They are breakfast and street food in Mombasa. They are not the fried Zambian vitumbuwa, which have their own page.

Cultural background

Coastal kitchens already cook with coconut and rice. Vitumbua use both, often with cardamom. The rounded pan gives each cake a dome instead of a flat griddle cake. Vendors sell them in the morning beside tea.

Why it tastes this way

Coconut milk is sweet and rich without dairy. Rice keeps the crumb pale and mild. Yeast adds a faint ferment flavor and lift, and cardamom perfumes the fat. The curved pan browns a shell while the covered side stays tender, so the cake tastes fried at the edge and steamed in the middle.

How it is made

Whisk rice flour with coconut milk, water, sugar, yeast, salt, and cardamom. Rest until bubbly. Oil the cups of a rounded pan and cook the batter, turning once, until each cake is brown on the curve and set inside.

Authentic touch. Cook them in a rounded or deeply cupped pan if you have one. A flat crepe loses the shape people expect on the coast. Leave out wheat if you want this gluten-free version.

How it is used

Vitumbua are a Swahili-coast breakfast or a street snack with tea. They are sweet enough to eat plain. A extra splash of coconut milk is a home habit, not a syrup.

Field notes

  • Vitumbua are often cooked in a rounded pan, which gives them a dome.
  • They belong to the Swahili coast, including kitchens in Tanzania.
  • Zambian vitumbuwa are fried rice fritters and are not this pan.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups rice flour (about 200 g)
  • 1 cup coconut milk
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 3 tablespoons sugar
  • 1 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground cardamom
  • 1/4 teaspoon fine salt
  • Oil for the cups

Steps

  1. Whisk the rice flour, coconut milk, water, sugar, yeast, cardamom, and salt into a thick batter. There is no wheat in this recipe.
  2. Cover and leave it for 45 to 60 minutes, until the surface is bubbly.
  3. Oil the cups of a rounded vitumbua pan, or a heavy dimpled pan, and heat them over medium heat.
  4. Fill the cups about two-thirds full. Cover and cook until the bottoms are browned, then turn each cake and cook until the center is set.
  5. Serve warm. These are pan-molded cakes, not the fried fritters called vitumbuwa in Zambia.

The batter contains coconut milk. Coconut is not listed here as a peanut or a tree nut. Skip the recipe if you avoid coconut. Gluten-free only if the rice flour is free of wheat.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat in the oiled pan. The coconut crumb dries out in the refrigerator after a day or two. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • An overnight rest makes the batter tangier.
  • Baking powder instead of yeast is a quicker cake and less fermented.
  • The Tanzanian coast cooks the same family of coconut rice cakes.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Snack
  • Street food
  • Cardamom tea
  • A little extra coconut milk
  • Fresh mango
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Khanom krok

Small coconut-rice cakes with a browned rim and a soft, sweet center, cooked in a dimpled pan.

15 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 20 small cakes

History

Khanom krok are Thai coconut rice pancakes cooked in a dimpled pan. Two batters are usual: a thinner rice batter that crisps, and a richer coconut-cream batter that settles into a custardy middle. They are street snacks and a sweet, not a meal. This recipe uses rice flour, coconut milk, and sugar, with no egg and no wheat, so it is vegan and gluten-free. Khanom bueang is the crisp folded crepe. This is the molded one.

Cultural background

The special pan is heated, oiled, and filled in two pours. The center often looks pale while the rim browns. That contrast is correct. Some cooks add corn or scallion to a salty version. This page stays with the sweet coconut cake.

Why it tastes this way

Coconut milk is both the liquid and the flavor, so the cake tastes sweet even before the sugar. Rice flour keeps it from being a pudding. The rim dries against hot iron and tastes toasted. The center stays soft because the second, richer batter does not cook as hard. A pinch of salt keeps the coconut from tasting flat. There is no ferment and no chile.

How it is made

Cook a rice and coconut batter in a dimpled pan, finishing each cup with a spoon of thick coconut cream.

Authentic touch. Pour the plain batter first, let it seize, then add the thick coconut mixture in the middle. One single batter still works, but the two-pour method is what gives the custard center.

How it is used

Unmold them while warm and eat them as a street snack. They are best the same day.

Field notes

  • Khanom krok are cooked in a dimpled pan, with a crisp rim and a softer coconut center.
  • The sweet version in this recipe has no egg and no wheat.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup rice flour, with no wheat added
  • 1 1/2 cups coconut milk
  • 3 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 cup coconut cream
  • Neutral oil for the molds

Steps

  1. Whisk rice flour, coconut milk, 2 tablespoons of sugar, and the salt into a pourable batter.
  2. Stir the coconut cream with the remaining sugar into a thick topping.
  3. Oil a hot dimpled pan. Fill each cup halfway with the rice batter.
  4. When the edges set, add a spoon of the coconut cream in the center of each cup.
  5. Cover and cook until the rims are brown and the centers are no longer liquid. Unmold and repeat.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A salty cup with scallion instead of sugar
  • A spoon of cooked corn in the center

Pairings and uses

  • Snack
  • Dessert
  • Street food
  • Hot tea
  • A few grains of salt on the sweet center
  • Fresh mango if it is in season
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Kue ape

A small coconut-rice cake with a crisp rim and a soft center that sinks into a crater.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 16 small cakes

History

Kue ape is a Betawi snack of Jakarta, cooked in a dimpled pan. Rice flour, coconut milk, and sugar make the batter, and a little yeast helps the center rise and then fall, leaving the crater that identifies the cake. The rim turns crisp. The middle stays soft and pale. It is a mold cake. Serabi, the thicker Javanese coconut pancake served with syrup, is a different sweet and should not be substituted under this name.

Cultural background

Street vendors in Jakarta sell kue ape from a specialized pan, often in the afternoon. The crater is not a mistake. If the center stays domed and dry, the yeast or the heat was off. This recipe uses no wheat and no egg.

Why it tastes this way

Coconut milk sweetens the rice and smells rich when it browns at the rim. Sugar in the batter is enough. The soft center tastes almost like a warm coconut custard without egg, because the starch sets around moisture. The crisp rim tastes toasted. Yeast adds a faint fermented note, not a sour dosa tang. The contrast of textures is the reason to use the pan.

How it is made

Ferment a rice and coconut batter briefly with yeast, then cook it in a dimpled pan until the rims crisp and the centers crater.

Authentic touch. Do not fill the molds to the top, and do not panic when the centers sink. That sink is the kue ape. Oil the pan lightly so the rims fry rather than steam.

How it is used

Eat them warm, rims first or center first, as a street snack. They are best the day they are cooked.

Field notes

  • Kue ape is a Betawi mold cake. The soft crater in the middle is part of the dish.
  • It is not serabi, which is a plumper pancake served with palm-sugar syrup.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup rice flour, with no wheat added
  • 1 cup coconut milk
  • 3 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1/4 teaspoon dry yeast
  • Neutral oil for the molds

Steps

  1. Whisk rice flour, coconut milk, sugar, salt, and yeast into a smooth batter.
  2. Rest it until slightly bubbly, about 30 to 45 minutes. That rest is not counted as active prep.
  3. Oil a hot dimpled pan. Fill each cup just over halfway.
  4. Cook on medium until the rims are brown and crisp and the centers have set and sunk.
  5. Unmold and repeat with the remaining batter.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A drop of pandan extract for a green crumb
  • A pinch more sugar if your coconut milk is unsweetened

Pairings and uses

  • Snack
  • Street food
  • Dessert
  • Hot tea
  • Grated coconut
  • A second cake, because they are small
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan

India · South Asia

Kuzhi paniyaram

Small fermented rice-and-urad balls, crisp outside, soft inside, with mustard seed and curry leaf.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 18 small cakes

History

Kuzhi paniyaram is fermented rice and urad batter cooked in a dimpled pan, a Tamil Nadu breakfast and tiffin. Kuzhi means the pit or hollow of the pan. The batter is the idli-dosa ferment. The pan, not a new grain, makes the shape. Savory versions take a tempering of mustard seed and curry leaf. Sweet versions use jaggery. This page is the savory one.

Cultural background

Leftover idli batter is the honest weeknight source. The dimples fry the outside while the center steams. A flat griddle would simply make dosa. Telugu and Kannada kitchens have cousin cakes in similar pans. This recipe stays with the Tamil savory paniyaram.

Why it tastes this way

The ferment is mildly sour and bready because the ball is thick. Mustard seed and curry leaf, fried and then folded in, perfume the batter before it ever hits the pan. Oil in the dimples browns a crust that a steamed idli never gets. The contrast, crisp shell and soft sour crumb, is the reason to use the special pan.

How it is made

Fold a mustard and curry-leaf tempering into fermented rice and urad batter, then fry spoonfuls in a heated dimpled pan.

Authentic touch. Heat the empty pan before you oil the pits. Cold oil in a cold mold sticks and pulls the cakes apart.

How it is used

Unmold them when the bottoms are deep gold. Eat with coconut chutney while the crust still crackles.

Field notes

  • The batter is the fermented rice and urad batter. The dimpled pan is the change.
  • Kuzhi refers to the hollows the batter is poured into.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups fermented rice and urad idli or dosa batter
  • 1 teaspoon mustard seeds
  • 10 curry leaves
  • 1 green chile, minced
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil, plus more for the pan
  • A pinch of salt if needed

Steps

  1. Use batter that has already fermented and smells pleasantly sour. Stir it down.
  2. Heat a tablespoon of oil, pop the mustard seeds, and add curry leaves and green chile for a few seconds.
  3. Fold that tempering into the batter.
  4. Heat a kuzhi paniyaram pan until hot. Add a few drops of oil to each dip. Fill the dips about three-quarters full.
  5. Cover and cook, turning the cakes once with a skewer, until both sides are brown and the centers are set. Drain briefly and serve.

Discard fermented batter that is moldy or smells putrid. Turn the cakes so the centers cook through.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Myanmar · Mild · 8 joined pairs

Flying Pancakes of the World

Myanmar · Southeast Asia

Mont lin maya

A pair of small rice cakes from a dimpled pan, one plain and one topped with split peas and shallot, joined together.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 joined pairs

History

Mont lin maya are Myanmar's small batter cakes cooked in a dimpled pan and sold as snacks. The name means husband and wife and refers to the pair: one plain cake and one with a topping, cooked in neighboring cups and then pressed together. Yangon street stalls turn them out through the day. The batter is rice flour. This recipe tops the second cake with boiled split peas and shallot, with no egg and no wheat. A quail egg is another common topping and would add an egg allergen.

Cultural background

The pan is the same idea as other Southeast Asian dimpled pans, with a Burmese batter and a paired serving. The cakes are finger food. A little chile in the pea topping keeps the heat mild. They are a snack, not a feast loaf.

Why it tastes this way

Rice batter fried in a little oil tastes lightly sweet and toasted at the rim. Split peas add an earthy, mealy contrast. Shallot and a green chile make the topping savory. Pressing the plain cake onto the topped one traps that filling and softens the crust just enough. Coconut milk in the batter, used here in a small amount, rounds the grain. The pair is the dish, which is what the name is describing.

How it is made

Cook rice batter in a dimpled pan, leave half the cups plain, top the other half with peas and shallot, and join each pair.

Authentic touch. Cook a plain cup and a topped cup at the same time, then clap them together while they are still flexible. Two plain cakes miss the point of the name.

How it is used

Eat them warm as a street snack. They are savory and mildly hot from the chile in the peas.

Field notes

  • The name mont lin maya refers to the pair of cakes, one plain and one topped, joined together.
  • They are cooked in a dimpled pan and sold as snacks in Myanmar.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup rice flour, with no wheat added
  • 1 cup water
  • 1/2 cup coconut milk
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 cup cooked yellow split peas
  • 1 shallot, minced
  • 1 green chile, minced
  • Neutral oil

Steps

  1. Whisk rice flour, water, coconut milk, and salt into a pourable batter.
  2. Mix the split peas with shallot and green chile.
  3. Oil a hot dimpled pan. Fill the cups halfway.
  4. Leave half the cups plain. Spoon peas onto the other half. Cook until the bottoms are brown and the centers are set.
  5. Press a plain cake onto a topped cake to make a pair. Repeat with the rest.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A quail egg on the topped half, which adds egg
  • A pinch of turmeric in the batter for color

India · No chili heat · 10 sweet appams

Flying Pancakes of the World

India · South Asia

Nei appam

A sweet, dense rice cake scented with banana, jaggery, cardamom, and ghee.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 5 · 10 sweet appams

History

Nei appam is a sweet Kerala cake of rice, jaggery, and banana cooked with ghee in an appam pan. Nei means ghee. It is a tea-time and temple-festival sweet in many Malayali homes, and it is not the savory bowl-shaped appam served with stew. The batter is mixed and cooked, not given the hopper's long coconut-milk ferment. A dimpled paniyaram pan makes a related sweet. This page stays with the appam pan.

Cultural background

Jaggery, not refined white sugar, is the usual sweetener, and it colors the crumb tan. Ripe banana is both flavor and moisture. Ghee is the cooking fat and part of the taste. Leaving it out would make a different sweet.

Why it tastes this way

Jaggery brings molasses and a slight bitterness that white sugar misses. Banana reinforces that sweetness with perfume. Rice flour keeps the cake tender rather than bready. Cardamom is the spice. Ghee browns at the contact point with the pan and smells like buttered toast around an otherwise dairy-free grain.

How it is made

Mix rice flour, melted jaggery, mashed banana, and cardamom, then cook spoonfuls in a ghee-filmed appam pan until the centers set.

Authentic touch. Cook covered on a low flame. High heat burns the jaggery on the outside before the center sets. A ripe, fragrant banana matters more than a perfectly smooth batter.

How it is used

Ease them out while warm and eat them the same day, with tea. They are a snack or a feast sweet, not a breakfast hopper.

Field notes

  • Nei is ghee. The savory coconut appam is a different recipe.
  • Jaggery and banana are the sweetening. This is not a stuffed crepe.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup rice flour, with no wheat added
  • 1/2 cup jaggery, chopped
  • 1 small ripe banana, mashed
  • 1/4 teaspoon cardamom powder
  • 1/4 cup water
  • 3 tablespoons ghee

Steps

  1. Warm the jaggery with the water until it melts. Strain out grit if the jaggery is raw.
  2. Cool the syrup a little and stir in rice flour, banana, and cardamom. The batter should be thick and spoonable. Rest it 10 minutes.
  3. Heat an appam pan over medium-low. Put a little ghee in the bowl of the pan.
  4. Spoon in enough batter to fill the bowl. Cover and cook until the top is set and the underside is deep brown, 4 to 6 minutes.
  5. Turn it out, add more ghee, and repeat. Serve warm.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A few chopped coconut pieces in the batter
  • A dimpled pan instead of an appam pan, which makes a rounder sweet

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Snack
  • Feast or fast day
  • Plain tea
  • A glass of milk
  • Sliced banana
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Nigeria · No chili heat · About 16 molded cakes

Flying Pancakes of the World

Nigeria · West Africa

Nigerian masa

Mildly sour rice cakes with crisp rims and a soft, slightly sweet center.

30 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 4 · About 16 molded cakes

History

Masa is the Hausa fermented rice cake cooked in a dimpled pan. Waina is another name for it. Rice is soaked, ground, and left to sour, then spooned into oiled cups so each cake takes a round, puffed shape. In Kano and across Hausa kitchens it is breakfast and street food, eaten with sugar, honey, or a soup. This recipe is rice only, so it can be gluten-free.

Cultural background

The pan matters as much as the ferment. Traditional cups are set into a wide clay or metal pan. A home cook can use a heavy dimpled pan if the cups are well oiled. The cake is a snack you buy by the piece as well as a dish made for the house.

Why it tastes this way

Fermentation takes the raw starch edge off the rice and leaves a light sourness. A little sugar in the batter keeps it in breakfast territory. The oiled mold fries the rim while the covered center stays tender. It does not taste like a wheat pancake, because there is no wheat.

How it is made

Soak rice, grind it with water into a thick batter, and ferment it until slightly sour. Stir in salt, sugar, and a pinch of yeast if the kitchen is too cool for a wild ferment. Oil the dimples of a masa pan and cook spoonfuls, turning once, until both sides are golden.

Authentic touch. Use a dimpled pan and oil the cups. Waina and masa are the same family of cakes. Do not add wheat flour if you are keeping the gluten-free tag.

How it is used

Masa is breakfast and a street snack. Sugar or honey goes on top, or the cakes are eaten with miyan taushe, a pumpkin soup, or another Hausa soup. Honey and soup are not in the batter.

Field notes

  • Waina is another name for these Hausa rice cakes.
  • They are cooked in a dimpled pan, not poured as a wide flatbread.
  • A rice-only batter can be gluten-free if the yeast and the pan are not carrying wheat.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups raw rice (about 400 g), soaked 4 hours and drained
  • 1 1/2 cups water, for grinding
  • 1/2 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
  • Oil for the dimples

Steps

  1. Grind the drained rice with the water to a thick, slightly grainy batter. Stir in the yeast and sugar. Leave the salt out.
  2. Cover and rest the batter for 4 to 8 hours, until it looks bubbly and smells faintly sour. This is a fermented rice batter.
  3. Stir in the salt. The batter should drop from a spoon in a heavy ribbon. Thin it with a spoon of water if it stands up in peaks.
  4. Oil the cups of a dimpled masa pan and heat them until the oil shimmers. Fill each cup about two-thirds full.
  5. Cover and cook until the bottoms are golden, then turn each cake and cook the second side. Serve warm. Waina is the same cake under another name.

Gluten-free only if the rice, yeast, and pan have no wheat on them. Hot oil in the dimples can spit.

Refrigerate leftover masa and reheat in a lightly oiled pan. The rims soften in a covered box. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Waina is another name, not a different grain.
  • A wild ferment without added yeast takes longer and tastes a little sharper.
  • Wheat flour stirred in for softness removes the gluten-free claim.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Snack
  • Street food
  • A sprinkle of sugar
  • Honey
  • Miyan taushe, pumpkin soup
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free

Netherlands · No chili heat · about 40 poffertjes

Flying Pancakes of the World

Netherlands · Western Europe

Poffertjes

Small yeast pancakes from a dimpled Dutch pan, buttered and dusted with sugar.

1 hr 20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 40 poffertjes

History

Poffertjes are small yeast pancakes cooked in a dimpled pan. Buckwheat is traditional in the batter along with wheat, which is how this recipe is written, so they are not gluten-free. They are fair food and restaurant food, served with melted butter and powdered sugar. A story that they began in a single monastery is not something this page can verify, so it is left out. The pan is the method.

Cultural background

You eat them hot, with a fork, at a stall or a poffertjes house. They are a snack and a dessert, not a dinner plate unless a child decides otherwise.

Why it tastes this way

Yeast and a little buckwheat make them tangy under the sugar. The dimples brown at the edges and stay soft inside. Butter and powdered sugar are the sauce. Without buckwheat they taste like plain doughnut holes. The buckwheat is the bitter note that keeps the sugar in check.

How it is made

A yeast batter of buckwheat, wheat flour, milk, and egg rises, then is spooned into a buttered dimpled pan, turned once, and served with butter and powdered sugar.

Authentic touch. Use a poffertjes pan if you can. Turn each cake once with a skewer or a fork when the top is set. A flat pan will cook the batter and will not make poffertjes.

How it is used

Hot, with butter and powdered sugar, as a snack, a fair treat, or dessert.

Field notes

  • They are cooked in a dimpled pan and turned once.
  • Buckwheat and wheat together are the traditional batter here.
  • Butter and powdered sugar are the serving, not syrup.

Ingredients

  • 80 g buckwheat flour
  • 80 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tsp instant yeast
  • 1 tsp sugar
  • 250 ml warm milk
  • 1 large egg
  • 1 pinch fine salt
  • 40 g unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the pan
  • Powdered sugar, for serving

Steps

  1. Whisk both flours, the yeast, sugar, milk, egg, salt, and melted butter into a thick batter. Cover until bubbly, about 1 hour.
  2. Heat a poffertjes pan and brush the cups with butter.
  3. Fill each cup about two-thirds full. Cook until the tops set, then turn and brown the other side.
  4. Serve at once with butter and a heavy dusting of powdered sugar.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • All wheat flour, which loses the buckwheat taste and is still not gluten-free
  • A spoon of chocolate spread, a modern stall topping this recipe does not require

Pairings and uses

  • Snack
  • Dessert
  • Street food
  • Powdered sugar
  • Melted butter
  • A little jam on the side
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Sweden · No chili heat · about 35 small pancakes

Flying Pancakes of the World

Sweden · Northern Europe

Swedish plättar

Tiny Swedish pancakes from a plätt pan, with jam and cream.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 35 small pancakes

History

Plättar are small Swedish pancakes cooked in a plättjärn, a pan with several round dips, often seven. The batter is the thin pannkaka batter, and the pan is what changes the cake. They are a home breakfast and a dessert, served with jam and sometimes cream. They are not poffertjes, which are yeasted and Dutch, and they are not the large pannkakor on the previous page.

Cultural background

A plätt pan is a normal tool in a Swedish kitchen. Without one, you can cook tiny pancakes in an ordinary pan, and you should say that is a substitute. The shape and the browned rim come from the mold.

Why it tastes this way

The small round browns at the edge and stays tender in the middle. Butter in the cups adds flavor fast because the cakes are thin. Jam is tart against a lightly sweet batter. They taste the same family as pannkakor, with more crust per bite.

How it is made

A thin egg batter is spooned into a buttered plätt pan, turned once, and served with jam.

Authentic touch. Grease each cup, not only the first one. Fill the dips shallowly. Turn the plättar with a thin skewer when the top has set.

How it is used

In a pile, with jam, at breakfast or as dessert. Cream is optional.

Field notes

  • A plätt pan often has seven dips.
  • The batter is close to pannkakor. The mold is the difference.
  • They are not Dutch poffertjes, which use yeast and buckwheat.

Ingredients

  • 120 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tbsp sugar
  • 1/4 tsp fine salt
  • 2 large eggs
  • 350 ml whole milk
  • 30 g unsalted butter, for the pan
  • Jam, for serving

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, sugar, salt, eggs, and milk into a thin batter. Rest 10 minutes.
  2. Heat a plätt pan and butter each cup.
  3. Spoon in a shallow layer. Cook until the surface sets, turn, and brown the other side.
  4. Serve in a pile with jam.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A savory plätt with cheese, less usual than jam
  • The same batter as larger pannkakor when you have no plätt pan

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Dessert
  • Lingonberry or strawberry jam
  • Whipped cream
  • Sugar
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Japan · No chili heat · 16 balls

Flying Pancakes of the World

Japan · East Asia

Takoyaki

Crisp-edged batter balls with a piece of octopus in the center, finished with sauce and bonito.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 16 balls

History

Takoyaki are octopus batter balls from Osaka, cooked in a pan of round molds. They are a cousin of the savory batter family that also produced okonomiyaki, and they are not a flat cake. Octopus, pickled ginger, and tenkasu sit in a thin wheat batter. The balls are turned with a pick until the outside browns and the inside stays soft. Octopus is a mollusk. This page tags shellfish and names the octopus in the recipe. Bonito flakes on top add fish. The snack is not vegetarian.

Cultural background

Osaka stalls sell them by the boat-shaped plate. The turn matters: an unturned ball is raw on top and burnt below. Mayonnaise and a brown sauce are the usual finish. No brand is required. Aonori and bonito complete the plate.

Why it tastes this way

The batter takes on dashi, so it tastes lightly marine even before the octopus. Octopus itself is sweet and chewy if it is not overcooked into rubber. Pickled ginger and scallion cut the softness. Crisped edges of batter contrast with a custardy center. Sauce adds sweetness, and bonito adds a smoky fish finish. The shape keeps a pocket of filling that a flat pancake would spread out.

How it is made

Pour a dashi batter into a takoyaki pan, add octopus and ginger, and turn the balls until they are round and browned.

Authentic touch. Fill the molds with batter first, add octopus, then keep turning. If you drop the octopus in late, it sits raw on the surface. Oil the pan well.

How it is used

Eat them hot from the plate, with picks. They are street food. The center stays very hot.

Field notes

  • Takoyaki are molded balls, a cousin of Osaka's savory batters, not a flat pancake.
  • The filling is octopus. The allergen tag is shellfish, and bonito on top is fish.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose wheat flour
  • 1 egg
  • 1 1/2 cups bonito dashi
  • 150 grams cooked octopus, cut in bite-size pieces
  • 2 tablespoons pickled ginger, chopped
  • 2 scallions, chopped
  • 2 tablespoons tenkasu (tempura bits), optional
  • Neutral oil
  • Sauce, mayonnaise, aonori, and bonito flakes to finish

Steps

  1. Whisk flour, egg, and dashi into a thin batter. Stir in ginger and scallion.
  2. Oil a hot takoyaki pan. Fill each mold with batter and add a piece of octopus.
  3. Cook until the bottoms set, then break the edges and turn each ball with a pick.
  4. Keep turning until the balls are golden and the batter is cooked through, adding a few tenkasu if you use them.
  5. Pile them on a plate, add sauce, mayonnaise, aonori, and bonito, and eat while hot.

Use octopus that has been kept cold, and cook the batter until the center is no longer liquid. The filling stays hot after cooking.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A cube of cheese in place of one octopus piece, which adds dairy
  • Extra pickled ginger

Pairings and uses

  • Street food
  • Savory meal
  • Snack
  • Savory brown sauce
  • Mayonnaise
  • Aonori and bonito flakes
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free
