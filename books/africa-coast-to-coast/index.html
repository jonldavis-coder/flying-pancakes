Baghrir

A semolina pancake cooked on one side, full of holes, eaten with butter and honey.

1 hr 10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 pancakes

History

Baghrir are Moroccan pancakes, sometimes called thousand-hole pancakes, made from semolina and flour, cooked on one side so the top becomes a honeycomb. They are a breakfast and tea food across the Maghreb. Melted butter and honey are poured into the holes. They are not flipped.

Cultural background

Yeast or baking powder lifts the holes. This recipe uses yeast for the traditional open crumb. Serve them warm. The holes are for the butter.

Why it tastes this way

Semolina gives a tender, slightly grainy crumb that wheat alone does not. A wet batter on a moderate pan boils into holes on top and sets before anyone flips it. Butter and honey fill those holes, which is why the texture is the whole point. The Maghreb’s semolina kitchen, shared with couscous, is why the pancake is not a wheat crepe.

How it is made

A thin semolina yeast batter is cooked on one side in a lightly oiled pan.

Authentic touch. Cook on medium-low and do not flip. The top should look dry and full of holes. A high heat burns the bottom while the top is still batter.

How it is used

Warm, with melted butter and honey.

Field notes

  • The holes are the dish. If there are none, the batter was too thick or the pan too hot.
  • They are cooked on one side only.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup fine semolina
  • 1/2 cup flour
  • 1 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 1/2 cups warm water
  • Butter and honey, to serve

Steps

  1. Blend or whisk all the batter ingredients until smooth. Rest 1 hour.
  2. Cook small rounds on a lightly oiled medium-low pan.
  3. When the top is full of holes and dry, remove them. Do not flip.
  4. Serve with melted butter and honey.
  5. The batter should be thin.
  6. Eat them the same day.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Algerian and Tunisian kitchens also make thousand-hole pancakes. The Moroccan name baghrir is the one used here.
  • Baking powder is a faster substitute and makes fewer, coarser holes.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Snack
  • butter
  • honey
  • Vegetarian
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Msemen

Square, layered pan-fried flatbread, flaky with oil.

40 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 8 · 8 squares

History

Msemen are Moroccan layered flatbreads, folded into squares and cooked on a griddle with oil. They are a breakfast and street bread, richer than a plain pancake because of the layers. They are often eaten with honey, cheese, or alongside a tagine. They are a griddled bread, included here as a pancake relative, not as a crepe.

Cultural background

The dough is soft. Each piece is oiled, folded, and flattened again so the layers stay. A good msemen peels a little when you tear it.

Why it tastes this way

Oil between thin layers of dough fries into flakes, which is why msemen taste rich without a sweet batter. Semolina on the board keeps them from sticking and adds a sandy crunch. Honey on a hot square melts into the layers. The technique is North African pan bread, closer to a laminated dough than to a poured pancake, and honest about that.

How it is made

A soft dough is divided, oiled, folded into squares, and cooked on a griddle.

Authentic touch. Oil your hands. If the dough fights you, it needs another spoon of water. Cook on medium until both sides are spotted and the center is not raw dough.

How it is used

Warm, with honey or soft cheese.

Field notes

  • Msemen are folded squares. The layers are the point.
  • They are a griddled bread, not a batter pancake.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups flour
  • 1/2 cup fine semolina
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 3/4 cup warm water
  • 1/4 cup oil, plus more for folding

Steps

  1. Mix flour, semolina, salt, water, and 1/4 cup oil into a soft dough. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Divide into 8. Oil each, flatten, fold into a square, and flatten again.
  3. Cook on a medium oiled griddle until spotted and cooked through, turning once.
  4. Serve warm.
  5. Honey is the usual sweet finish.
  6. They are best the hour they are cooked.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Meloui is the coiled round cousin.
  • Stuffed msemen with peppers or meat are a meal and a different recipe.

Kesra

A semolina griddle bread, sturdy and lightly browned.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · one 8-inch round

History

Kesra is an Algerian home bread, often semolina, cooked on a griddle or in a pan. There are several kesra styles, some closer to a thick flatbread. This is a pan-cooked semolina round, the everyday bread, not a sweet pancake and not baghrir. It is included because Algeria’s griddle bread is not the same as Moroccan msemen.

Cultural background

It is torn and eaten with olive oil, honey, or a tagine. The crumb is tighter than a coiled meloui.

Why it tastes this way

Durum semolina tastes sweeter and sandier than soft wheat, which is the flavor of a lot of Maghreb bread. A pan rather than a bakery oven gives a spotted crust and a moist center. Olive oil is the fat of the region. The bread is plain because it is meant to meet a stew.

How it is made

Semolina, salt, water, and oil are kneaded briefly, rested, shaped into a round, and cooked in a covered skillet.

Authentic touch. Cook it covered and relatively slowly. Semolina dough browns before it cooks if you rush. It is done when it sounds hollow and the center is not wet.

How it is used

Warm, with olive oil or alongside a stew.

Field notes

  • Kesra here means the pan-cooked semolina bread.
  • It is a bread for savory food, not a dessert stack.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups fine semolina
  • 1/2 cup flour
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2/3 cup warm water
  • 2 tablespoons olive oil

Steps

  1. Mix everything into a firm dough. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Shape a round about 1/2 inch thick.
  3. Cook in a lightly oiled covered skillet over medium-low, turning once, until both sides are golden and the center is cooked.
  4. Cool briefly.
  5. Cut or tear.
  6. Serve warm.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Stuffed kesra exists and is a different recipe.
  • Baghrir is the holey pancake, not this bread.

Pairings and uses

  • Side dish
  • olive oil
  • honey

Mlewi

A soft, oiled Tunisian flatbread cooked on a griddle.

30 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 breads

History

Mlewi, also spelled mlawi, is a Tunisian pan flatbread related to msemen, oiled and folded or rolled, then cooked. It is street and home breakfast food, often eaten with harissa, tuna, or honey. Harissa adds chile. This recipe is the bread. Tuna would add fish.

Cultural background

It should be soft and layered. Vendors cook it to order. Honey and harissa are two different breakfasts, sweet and hot.

Why it tastes this way

Oil in the layers makes mlewi rich and pliable, a wrap for harissa or tuna rather than a dry cracker. Tunisia’s breakfast culture puts chile paste on bread, which is why the same dough can be sweet with honey or hot with harissa. The dough itself is mild. The condiment is the decision.

How it is made

A soft dough is oiled, flattened, and cooked on a griddle until supple and spotted.

Authentic touch. Keep the dough soft and the griddle moderate. A hard crust means the bread will not fold around a filling.

How it is used

Warm, with honey or with harissa. Harissa makes the plate spicy. The bread is not.

Field notes

  • Mlewi is the Tunisian oiled flatbread in this family.
  • Harissa is a condiment, not part of the dough.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups flour
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 3/4 cup water
  • 3 tablespoons oil, plus more for the pan

Steps

  1. Mix flour, salt, water, and oil into a soft dough. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Divide, oil, and flatten into rounds.
  3. Cook on a medium griddle until spotted and cooked through.
  4. Keep them soft under a towel.
  5. Serve with honey or harissa.
  6. They are best warm.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A tuna and egg filling is a common street meal and adds fish and egg.
  • Msemen is the Moroccan square cousin.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Bread course
  • honey
  • harissa

Tatale

Ripe plantain fritters, sweet and spiced, fried in small cakes.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 12 fritters

History

Tatale are Ghanaian ripe-plantain fritters, mashed with flour, ginger, and chile, then fried. They are a snack and a side, especially along the coast and in home cooking. They are fritters, not flipped pancakes, and they belong here as the plantain cake of the Gulf of Guinea.

Cultural background

Ripe, spotted plantains are essential. Green plantains will not mash into a sweet batter. They are eaten hot, with beans or on their own.

Why it tastes this way

A ripe plantain is sweet because its starch has turned to sugar, which is why tatale taste like a dessert and a snack at once. Ginger and chile are the heat and perfume of Ghanaian frying. A little flour holds the mash. Palm oil is a traditional frying fat with its own red color and flavor. Neutral oil is the plainer home choice written here, and palm oil is the variation.

How it is made

Mashed ripe plantain is mixed with flour, ginger, and chile and fried as small cakes.

Authentic touch. Use plantains that are yellow with black spots. Mash them fine. Fry spoonfuls in enough oil to brown the sides, and drain them well.

How it is used

Hot, as a snack or beside beans.

Field notes

  • Ripe plantains are the requirement. Green ones are a different, starchier food.
  • Tatale are fritters. They are not kenkey or banku, which are fermented doughs.

Ingredients

  • 2 very ripe plantains
  • 1/4 cup flour
  • 1 teaspoon grated ginger
  • 1 fresh chile, seeded and minced, or 1/4 teaspoon cayenne
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Oil for frying

Steps

  1. Mash the plantains. Stir in flour, ginger, chile, and salt.
  2. Heat 1/2 inch of oil over medium.
  3. Fry spoonfuls until deep golden on both sides.
  4. Drain.
  5. Serve hot.
  6. The center should be hot and set.

Fry over steady heat. Chile heat is real. Seed the chile if you want it milder.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Palm oil for frying is traditional and changes the color and flavor.
  • A milder cake uses less chile.

Pairings and uses

  • Snack
  • Side dish
  • beans
  • stew
  • Vegetarian

Masa

Sour rice cakes, cooked in small molds, crisp at the edge.

40 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 12 cakes

History

Masa, also called waina, is a Hausa fermented rice cake from northern Nigeria and the wider Sahel, including Niger. Rice or sometimes millet is soaked, ground, fermented, and cooked in small round molds or a pan. It is a street and home breakfast, eaten plain, with honey, or with a savory sauce. This version uses rice and a skillet if you do not have the clay molds.

Cultural background

The ferment is the sourness. Yeast can hurry a home batch, and a natural ferment is the older method. The recipe uses a pinch of yeast so the timing is reliable, and it says so.

Why it tastes this way

Rice, soaked and soured, tastes gently tart and a little sweet when the edges fry. The small molds, or a small skillet cake, give a crisp rim and a soft center. Masa is a Sahel food, where rice and millet are the grains, not wheat. Honey or pepper sauce are the two directions, sweet or hot, and both are served.

How it is made

Soaked rice is blended, fermented with a little yeast, and fried as small thick cakes.

Authentic touch. Grind or blend the soaked rice until smooth. Lumpy batter makes a raw center. Oil the pan well. The cakes stick when the surface is dry.

How it is used

Plain, with honey, or with a pepper sauce on the side.

Field notes

  • Masa and waina are names you will both meet for these rice cakes.
  • The yeast here is a home timing aid. A wild ferment of the same batter is traditional.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup raw rice, soaked 8 hours and drained
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 1/4 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1/2 teaspoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Blend the soaked rice with water until smooth.
  2. Stir in yeast, sugar, and salt. Rest 1 to 2 hours until bubbly.
  3. Oil a skillet or muffin pan over medium.
  4. Fry small mounds until set and browned on both sides.
  5. The center should be cooked, not gritty.
  6. Serve warm.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Millet masa is a grain variation.
  • Sinasir is the related Hausa cake often made from millet, on its own page.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Snack
  • Street food
  • honey
  • pepper sauce

Sinasir

A fermented millet pancake, sour and soft, from the Hausa kitchen.

1 hr 10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 10 cakes

History

Sinasir is a Hausa fermented millet pancake, related to masa and often named separately. Millet is soaked, ground, and soured, then cooked as cakes. It is a northern Nigerian breakfast grain food. Recipes vary by town. This is a home version with millet flour and a short ferment.

Cultural background

It is eaten with sugar, honey, or a soup. The millet tastes earthier than rice masa.

Why it tastes this way

Millet is a Sahel staple that stores well in a hot, dry climate, which is why the cake tastes of millet rather than of wheat. Fermentation adds sourness and makes the flour easier to digest and more aromatic. A short ferment with a pinch of yeast is the reliable home path. A longer wild ferment is more sour.

How it is made

Millet flour is mixed with water and a little yeast, rested, and cooked as small pancakes.

Authentic touch. Rest the batter until it smells faintly sour and looks a little risen. Cook the cakes covered for a minute so the millet center sets.

How it is used

Warm, with honey or alongside a soup.

Field notes

  • Sinasir is the millet pancake. Masa is often the rice one. Households do blur the names.
  • Millet keeps the cake gluten-free.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup millet flour
  • 1 1/4 cups water
  • 1/4 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Stir millet, water, and yeast. Rest 1 hour, until slightly bubbly.
  2. Add salt.
  3. Fry small cakes in a lightly oiled pan, covered briefly, until set.
  4. Flip and brown.
  5. Serve warm.
  6. The center should not taste raw.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Masa is the rice cake in the same family.
  • Without yeast, ferment the batter 24 hours if your kitchen is warm, and watch for mold.

Pairings and uses

  • soup

Kisra

A thin, sour sorghum crepe, used to scoop stew.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 sheets

History

Kisra is the fermented sorghum bread of Sudan, cooked paper-thin on a hot plate. It is a daily bread for stews, in the same broad family as injera and laxoox and not the same recipe. Sorghum is the grain. The ferment is sour. South Sudanese cooking also uses kisra. This page uses Sudan as the home and lists South Sudan as a place it is also cooked.

Cultural background

The sheets are thin enough to fold. They are not fluffy injera. A wide nonstick pan is the home version of the traditional hot plate.

Why it tastes this way

Sorghum tolerates heat and drought better than wheat, which is why the daily bread of much of Sudan tastes of sorghum. Fermentation softens the grain and adds sourness that balances rich stews. Cooking it thin and fast dries it into a flexible sheet rather than a sponge. The difference from injera is thickness and grain.

How it is made

Fermented sorghum batter is spread thin and cooked briefly on one side.

Authentic touch. The batter should be as thin as crepe batter. Spread it fast on a hot pan. If it tears, thin it with a spoon of water and work cleaner. Do not expect injera’s tall sponge.

How it is used

Folded, with stew.

Field notes

  • Kisra is thinner than injera and is typically sorghum.
  • It is a sour bread for stews, not a sweet pancake.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup sorghum flour
  • 1 1/2 cups water
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Mix sorghum and water. Ferment 1 to 2 days until sour and bubbly.
  2. Add salt and thin to crepe consistency.
  3. Spread very thin on a hot nonstick pan.
  4. Cook until the sheet lifts. Do not brown it hard.
  5. Fold and stack under a cloth.
  6. Serve with stew.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Injera is thicker and usually teff.
  • A wheat kisra exists in some cities and is not gluten-free.

Pairings and uses

  • Bread course
  • Supper
  • stew
  • lentils
  • Vegetarian

Kita

A quick, unfermented teff flatbread, milder than injera.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 6 rounds

History

Kita is a quick Ethiopian flatbread, often teff or other flour mixed and cooked without the long ferment of injera. It is everyday bread, softer and less sour. Chechebsa, or kita firfir, is torn kita cooked with spices and is a breakfast. This page is the plain kita. It is not a failed injera.

Cultural background

When there is no time to ferment, kita is the bread. It can be torn and dressed with spiced butter for breakfast. This version is teff and salt, vegan as written.

Why it tastes this way

Without fermentation, teff tastes toasted and grainy rather than sour. That is the point of kita: the same grain, a different schedule. Injera’s sourness takes days. Kita takes minutes. The flavor is the grain itself, which is why a spiced butter breakfast exists, to dress a plain bread.

How it is made

Teff flour and water are mixed into a batter and cooked on both sides.

Authentic touch. Cook it on a medium pan like a thick crepe, and flip it, unlike injera. It will not grow eyes. Do not wait for them.

How it is used

Plain, or torn and finished with a spiced butter if you eat dairy. The bread itself is vegan.

Field notes

  • Kita is the quick bread. It is not supposed to taste like injera.
  • Teff keeps it gluten-free.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup teff flour
  • 1 cup water
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon oil

Steps

  1. Stir flour, water, and salt into a thick batter.
  2. Oil a skillet lightly over medium.
  3. Spread a round and cook until the underside browns.
  4. Flip and cook through.
  5. Stack under a cloth.
  6. Serve warm.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Chechebsa is torn kita warmed with spices.
  • Injera is the fermented bread.

Pairings and uses

  • Bread course
  • Breakfast
  • spiced butter
  • honey

Injera

Sour, spongy teff flatbread, cooked on one side so the top is full of eyes.

30 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 rounds

History

Injera is the fermented teff flatbread of Ethiopia, and a close relative is eaten in Eritrea. Teff is a tiny grain domesticated in the highlands. The batter ferments for days, then is poured on a covered mitad, a clay griddle, and cooked on one side until it is spongy with holes. It is the plate and the utensil for stews. This recipe uses 100 percent teff so it is gluten-free. Many restaurant injeras mix in wheat or barley, which changes the taste and the gluten status.

Cultural background

A meal is injera with several stews, wat, shared from one platter. The sourness is desired. A sweet injera is a different, less typical bread.

Why it tastes this way

Teff grows in the highlands where other grains struggle, which is why the daily bread tastes of teff rather than of wheat. Fermentation makes the sourness and the bubbles. Cooking on one side, covered, steams the top into a sponge instead of a cracker. The eyes, the holes, hold the stew. Climate, grain, and fermentation are the whole flavor.

How it is made

Teff flour and water ferment, then the batter is cooked like a large pancake on one side in a covered pan.

Authentic touch. Ferment until the batter smells sour and looks bubbly, usually one to three days in a warm kitchen. Pour in a spiral on a hot nonstick pan, cover, and do not flip. If it browns black, the pan is too hot.

How it is used

Under and with stews, or rolled up beside them. It is not a syrup bread.

Field notes

  • One hundred percent teff injera is gluten-free. Many restaurant versions add other grains.
  • It is cooked on one side. The holes are the sign it worked.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups teff flour
  • 3 cups water, plus more as needed
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Stir teff and water into a thin batter. Cover loosely and ferment 1 to 3 days until sour and bubbly.
  2. Stir in the salt and thin with water if it is thicker than crepe batter.
  3. Heat a nonstick skillet, wipe it with a drop of oil, and pour a thin round.
  4. Cover and cook until the top is set and full of holes, about 2 minutes. Do not flip.
  5. Cool on a cloth.
  6. Stack and serve with stew.

Ferment in a clean container. If the batter smells rotten or grows mold, discard it. Sour and yeasty is the goal.

Keep cooked injera covered at room temperature for a day or refrigerated for a few days. Do not can the batter. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Eritrean injera is the sister bread.
  • A mixed-grain injera is common and is not gluten-free.

Pairings and uses

  • Supper
  • lentil stew
  • greens
  • spiced chicken

Eritrean Injera

A sour teff flatbread, the sister of Ethiopian injera, served with stews.

30 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 rounds

History

Eritrean injera is the same highland family as Ethiopian injera: fermented teff batter cooked on a mitad into a spongy bread. Eritrea and Ethiopia share the bread and differ in some stews and in history. This page does not treat the bread as the property of one capital. The home method here is 100 percent teff, with the same warning that wheat blends are common and not gluten-free.

Cultural background

The bread lines a platter. Diners tear pieces and pick up stew. The sourness is part of the meal’s balance against spiced sauces.

Why it tastes this way

Teff and fermentation taste sour and earthy because that is what the grain does when it is soured in water. The highlands are why teff is the grain. A covered griddle is why the texture is a sponge. Eritrean and Ethiopian injera can differ in thickness and sourness by household. The written method is the shared one.

How it is made

Fermented teff batter is poured thin and cooked covered until the eyes form.

Authentic touch. Use the freshest ferment that already smells pleasantly sour. Over-fermented batter tastes harsh. Cook covered, one side only.

How it is used

With stews, as the bread of the meal.

Field notes

  • Eritrea and Ethiopia share injera. The stews are where many differences show up.
  • This recipe is teff only.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups teff flour
  • 3 cups water
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Mix teff and water and ferment 1 to 3 days until bubbly and sour.
  2. Salt the batter and thin it if needed.
  3. Cook thin rounds in a hot covered nonstick pan until the top sets. Do not flip.
  4. Cool on a cloth.
  5. Serve with stew.
  6. Discard any batter that molds.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Ethiopian injera is the neighboring page.
  • Wheat-mixed injera is a different bread.

Pairings and uses

  • Bread course
  • Supper
  • lentil stew
  • greens
  • Vegetarian

Laxoox

A spongy, lightly sour flatbread, softer and often smaller than injera.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 cakes

History

Laxoox, also called canjeero, is the fermented Somali pancake, a relative of injera and of Yemeni lahoh. It is breakfast food, eaten with honey, ghee, or a stew. Sorghum, teff, or a mix of flours appear in different houses. This version uses teff or sorghum flour, both gluten-free, and says to pick one and name it.

Cultural background

It is cooked in a pan on one side until holes form. Diaspora kitchens keep the ferment going with a spoon of yesterday’s batter. That spoon is a starter, not a packaged yeast.

Why it tastes this way

Fermentation sours the grain and lifts the holes, the same science as injera, with a Somali breakfast habit of honey or ghee on a smaller cake. Sorghum is a dryland grain, which fits the climate better than wheat. The sourness should be gentle. A harsh ferment means it went too long.

How it is made

A fermented sorghum or teff batter is cooked as small spongy pancakes on one side.

Authentic touch. Keep a spoon of batter in the refrigerator to start the next batch. Cook on medium, covered, without flipping, until the top is dry.

How it is used

With honey or ghee, or with a stew. Honey is not vegan. The bread is.

Field notes

  • Laxoox and canjeero are names for the Somali fermented pancake.
  • A saved spoon of batter starts the next batch.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup teff flour or sorghum flour
  • 1 1/2 cups water
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon of a previous batter, if you have it

Steps

  1. Mix flour, water, and starter if you have it. Ferment 1 to 2 days until bubbly.
  2. Stir in the salt.
  3. Cook small rounds in a covered nonstick pan until holes form and the top sets. Do not flip.
  4. Serve warm.
  5. Save a spoon of batter, refrigerated, for next time.
  6. Discard moldy batter.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Yemeni lahoh is the related pancake across the water.
  • Wheat flour versions exist and are not gluten-free.

Malawah

A soft, sweet pan-fried bread, often with egg, cardamom, and a little sugar.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 cakes

History

Malawah, or malawax, is a sweet Somali pancake, different from the sour laxoox. It is a softer, richer breakfast or snack, often with egg, sugar, and cardamom, pan-fried in oil or ghee. It is closer to a sweet griddle cake than to injera.

Cultural background

It is eaten with honey or on its own. Cardamom is the perfume. Street vendors and home cooks both make it.

Why it tastes this way

Sugar, egg, and cardamom move this cake into the sweet breakfast category, while laxoox stays sour and bready. Cardamom is a trade spice of the Indian Ocean coast, which is why it shows up in Somali sweets. Oil or ghee gives a fried tenderness a dry sour bread does not have.

How it is made

A sweet egg batter with cardamom is fried as small thick pancakes.

Authentic touch. Cook on medium and flip once. It should be golden, not as pale as laxoox and not as holey. A cover helps the center set.

How it is used

Warm, plain or with honey.

Field notes

  • Malawah is the sweet Somali pancake. Laxoox is the sour one.
  • Cardamom is a typical spice in the sweet batter.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup flour
  • 1 large egg
  • 3/4 cup milk
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon cardamom
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • Oil or ghee for the pan

Steps

  1. Whisk the batter until smooth.
  2. Fry small cakes in a little oil over medium.
  3. Flip when the underside is golden.
  4. Cook until the center is set.
  5. Serve warm.
  6. Honey on top is optional.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Laxoox is the sour fermented bread. Do not swap the names.
  • Ghee instead of oil is richer.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Dessert
  • honey
  • tea
  • Vegetarian

Kenyan Chapati

A soft, layered pan bread, flaky with oil.

40 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 chapatis

History

East African chapati is a pan-fried wheat bread brought and adapted through the Indian Ocean world, especially by South Asian cooks and traders on the Swahili coast, and now a Kenyan everyday bread. It is flakier and often oilier than many Indian rotis. A Ugandan rolex is a chapati rolled around an omelette, a street meal that depends on this bread. This page is the chapati.

Cultural background

It is served with stew, beans, or tea. The dough is rolled, oiled, coiled, and rolled again, which makes the layers.

Why it tastes this way

Oil between coils fries into flaky layers, which is why Kenyan chapati tastes richer than a dry roast. Wheat flour is an imported staple that became local through trade and, later, through daily cooking far from the coast. The bread tastes of wheat and fry, a partner for spicy stews rather than a sweet pancake. The South Asian origin and the East African life are both true, and neither cancels the other.

How it is made

A soft dough is oiled, coiled, rolled, and cooked on a griddle.

Authentic touch. Coil and reroll so you can see layers when you tear it. Cook on medium with a little oil, pressing with a spatula so it puffs in spots. Keep the cooked breads wrapped so they stay soft.

How it is used

With beans or stew. For a rolex, a cook rolls it around a thin omelette. That adds egg and is a different dish.

Field notes

  • Kenyan chapati is an Indian Ocean bread that became East African daily food.
  • The coil is what makes the layers.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups flour
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 3/4 cup warm water
  • 3 tablespoons oil, plus more for cooking

Steps

  1. Mix flour, salt, water, and 1 tablespoon oil into a soft dough. Rest 20 minutes.
  2. Divide into 6. Roll each, oil it, roll it into a coil, and roll it out again.
  3. Cook on a medium griddle with a little oil, turning, until spotted and puffed in places.
  4. Wrap in a towel.
  5. Serve warm.
  6. They stiffen if they cool uncovered.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Ugandan rolex adds an omelette.
  • A plain rolled chapati without the coil is less flaky.

  • Side dish

Vitumbua

Coconut rice cakes, sweet and cardamom-scented, crisp outside.

1 hr 10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · about 12 cakes

History

Vitumbua are Swahili coast rice-and-coconut cakes, cooked in a special pan with round wells, related in shape to appam pans and to æbleskiver pans. They are a Zanzibar and coastal Tanzanian breakfast and snack. Rice, coconut milk, sugar, and cardamom are the flavor. Yeast lifts them.

Cultural background

The coconut is the coast. Cardamom is the Indian Ocean trade. They are sweet, not savory, and they are eaten with tea.

Why it tastes this way

Coconut milk makes the crumb rich without dairy, and rice keeps it soft. Cardamom is a traded spice that became local in coastal sweets. The well pan fries the sides so the cake is crisp outside and tender inside, a shape that suits street selling. Sugar is moderate. The coconut is the sweetness people remember.

How it is made

A yeasted batter of rice flour and coconut milk is cooked in round wells.

Authentic touch. Use full-fat coconut milk. Light coconut milk makes them dry. Turn the cakes in the wells if your pan is deep, so both ends brown. An æbleskiver or appe pan is a fair tool.

How it is used

Warm, with tea. They are already sweet.

Field notes

  • Vitumbua are a Swahili coast coconut rice cake.
  • Cardamom points at Indian Ocean trade, not at a random spice.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup rice flour
  • 1 cup coconut milk
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1/2 teaspoon cardamom
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • Oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Stir the batter and rest 1 hour until slightly risen.
  2. Oil an æbleskiver, appe, or similar well pan over medium.
  3. Cook the batter in the wells, turning, until golden and set.
  4. Serve warm.
  5. Do not use low-fat coconut milk if you can help it.
  6. They are best the day they are cooked.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A little mashed banana in the batter is a home variation.
  • Plain rice flour pancakes in a flat pan are not quite vitumbua. The wells matter.

Mofo Gasy

Sweet, tangy rice cakes cooked in rounds, a street breakfast.

30 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 12 cakes

History

Mofo gasy means Malagasy bread. The common street version is a fermented rice cake, slightly sweet and sour, cooked in small molds or a pan and sold in the morning. Rice is the staple of Madagascar. The cake is what a rice country does for a portable breakfast.

Cultural background

Vendors cook them in ring molds. A home skillet works. They are eaten plain or with coffee. The sourness comes from a ferment or from a little yeast and time.

Why it tastes this way

Rice, soured, tastes mildly sweet and tangy, and sugar in the batter pushes it toward a cake rather than a bread. Madagascar’s daily rice is why the breakfast is not wheat. A ring mold gives a neat round that cooks through at the edge first. The ferment is the flavor that makes them more than plain rice flour pancakes.

How it is made

Soaked rice is blended, sweetened, fermented briefly with yeast, and cooked as small cakes.

Authentic touch. Blend soaked rice very smooth. Rest the batter until it is a little sour and bubbly. Oil the rings or the pan or the cakes will stick and tear.

How it is used

Warm, as breakfast, plain or with a little honey if you eat honey.

Field notes

  • Mofo gasy is a rice cake, a breakfast sold on the street.
  • Rice keeps it free of gluten.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup rice, soaked 8 hours and drained
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • Oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Blend the rice and water until smooth.
  2. Stir in sugar, yeast, and salt. Rest 1 to 2 hours until bubbly.
  3. Oil a skillet or small rings over medium.
  4. Cook small cakes until set and browned on both sides.
  5. Serve warm.
  6. The center should be tender, not gritty.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A wild ferment without yeast takes longer and can be more sour.
  • Savory mofo exist. This is the sweet street style.

South African Pannekoek

A thin, large pancake with cinnamon sugar, a fair and church-fete food.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 large pancakes

History

South African pannekoek are thin pancakes in the Dutch tradition, renamed and kept at church fetes, school events, and home kitchens. Cinnamon sugar is the usual finish. The Dutch settlement at the Cape is why the name and the crepe method are there. They are a social food: someone stands at a large pan and rolls them to order.

Cultural background

A pannekoek stall at a fete is a recognizable scene. Lemon and cinnamon sugar are both used. The cake is thin and large.

Why it tastes this way

The batter is the Dutch thin pancake, and cinnamon sugar is the spice the Cape kitchen put on it. Cinnamon was a trade spice. The cake itself is mild egg and flour, a carrier. Cooking one large cake and rolling it is why the edge is crisper than the middle. The fete context is the culture: a shared pan, not a single plated dessert.

How it is made

A thin batter is cooked as a large pancake, sugared with cinnamon, and rolled.

Authentic touch. Spread the batter thin in a wide pan and roll the pancake while it is hot so the cinnamon sugar sticks and the cake does not crack.

How it is used

Rolled, warm, in a paper if you are pretending you are at the fete.

Field notes

  • Church fetes and school events are a real home of the South African pannekoek.
  • Cinnamon sugar is the classic sweet finish.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup flour
  • 2 large eggs
  • 1 1/4 cups milk
  • Pinch of salt
  • 1 tablespoon melted butter
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1 teaspoon cinnamon

Steps

  1. Whisk flour, eggs, milk, salt, and butter. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Cook thin large pancakes in a buttered pan.
  3. Mix sugar and cinnamon and sprinkle each pancake.
  4. Roll and serve.
  5. The first one tests the heat.
  6. Eat them the day they are made.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A savory mince filling exists and adds meat.
  • Dutch pannenkoeken are the European source of the method.
