Moroccan baghrir

Spongy semolina cakes, yeasted and sour-sweet, soaked with butter and honey.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · About 12 small pancakes

History

Baghrir are the thousand-hole semolina pancakes of Morocco, raised with yeast rather than with a long teff ferment. The batter is blended smooth and thin, cooked on one side, and covered in holes that drink a sauce of melted butter and honey. The same pancake is also cooked in Algeria and Tunisia. Semolina is wheat, so this is not a gluten-free bread.

Cultural background

Baghrir belong to breakfast and to the hour when guests drink tea. They are sold at stalls and cooked at home in a small pan. The first cake of the batch is the test: if the holes do not open, the pan is too cool or the batter is too thick.

Why it tastes this way

Fine semolina gives a faint grain sweetness that white flour alone does not. Yeast opens the crumb and leaves a mild ferment flavor. Butter and honey run into the holes, so each bite is rich and sweet on a bread that was not sugary in the batter. Cooking only one side keeps the holes intact.

How it is made

Bloom yeast in warm water, then blend it with fine semolina, a little wheat flour, sugar, and salt until the batter is thin and silky. Rest it until foamy. Cook small rounds in a nonstick pan on one side until the surface is all holes and no wet shine remains. Pour warm butter and honey over the stack.

Authentic touch. Blend the batter until it is perfectly smooth and cook it on one side only. The thousand holes are the dish. A flipped baghrir is just a heavy semolina pancake.

How it is used

Baghrir are breakfast or a sweet snack, often with mint tea. They are eaten with a spoon once the butter and honey have soaked in. Savory toppings are a separate habit and not this recipe.

Field notes

  • English descriptions call them thousand-hole pancakes because the surface should be covered in open holes.
  • Semolina is wheat. This recipe contains wheat, dairy, and honey.
  • The batter is yeasted and cooked on one side. It is not a buckwheat crepe.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups fine semolina (about 240 g)
  • 1/2 cup wheat flour
  • 2 teaspoons instant yeast
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
  • 2 1/2 cups warm water
  • 3 tablespoons unsalted butter
  • 3 tablespoons honey

Steps

  1. Blend the semolina, wheat flour, yeast, sugar, salt, and warm water until the batter is completely smooth and about as thin as cream.
  2. Cover and rest for 30 to 45 minutes, until the surface is foamy. Stir once.
  3. Heat a nonstick pan over medium heat. Do not add a pool of oil. Pour a small ladle of batter and let it spread.
  4. Cook on one side until the top is covered with holes and looks dry. Do not flip. Slide the pancake onto a towel.
  5. Melt the butter with the honey and spoon that sauce over the warm pancakes so it runs into the holes.

Semolina and wheat flour both contain gluten. The finish contains dairy and honey.

Eat baghrir soon after the honey goes on. Plain cooked cakes can be refrigerated and reheated, then sauced. Do not leave buttered cakes out. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Orange-blossom water in the honey is a common home perfume.
  • A longer rest makes a tangier cake. A short rest tastes more of plain yeast.
  • Algerian and Tunisian batches follow the same hole-and-honey idea with local pans.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Snack
  • Mint tea
  • More warm honey, if the first spoon was shy
  • A handful of fresh fruit
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Msemen

Flaky, lightly salty layered flatbread with toasted semolina between the folds.

40 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 square flatbreads

History

Msemen is a Moroccan griddled flatbread made by oiling and folding a soft semolina dough into a square. It is a laminated dough, not a pourable batter. Street griddles in Marrakesh turn out stacks for breakfast, plain or filled. The plain bread is the one written here. You will also see the name written m'smen.

Cultural background

Msemen is street food and home food at once. A cook stretches the dough thin, brushes it with oil, folds it, and cooks it on a hot plate until the layers blister. Honey comes at the table for a sweet breakfast. A savory filling is a different batch.

Why it tastes this way

The flavor is toasted wheat and semolina, plus the oil that fries a little between the folds. Salt in the dough keeps it from tasting like pastry. Because the dough is laminated rather than poured, the bite is flaky and chewy at the same time. Honey on top is optional sweetness, not a flavor baked into the square.

How it is made

Mix flour, fine semolina, salt, and water into a soft dough and rest it. Divide it, oil each piece, stretch it paper thin, fold it into a square, and griddle it in a lightly oiled pan until both sides are spotted and the layers separate at the edge.

Authentic touch. Stretch the dough thin enough to see the board through the center before you fold it. Thick folds stay bready. This is laminated dough, not a batter you pour into a pan.

How it is used

Plain msemen is breakfast with honey or with cheese and olives. It is also a street snack eaten from a paper square. Filled versions, including those with preserved meat, are variations and change the diet tags.

Field notes

  • Msemen is a laminated dough. It is not a pourable batter.
  • The usual shape is a square, folded so the layers stay inside.
  • The name is also written m'smen.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups wheat flour (about 250 g)
  • 1/2 cup fine semolina
  • 1 teaspoon fine salt
  • 3/4 cup warm water, plus more if the dough is stiff
  • 1/4 cup neutral oil, plus more for folding and the pan

Steps

  1. Mix the flour, semolina, salt, water, and a spoon of oil into a soft dough. Knead until smooth. Rest it, covered, for 15 minutes.
  2. Divide the dough into 8 pieces and roll each into a ball. Coat the balls with oil so they do not dry.
  3. Oil the board. Stretch one ball into a very thin rectangle. This is laminated dough, not a batter. Brush it with oil and sprinkle on a pinch of semolina.
  4. Fold the sides inward to make a square, then flatten the square gently. Repeat with the rest.
  5. Cook the squares on a hot griddle filmed with oil, turning once, until both sides are blistered and golden and the edge shows layers.

The dough contains wheat. A meat filling is a different recipe and is not vegetarian.

Eat msemen warm. Refrigerate leftovers and reheat them on a dry skillet so the layers crisp again. Do not store them soaked in honey. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Butter or smen, aged butter, can replace the oil. That version contains dairy.
  • A filling of khlii, preserved meat, makes the bread not vegetarian.
  • An onion and spice filling is a savory street version and is still a folded dough.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Street food
  • Snack
  • Honey
  • Olives and fresh cheese
  • Mint tea
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Moroccan harcha

Crumbly semolina pan bread, buttery and lightly salty, with a toasted crust.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 thick rounds

History

Harcha is a Moroccan semolina pan bread, pressed into rounds and cooked on a griddle. It is not a pourable pancake and not a laminated msemen. Coarse or fine semolina is bound with fat and a little milk, then flattened and toasted in a pan until the crumb is tender at the center and crusty at the rim. It belongs to breakfast and to the tea tray.

Cultural background

Home cooks and café counters both keep harcha for the morning. It is cut into wedges and served warm. The texture is closer to a crumbly cake of semolina than to a folded flatbread. Semolina is the point. A wheat-flour pancake will not do this job.

Why it tastes this way

Semolina tastes sweeter and grittier than white flour, and butter carries that grain flavor. A small amount of sugar, if you use it, stays in the background. The griddle browns the surface so the outside is toasted while the inside stays moist and sandy. Milk softens the crumb.

How it is made

Rub melted butter into semolina with salt, a little sugar, and baking powder. Add warm milk until the dough holds together. Shape thick rounds, cook them on a moderate griddle under a loose cover, and turn them once. Slice into wedges.

Authentic touch. Pat the dough into thick disks. Do not try to pour it. Harcha is a pan bread. Cook it slowly enough that the center sets before the crust burns.

How it is used

Harcha is breakfast or an afternoon snack with mint tea, butter, honey, or cheese. It is not a street crepe and not a stew bread. Honey and extra butter at the table add more dairy and honey on top of a bread that already contains dairy.

Field notes

  • Harcha is semolina pan bread, cooked on a griddle in thick rounds.
  • It is not msemen. There are no thin folded layers.
  • Semolina is a form of wheat, so the bread contains gluten.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups fine semolina (about 320 g)
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/2 cup melted unsalted butter
  • 1/2 cup warm milk, plus a spoon more if needed

Steps

  1. Stir the semolina, salt, sugar, and baking powder in a bowl. Pour in the melted butter and rub it through until the grain looks like damp sand.
  2. Add the warm milk and mix to a soft dough that holds a shape. It should not pour.
  3. Divide the dough into 6 pieces and pat each into a round about a finger thick. This is pan bread, not a batter.
  4. Cook the rounds on a moderate griddle, covered loosely, for several minutes a side. Turn once. The crust should spot and the center should set.
  5. Rest them for a minute, then cut into wedges. Serve warm.

Semolina is wheat. The dough contains butter and milk.

Hold leftovers in a covered tin and reheat on a dry pan. Refrigerate if the kitchen is hot. The crumb dries faster than a loaf of bread. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Oil instead of butter makes a dairy-free pan bread. That is not the version written here.
  • A coarser semolina gives a more granular crumb.
  • A pinch of anise is a regional habit. This recipe leaves it out.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Snack
  • Mint tea
  • Honey
  • Soft cheese
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Algerian mahjouba

Thin semolina flatbread folded around a tomato, onion, and chile filling.

40 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 folded squares

History

Mahjouba, also spelled mhadjeb, is an Algerian street bread: a soft semolina dough stretched thin, filled with cooked tomato and onion, folded, and cooked on a griddle. English sometimes calls these stuffed semolina crepes because the sheet is thin. The dough is spread by hand. It is not a thin egg batter poured from a jug. Stalls sell it across Algerian cities, folded into squares you can eat walking.

Cultural background

The filling is the cook's signature: tomato, onion, garlic, cumin, and chile. Heat sits in the filling, not in a dry spice rubbed on afterward. The square is cut or eaten whole from paper. It is a snack and a light meal.

Why it tastes this way

Semolina keeps the wrapper faintly sweet and elastic, so it stands up to a wet filling. The tomato cooks down with onion until it is jammy, and chile plus cumin make it savory rather than like a sauce on pasta. Oil on the griddle toasts the outside of the folds. The center stays soft.

How it is made

Cook onion, garlic, tomato, and chile with cumin and salt until the filling is thick. Mix a soft semolina dough, rest it, and stretch each piece thin on an oiled board. Spread filling in the center, fold into a square, and griddle in a film of oil until both sides are spotted.

Authentic touch. Spread the dough by hand until it is thin and even, then fold the filling inside a square. A poured wheat crepe will tear under this tomato filling. The chile in the filling is the heat.

How it is used

Mahjouba is street food, eaten hot off the griddle. It can be a snack or the savory part of a casual meal. It is not a dessert pancake. Extra harissa at the table pushes the heat higher than this recipe.

Field notes

  • Mahjouba is a stuffed semolina flatbread, spread by hand and folded into a square.
  • You will also see the spelling mhadjeb.
  • The heat is in the tomato and chile filling, not in the dough.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups fine semolina (about 320 g)
  • 1/2 cup wheat flour
  • 1 teaspoon fine salt, divided
  • 1 cup warm water
  • 3 tablespoons oil, plus more for the griddle
  • 2 onions, chopped
  • 2 garlic cloves, chopped
  • 4 ripe tomatoes, chopped
  • 1 fresh chile, chopped
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin

Steps

  1. Cook the onions in a tablespoon of oil until soft. Add the garlic, tomatoes, chile, cumin, and half the salt. Simmer until the filling is thick and not watery.
  2. Mix the semolina, flour, remaining salt, water, and a spoon of oil into a soft dough. Rest it for 15 minutes.
  3. Divide the dough into 6 pieces. Oil the board and stretch each piece by hand into a thin sheet. This is a stuffed flatbread, not a poured crepe batter.
  4. Spread a spoon of filling in the center and fold the sheet into a square, sealing the filling inside.
  5. Griddle the squares in a film of oil, turning once, until both sides are spotted and the dough is cooked through.

The filling is medium heat because of the chile. Wheat is in the semolina and the flour. The pan oil is hot.

Refrigerate filled mahjouba and reheat on a skillet. Do not leave the tomato filling at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A greener filling uses more herbs and less tomato.
  • Some cooks add a spoon of paprika and leave out the fresh chile. That batch is milder.
  • The name mhadjeb is another spelling of the same folded bread.

Pairings and uses

  • Street food
  • Savory meal
  • Snack
  • Harissa on the side, if you want more heat
  • A glass of buttermilk, for drinkers of dairy
  • A simple green salad
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Tunisian mlawi

Soft, oiled, layered round with a flaky edge and a mild wheat taste.

35 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 round flatbreads

History

Mlawi, also written mlaoui, is the layered flatbread of a Tunisian breakfast. Oiled dough is coiled or folded into a round, flattened, and cooked on a griddle. It is a cousin of Moroccan msemen and it is not the same bread: the Tunis version is usually a round breakfast flatbread, eaten with harissa, olive oil, honey, or an egg. This page keeps that shape and that table.

Cultural background

In Tunis, mlawi is early food, sold at stalls and cooked at home before the day heats up. The bread itself is not a chile dough. Heat arrives when someone spoons harissa beside it. A tuna or egg breakfast is a stall habit and adds fish or egg.

Why it tastes this way

Oil between the layers fries them lightly, so the bread tastes toasted and rich without butter. Semolina in the dough gives a fine grit. Because it is coiled into a round and then pressed, the flakes spiral instead of sitting in a Moroccan square. Harissa on the side is sour and hot against a mild crumb.

How it is made

Make a soft dough of flour, semolina, salt, water, and oil. Rest it, stretch it into a ribbon, brush it with olive oil, and coil it into a disk. Flatten the disk and griddle it until puffed in places and golden on both sides.

Authentic touch. Coil the oiled strip into a round, then flatten it. Do not cut the recipe across into msemen squares and rename them. The bread is unspiced. Put harissa on the plate, not in the dough, if you want this version.

How it is used

Mlawi is breakfast and street food. Eat it with olive oil and harissa, with honey, or as a wrap around an egg. Tuna is a common stall addition and would add a fish allergen that this dough does not have.

Field notes

  • Mlawi is a Tunisian layered flatbread, distinct from Moroccan msemen.
  • It is also written mlaoui.
  • The dough is mild. Harissa is served beside it rather than mixed in.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups wheat flour (about 250 g)
  • 1/2 cup fine semolina
  • 1 teaspoon fine salt
  • 3/4 cup warm water
  • 3 tablespoons olive oil, plus more for brushing

Steps

  1. Mix the flour, semolina, salt, water, and a spoon of olive oil into a soft dough. Knead briefly and rest it, covered, for 15 minutes.
  2. Divide the dough into 6 pieces. Stretch one piece into a long thin ribbon and brush it with olive oil.
  3. Coil the ribbon into a round, then press or roll the round flat. This layered round is mlawi, not a square of msemen.
  4. Repeat with the remaining dough, keeping the coils covered so the oil does not dry.
  5. Cook on a hot griddle with a trace of oil, turning once, until both sides are golden and the layers at the edge flake.

The dough contains wheat. Harissa served alongside is hot even though the bread is tagged with no chile heat.

Reheat leftover mlawi on a skillet. Refrigerate it once cool. Olive oil on the surface can go stale if the bread sits out. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • The spelling mlaoui points to the same bread.
  • A square fold is closer to msemen. This recipe stays round.
  • Adding tuna or egg at the stall changes the allergens and the diet tags.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Street food
  • Snack
  • Harissa and olive oil
  • Honey
  • A fried egg, which adds egg
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Egypt · North Africa

Egyptian qatayef

Yeasted pancakes stuffed with cinnamon walnuts and soaked in a lemon-rose syrup.

35 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 8 · About 16 stuffed pancakes

History

Qatayef are the pancakes of Ramadan evenings in Egypt, and they are also a feast sweet in Lebanon, Palestine, and Syria. A yeasted batter is cooked on one side, filled, pinched shut, and fried or baked before a bath of syrup. Walnuts are a classic filling. Cheese is another. The smallest pancakes, asafiri, are left unstuffed and dipped in syrup. The name asafiri refers to little birds. This recipe is the walnut one.

Cultural background

During Ramadan, qatayef move from bakery windows to night streets. At home, one person pours, another fills and pinches. The syrup is cooked first so it can cool. The pancakes must be stuffed while the pale side is still flexible, or the seam splits.

Why it tastes this way

The batter itself is only lightly sweet. The flavor arrives twice: cinnamon and walnut inside, and a lemon-rose syrup outside. Frying toasts the pinched edge. Because the pancake was cooked on one side first, the inside stays soft enough to hold the filling instead of cracking like a cracker.

How it is made

Cook a sugar syrup and cool it. Blend a thin yeast batter and rest it until foamy. Pour small rounds and cook them on one side only. Fill each with a cinnamon walnut mixture, pinch it into a half-moon, and fry until golden. Dip the hot pastries in the cool syrup.

Authentic touch. Cook the pancakes on one side until bubbles cover the top, and fill them before they cool and turn brittle. Walnuts make this batch a tree-nut dish. It is not nut-free. Asafiri, the little unstuffed ones, are a different shape of the same batter.

How it is used

Qatayef are a feast dessert, especially on Ramadan nights, and they are street food in that season. They are not a breakfast stack. Cheese-filled and asafiri versions sit beside this walnut one in the same bakeries.

Field notes

  • Qatayef are the pancakes most associated with Ramadan evenings.
  • Asafiri are the small unstuffed ones. The name refers to little birds.
  • This walnut filling contains tree nuts, so the recipe is not nut-free.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups wheat flour (about 190 g)
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/4 teaspoon fine salt
  • 1 1/2 cups warm water
  • 1 cup finely chopped walnuts
  • 2 tablespoons sugar, for the filling
  • 1 teaspoon ground cinnamon
  • 1 cup sugar, for the syrup
  • 1/2 cup water, for the syrup
  • 1 teaspoon lemon juice
  • 1 teaspoon rose water
  • Oil for shallow frying

Steps

  1. Simmer the syrup sugar and syrup water for 8 minutes, then add the lemon juice and rose water. Cool the syrup completely.
  2. Whisk the flour, the tablespoon of sugar, yeast, baking powder, salt, and warm water into a thin batter. Rest it for 20 minutes, until foamy.
  3. Cook small rounds on a lightly oiled nonstick pan, one side only, until the top is bubbly and set. Do not flip. Cover the pancakes with a towel so they stay flexible.
  4. Mix the walnuts, filling sugar, and cinnamon. Place a spoon on the pale side of each pancake and pinch the edges into a half-moon. This filling contains tree nuts.
  5. Fry the pinched pancakes in shallow oil until golden, drain them, and dip them in the cool syrup while they are hot.

Walnuts are a tree nut. The syrup is very hot while it cooks, and the frying oil is hot. This recipe is a feast sweet, not a nut-free food.

Keep syrup-soaked qatayef covered in the refrigerator and eat them within a couple of days. They soften as they sit. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A white-cheese filling is the dairy version and is not vegan.
  • Asafiri are left small and unstuffed, then dipped in syrup.
  • Baking the pinched pancakes instead of frying dries them a little and uses less oil.

Pairings and uses

  • Feast or fast day
  • Dessert
  • Street food
  • Unsweetened tea
  • A smaller extra dip of the same syrup
  • Chopped pistachios, which are also a tree nut
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Dairy-free

Nigeria · West Africa

Nigerian masa

Mildly sour rice cakes with crisp rims and a soft, slightly sweet center.

30 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 4 · About 16 molded cakes

History

Masa is the Hausa fermented rice cake cooked in a dimpled pan. Waina is another name for it. Rice is soaked, ground, and left to sour, then spooned into oiled cups so each cake takes a round, puffed shape. In Kano and across Hausa kitchens it is breakfast and street food, eaten with sugar, honey, or a soup. This recipe is rice only, so it can be gluten-free.

Cultural background

The pan matters as much as the ferment. Traditional cups are set into a wide clay or metal pan. A home cook can use a heavy dimpled pan if the cups are well oiled. The cake is a snack you buy by the piece as well as a dish made for the house.

Why it tastes this way

Fermentation takes the raw starch edge off the rice and leaves a light sourness. A little sugar in the batter keeps it in breakfast territory. The oiled mold fries the rim while the covered center stays tender. It does not taste like a wheat pancake, because there is no wheat.

How it is made

Soak rice, grind it with water into a thick batter, and ferment it until slightly sour. Stir in salt, sugar, and a pinch of yeast if the kitchen is too cool for a wild ferment. Oil the dimples of a masa pan and cook spoonfuls, turning once, until both sides are golden.

Authentic touch. Use a dimpled pan and oil the cups. Waina and masa are the same family of cakes. Do not add wheat flour if you are keeping the gluten-free tag.

How it is used

Masa is breakfast and a street snack. Sugar or honey goes on top, or the cakes are eaten with miyan taushe, a pumpkin soup, or another Hausa soup. Honey and soup are not in the batter.

Field notes

  • Waina is another name for these Hausa rice cakes.
  • They are cooked in a dimpled pan, not poured as a wide flatbread.
  • A rice-only batter can be gluten-free if the yeast and the pan are not carrying wheat.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups raw rice (about 400 g), soaked 4 hours and drained
  • 1 1/2 cups water, for grinding
  • 1/2 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
  • Oil for the dimples

Steps

  1. Grind the drained rice with the water to a thick, slightly grainy batter. Stir in the yeast and sugar. Leave the salt out.
  2. Cover and rest the batter for 4 to 8 hours, until it looks bubbly and smells faintly sour. This is a fermented rice batter.
  3. Stir in the salt. The batter should drop from a spoon in a heavy ribbon. Thin it with a spoon of water if it stands up in peaks.
  4. Oil the cups of a dimpled masa pan and heat them until the oil shimmers. Fill each cup about two-thirds full.
  5. Cover and cook until the bottoms are golden, then turn each cake and cook the second side. Serve warm. Waina is the same cake under another name.

Gluten-free only if the rice, yeast, and pan have no wheat on them. Hot oil in the dimples can spit.

Refrigerate leftover masa and reheat in a lightly oiled pan. The rims soften in a covered box. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Waina is another name, not a different grain.
  • A wild ferment without added yeast takes longer and tastes a little sharper.
  • Wheat flour stirred in for softness removes the gluten-free claim.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Snack
  • Street food
  • A sprinkle of sugar
  • Honey
  • Miyan taushe, pumpkin soup
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Ghana · Mild · About 12 fritters

Flying Pancakes of the World

Ghana · West Africa

Ghanaian tatale

Sweet ripe plantain fritters with ginger and a mild chile warmth, fried until the edges crisp.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · About 12 fritters

History

Tatale are ripe plantain cakes from Ghana. Very ripe plantain is mashed with ginger, onion, and chile, bound with a little flour, and fried. They are a snack and a street food, not a breakfast stack poured on a dry griddle. The method here is frying, which is how they are often cooked. The chile and ginger put the heat at mild.

Cultural background

Black-spotted plantains are the right fruit. Green plantain is a different, starchy dish. Tatale show up at home and at stalls, eaten hot, sometimes with beans and sometimes on their own. The spice paste is pounded or grated into the mash.

Why it tastes this way

Ripe plantain brings banana-like sweetness and a soft mash. Frying in oil browns the outside and keeps the center custardy. Ginger is floral and warm. A small chile adds mild heat without covering the fruit. Wheat flour only holds the mash together. It is not the flavor.

How it is made

Mash very ripe plantains. Stir in grated ginger, chopped onion, a mild chile, salt, and enough wheat flour to make a thick paste. Fry spoonfuls in oil until deep golden on both sides. Drain and eat warm.

Authentic touch. Use plantains that have gone soft and sweet, and fry the mash. A dry-skillet pancake made from green plantain is not tatale. The ginger and chile are part of the mash, not a sauce.

How it is used

Tatale are a snack or street food, hot from the oil. They can sit beside beans. They are not a dessert with syrup, though the plantain is sweet. This version is mild, not extra-hot.

Field notes

  • Tatale are fried ripe-plantain cakes. This recipe fries them.
  • Ginger and a little chile make the heat mild.
  • Green plantain will not give the sweet mash this dish needs.

Ingredients

  • 3 very ripe plantains, peeled and mashed
  • 1 tablespoon grated fresh ginger
  • 1 small onion, finely chopped
  • 1 mild fresh chile, seeded and chopped
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
  • 1/2 cup wheat flour
  • Oil for shallow frying

Steps

  1. Mash the ripe plantains until mostly smooth, with a few soft lumps left.
  2. Stir in the ginger, onion, chile, salt, and wheat flour. The paste should hold a spoonful. The chile keeps the heat mild.
  3. Heat a finger's depth of oil in a wide pan until a drop of mash sizzles at once.
  4. Fry spoonfuls, turning once, until both sides are deep golden. Do not crowd the pan. This method is frying, not a dry griddle.
  5. Drain on a rack and eat while hot.

Hot oil is the cooking fat. The dish contains wheat and a mild chile. Do not leave the pan unattended.

Tatale are best the hour they are fried. Refrigerate leftovers and reheat in a hot pan. They will not be as crisp the next day. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Injera

Sour, spongy teff bread with an earthy crumb and open holes on top.

25 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 6 · About 8 large rounds

History

Teff, Eragrostis tef, was domesticated in the Horn of Africa, and injera is the fermented flatbread made from it. A teff batter is left to sour, then cooked on one side on a mitad, the clay griddle. The bread covers a platter and is torn to pinch stews. This recipe is 100 percent teff. Wheat blends used by some bakeries are a different loaf and are not in this method.

Cultural background

Injera is both the starch and the utensil at a shared Ethiopian meal. Guests tear pieces and use them to pick up wat. The sourness is expected. A fresh injera should bend and hold a stew without cracking into dust.

Why it tastes this way

Two to three days of fermentation feed lactic acid bacteria and wild yeast, so the bread tastes sour rather than sweet. Teff itself is earthy and slightly nutty. On the hot mitad the bubbles set into ayen, the eyes. The top stays soft because the bread is never flipped, and steam under a cloth finishes it so it can soak up sauce.

How it is made

Whisk teff flour and water into a batter like heavy cream and ferment it, covered, for 2 to 3 days until sour and bubbly. Salt it only after that rest. Pour it in a spiral on a hot mitad or a wide nonstick skillet, cover, and cook on one side until the ayen are open and the top is matte. Do not flip. Stack the rounds in a cloth so they steam and soften.

Authentic touch. Cook on one side only and leave the ayen open. Do not flip. If the eyes close under a second side of heat, the surface toughens and stops drinking the stew.

How it is used

Injera lines a platter under meats and vegetable stews, or under a fasting spread of lentils and greens. It is the accompaniment to the meal, not a side dish in a separate basket. Leftover bread is torn into firfir the next day.

Field notes

  • The holes on the surface are called ayen, the Amharic word for eyes.
  • A mitad is the round clay griddle used to cook injera on one side.
  • One hundred percent teff injera is gluten-free only if the flour was not milled with wheat.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups teff flour, ivory or brown (about 280 g)
  • 3 cups water, plus more for thinning
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
  • A few drops of neutral oil, only if the pan sticks

Steps

  1. Whisk the teff flour and water into a smooth batter about as thick as heavy cream. There is no wheat in this recipe. Cover the bowl.
  2. Leave the batter at room temperature for 2 to 3 days, until it smells sour and looks bubbly. Stir it once a day. This is the ferment. Do not add baking powder.
  3. Stir in the salt only after the ferment. Add a spoon of water if the batter is thicker than pourable cream.
  4. Heat a mitad or a wide nonstick skillet over medium heat. Wipe it with a few drops of oil only if the surface sticks. Pour the batter in a spiral and tilt the pan into a large thin round.
  5. Cover and cook on one side until the top is set and the ayen, the eyes, are open. Do not flip. Move the bread to a clean cloth and let the steam soften it while you cook the rest.

This loaf is gluten-free only when the teff flour has not been milled or stored with wheat. A shared griddle can transfer wheat flour.

Wrap cooked injera in a clean cloth for the day, then refrigerate and reheat in a covered pan. Keep sour batter refrigerated if you are not cooking it the same day. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Ivory teff makes a paler injera with the same ferment.
  • A spoon of saved batter from a previous batch can start the next one.
  • Adding wheat flour is a bakery shortcut. It is not this recipe, and it removes the gluten-free diet tag.

Eritrean taita

Sour teff flatbread with a spongy top, meant to carry tsebhi.

20 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 6 · About 8 large sheets

History

In Eritrea the fermented teff bread is called taita. It belongs to the same teff family as Ethiopian injera and uses the same grain, but the meal around it is organized as tsebhi, the Tigrinya name for the stews. Zigni is a berbere stew, alicha is the milder one, hamli is greens, and shiro is a paste of ground chickpeas or broad beans. This page is that Asmara bread, not a second copy of the Ethiopian platter.

Cultural background

Taita is torn at the table and used to scoop tsebhi from a shared tray. The bread is the plate. Bakeries in Asmara sometimes blend wheat into the batter for a paler, less fragile sheet. This home recipe does not. It stays with teff.

Why it tastes this way

The sourness comes from a teff batter that has had time to ferment, and the earthy taste is the grain itself. Cooking on one side leaves a soft, holey top that soaks up a rich tsebhi. A wheat blend tastes milder and tighter. This version keeps the sharper teff flavor because nothing else is in the flour.

How it is made

Mix teff flour and water, cover the bowl, and let it sour until bubbly. Salt it at the end. Cook thin rounds on a mogogo, the Tigrinya clay griddle, or on a wide nonstick pan. Cover each round and cook one side until the surface is set and full of holes. Stack the sheets in a cloth. The stews are cooked apart from the bread.

Authentic touch. Serve the bread with at least two tsebhi, one hot with berbere and one milder, so the same piece of taita tastes different from bite to bite. Do not flip the bread.

How it is used

Taita is the base of an Eritrean meal: a tray of tsebhi zigni, hamli, and shiro, with the bread torn as the utensil. It is an accompaniment, not a rolled snack. The stews can be meat or fasting dishes. The bread recipe itself has neither.

Field notes

  • Taita is the Tigrinya name for this teff bread. The stews beside it are tsebhi.
  • The clay griddle is a mogogo. The bread cooks on one side.
  • South of the city, the same teff batter is still the everyday starch, with the stew changing by season.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups teff flour (about 280 g)
  • 3 1/4 cups water
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
  • A few drops of neutral oil if the griddle sticks

Steps

  1. Stir the teff flour and water into a lump-free batter a little thinner than pancake batter. Cover it. Do not add wheat flour.
  2. Leave it at cool room temperature until it is sour and foamy, usually two or three days. Stir once each day.
  3. Add the salt. Thin with a splash of water if the batter no longer pours in a ribbon.
  4. Heat a mogogo or a wide nonstick skillet. Spread a ladle of batter from the outside inward into a round that reaches the edges.
  5. Cover and cook until the top changes from glossy to set and the holes stay open. Do not flip. Lay each sheet on a cloth and cover it while the next one cooks.

Gluten-free only if the teff was not milled with wheat. Berbere stews served alongside can be very hot even though the bread is not.

Keep taita wrapped so it does not dry out. Refrigerate leftovers and warm them in a covered pan. Do not leave a meat stew on the bread at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of an older sour batter makes the next batch more reliable.
  • Some Asmara bakeries add wheat. That bread is not gluten-free and is not written here.
  • Brown teff gives a darker sheet than ivory teff. Both are taita.

Ethiopian kita

Mild, soft teff flatbread with a light toasted face and no sour ferment.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · About 8 small rounds

History

Kita is a quick unfermented flatbread, cooked the day it is mixed. It is not injera. Injera needs days of souring and is cooked on one side. Kita is mixed, rested only long enough for the flour to drink the water, and cooked on a skillet. Torn kita becomes chechebsa, tossed with spiced butter and berbere, or kita firfir, soaked in a spiced sauce. This recipe uses teff and stays unfermented.

Cultural background

Kita is a breakfast and a way to use a hot pan when there is no sour batter waiting. In home kitchens it is torn by hand. The bread itself is plain. The chile and the spiced butter arrive when it is turned into chechebsa or firfir.

Why it tastes this way

Because the batter is unfermented, kita tastes of toasted teff rather than of lactic sourness. There are no ayen. The crumb is closer to a soft griddled flatbread than to a sponge. If it sits for days it will start to become something else, which is why this method cooks it the same morning.

How it is made

Stir teff flour, water, and salt into a thick batter and rest it for about ten minutes so the flour hydrates. Do not ferment it. Cook small rounds on a moderate skillet, flipping once, until both faces are set and lightly spotted. Tear the bread while it is warm if you are making chechebsa or kita firfir.

Authentic touch. Say it plainly while you cook: this dough is unfermented. Tear the finished bread for chechebsa or kita firfir instead of leaving it as a neat stack. A fermented sour loaf is injera, and it does not belong on this page.

How it is used

Plain kita is breakfast with tea. Torn and mixed with niter kibbeh, the spiced clarified butter, and berbere, it becomes chechebsa. Torn into a berbere sauce, it becomes kita firfir. Those finishes add dairy and chile. They are not in the bread.

Field notes

  • Kita is unfermented. Leaving the batter for days would turn the method into a different bread.
  • Chechebsa is torn kita tossed with spiced butter and berbere.
  • Kita firfir is the torn bread soaked in a spiced sauce rather than folded.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups teff flour (about 210 g)
  • 1 1/2 cups water
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
  • 1 teaspoon neutral oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Stir the teff flour, water, and salt into a thick batter with no dry pockets. This dough is unfermented. Do not add yeast or sourdough.
  2. Rest it for 10 minutes so the flour hydrates. Do not leave it overnight.
  3. Heat a skillet over medium heat and wipe it with the oil. Spread a small ladle of batter into a round about the width of a saucer.
  4. Cook until the underside is set and spotted, then flip once and cook the second side. Kita is cooked on both sides, unlike injera.
  5. Stack the rounds under a cloth. Tear them for chechebsa or kita firfir, or eat them plain while warm.

The bread is gluten-free only if the teff flour is free of wheat. Berbere used later for chechebsa is a chile blend and can be very hot.

Eat kita the day it is cooked. Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Do not ferment leftover batter and call it this recipe. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Wheat kita is common and must be tagged as wheat. It is not gluten-free. This written recipe does not use it.
  • Chechebsa uses spiced butter, so that version is not vegan or dairy-free.
  • A drier dough makes a firmer bread for tearing. A looser batter makes a softer round.

Somali canjeero

Soft, lightly sour wheat cakes full of small holes, mildly sweet at the edge.

History

Canjeero is the spongy fermented pancake of a Somali breakfast. The same bread is also called laxoox and anjero. Those are names for this cake, not three different recipes on this page. Cultural background

Breakfast can be a stack of canjeero, a spoon of subag, which is ghee, and tea. Some houses prefer honey. Others set the bread next to a thin soup or a bit of stewed meat. The batter is a home ferment, often helped with a pinch of yeast when the kitchen is cool.

Why it tastes this way

An overnight ferment gives a gentle sourness, and a little sugar keeps it from tasting like injera. Wheat makes the crumb softer and less earthy than teff. The holes hold ghee or honey. A soup-soaked piece tastes savory because of the broth, not because the batter was seasoned like a stew.

How it is made

Whisk wheat flour with warm water, a little sugar, and a pinch of yeast. Rest it overnight until bubbly and slightly sour, then salt it. Cook small rounds in a lightly oiled pan on one side until the top is spongy and set. Serve warm. The ghee, honey, and soup stay at the table, not in the batter.

Authentic touch. Cook one side and wait until the holes cover the surface before you lift the cake. A flip makes it bready. Keep the Somali breakfast toppings, ghee, honey, or soup, and do not dress this page as Yemeni lahoh.

How it is used

Canjeero is breakfast. A common plate is the bread with ghee and honey, or the bread torn into a light soup. It is not the liner under a large platter of stews. Sesame oil is another home finish.

Field notes

  5. Cover and cook on one side until the holes set and the top is no longer wet. Do not flip. Repeat with the rest of the batter and serve warm.

Stir sorghum flour into water and ferment it until sour, salting only at the end. On a hot flat griddle, spread a ladle of batter in a quick thin sweep and cook it on one side until it peels free. Fold the sheets and keep them covered so they stay pliable for scooping stew.

How it is used

Kisra is the utensil for Sudanese stews. Okra mullah and other vegetable or meat stews sit beside a stack of folded sheets. The bread on this page has no meat in it. South Sudanese tables use the same kind of sheet.

Ugandan kabalagala

Sweet fried cakes of ripe banana and wheat flour, crisp outside and soft within.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · About 12 small cakes

History

Kabalagala are ripe banana street cakes in Uganda, sold around Kampala and cooked at home. Street versions are often fried, and this recipe is fried. Many street cooks bind the mash with cassava flour. This recipe uses wheat flour instead and does not use cassava. It does not describe how to process cassava. The flavor is the small sweet banana, mashed and fried in rounds.

Cultural background

The bananas for kabalagala are ripe and aromatic, not green cooking bananas. Vendors fry batches through the day and sell them as a snack. At home the same mash is a way to use a bunch that has ripened all at once.

Why it tastes this way

Ripe banana sweetens the dough without much added sugar. Frying turns the surface brown and slightly caramelized while the center stays moist. Wheat flour makes the crumb a little bready compared with a pure fruit mash. There is no chile and no savory spice in this version.

How it is made

Mash very ripe bananas, stir in wheat flour, a pinch of salt, and a little sugar until you have a soft dough. Shape small patties and fry them in oil until golden on both sides. Drain and eat them warm.

Authentic touch. Fry the cakes. A baked disk is not the street method. Use wheat flour as written. Do not substitute raw cassava, and do not look to this page for a cassava process.

How it is used

Kabalagala are street snacks, eaten hot from a paper bag or a rack. They can accompany tea. They are sweet, not a stew starch. This home batch uses the fried street method.

Field notes

  • Street kabalagala are often fried, and this recipe fries them.
  • The binder here is wheat flour, not cassava.
  • Use ripe sweet bananas. Green bananas will not mash into the same cake.

Ingredients

  • 4 very ripe bananas, mashed
  • 1 cup wheat flour (about 125 g)
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon fine salt
  • Oil for frying

Steps

  1. Mash the ripe bananas until soft and mostly smooth.
  2. Stir in the wheat flour, sugar, and salt to make a soft dough you can shape. There is no cassava in this recipe.
  3. Form small patties with wet hands.
  4. Fry the patties in hot oil, turning once, until both sides are golden and the center is cooked. Street versions are often fried, and this is that method.
  5. Drain and serve warm.

The dough contains wheat. Frying oil is hot. This page does not use cassava and does not explain cassava processing.

Eat kabalagala the day they are fried. Refrigerate leftovers and reheat them in a pan. The banana crumb does not keep a crisp crust. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Very ripe bananas need less sugar. Greener fruit will taste starchy and are the wrong bunch.
  • A spoon of ground cardamom is optional and not required for the street cake.
  • Cassava flour is what some stalls use. It is not in this recipe, and this page gives no cassava method.

Pairings and uses

  • Street food
  • Snack
  • Tea
  • A little extra sugar on the hottest cakes
  • Fresh passion fruit on the side

Madagascar · East Africa

Mofo gasy

Small, sweet rice cakes with a yeasted crumb and a browned top.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · About 12 small cakes

History

Mofo gasy means Malagasy bread, and on a morning street in Antananarivo it is a small sweet cake of rice flour, sold by the piece with coffee. The batter is yeasted, sweetened, and cooked in small rounds. Rice is the grain on this page. A wheat version exists in some pans and is not the one written here. Madagascar sits in East Africa on this map.

Cultural background

Vendors cook mofo gasy early, because it is breakfast. The cakes are thick for their width, not crepes. People eat them walking to work. The sweetness is in the batter. There is no savory filling.

Why it tastes this way

Rice flour tastes mild and slightly grassy, and sugar turns that mildness into a breakfast cake. Yeast gives a soft dome and a faint fermented smell, closer to a little brioche than to a sour injera. The pan browns the bottom, which is the bitter-sweet edge against the sweet crumb.

How it is made

Mix rice flour, sugar, yeast, salt, and water into a thick batter. Rest it until it doubles and looks bubbly. Cook small rounds on a lightly oiled griddle, covered, until the bottoms brown and the centers set. Serve them warm with coffee.

Authentic touch. Keep the cakes small and sweet, and cook them in batches the way a street pan does. A large unsweetened rice pancake is a different food. This version stays with rice flour.

How it is used

Mofo gasy is street breakfast and a sweet snack. Coffee is the usual partner. It is not a stew bread and not a dessert with sauce, though it is sweet.

Field notes

  • The name mofo gasy means Malagasy bread.
  • Street vendors sell the sweet cakes in the morning.
  • This recipe uses rice flour and is gluten-free if the flour is pure.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups rice flour (about 260 g)
  • 1/3 cup sugar
  • 1 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1/4 teaspoon fine salt
  • 1 1/2 cups warm water
  • Oil for the griddle

Steps

  1. Stir the rice flour, sugar, yeast, salt, and warm water into a thick batter. Cover the bowl.
  2. Leave it until it is bubbly and visibly risen, about 45 to 75 minutes depending on the room.
  3. Heat a griddle over medium heat and wipe it with oil. Drop small rounds, about a spoon each, leaving space between them.
  4. Cover and cook until the bottoms are brown and the tops are set. Flip once if the top is still wet, and cook briefly.
  5. Serve warm. These are the small sweet street cakes, not a savory rice crepe.

Gluten-free only if the rice flour has no wheat in it. The sugar level makes this a sweet breakfast, which matters if you are limiting sugar.

Eat mofo gasy the morning they are cooked. Refrigerate leftovers and reheat them covered. They dry out quickly. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A wheat-flour mofo gasy is softer and is not gluten-free.
  • More sugar makes a cake closer to a sweet bun. Less sugar tastes grainier.
  • A spoon of mashed banana moistens the batter and changes the flavor.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Street food
  • Snack
  • Coffee
  • A second cake, because they are small
  • Fresh pineapple

South African pannekoek

Thin wheat pancakes rolled around cinnamon sugar, with a buttery edge.

15 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · About 8 thin pancakes

History

Pannekoek is the thin cinnamon-sugar pancake of South African homes and market stalls. The name is the Dutch and Afrikaans word for pancake, and the cake is a thin batter cooked in a wide pan, then rolled. In South Africa and in Britain, the word flapjack can mean a baked oat bar. This page is the thin pannekoek, not that bar. Market stands and church fetes still cook them to order.

Cultural background

A pannekoek stall is a griddle, a jug of batter, and a shaker of cinnamon sugar. At home the same batter is a weekend pan. The cake is thin enough to roll. It is a national snack, not a single city's secret, even though the Dutch name has a Cape history.

Why it tastes this way

Egg and milk make a flexible sheet that browns in spots. There is little baking powder, so the pancake stays flat instead of rising into a pikelet. Cinnamon sugar melts against the hot bread and tastes toasted rather than like a raw spice. Lemon is a sharper finish some cooks prefer. This recipe stays with cinnamon sugar.

How it is made

Rest a thin batter of flour, milk, egg, and a spoon of melted butter. Cook wide pancakes in a buttered pan, one at a time, until spotted. Sprinkle each with cinnamon sugar and roll it. Keep the rolls warm under a cloth.

Authentic touch. Cook the batter thin and roll it around cinnamon sugar while it is hot. If you bake a tray of oats and syrup, you have made the other flapjack, the oat bar, and not this pannekoek.

How it is used

Pannekoek are a snack, a dessert, and stall food. They are eaten by hand once rolled. They can be a weekend treat rather than a daily bread. Ice cream is a later fairground addition and not required.

Field notes

  • In South Africa and Britain, flapjack can mean a baked oat bar. This page is the thin pannekoek.
  • The cake is rolled around cinnamon sugar while it is hot.
  • The name is Dutch and Afrikaans for pancake.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup wheat flour (about 125 g)
  • 1 1/4 cups milk
  • 1 egg
  • 1 tablespoon melted unsalted butter, plus more for the pan
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon fine salt
  • 2 tablespoons sugar mixed with 1 teaspoon ground cinnamon

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, milk, egg, melted butter, sugar, and salt into a thin batter. Rest it for 15 minutes.
  2. Heat a wide pan, add a little butter, and pour enough batter to coat the bottom in a thin sheet. This is a thin pannekoek, not an oat bar.
  3. Cook until the top looks set and the underside is spotted, then flip once and cook briefly.
  4. Sprinkle cinnamon sugar over the hot pancake and roll it up.
  5. Repeat with the rest of the batter. Serve the rolls warm.

The batter contains wheat, egg, and dairy. Cinnamon sugar is sweet. This is not the baked oat flapjack.

Rolled pannekoek are best fresh. Refrigerate leftovers and reheat them covered. The sugar will weep a little. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Lemon and sugar instead of cinnamon is the other classic finish.
  • A savory mince filling exists at some stalls and is a different dish.
  • Whole wheat in part of the flour makes a heartier cake and still contains wheat.

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Snack
  • Street food
  • More cinnamon sugar
  • Lemon wedges, if you want the sharper finish
  • Rooibos tea
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free
