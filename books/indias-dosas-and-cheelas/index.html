Plain dosa

Crisp at the edge, faintly sour, with a toasted rice smell and a soft underside.

40 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 dosas

History

Dosa is old in South India. The exact origin century is not settled, so this page does not name one. What the kitchens agree on is the batter: raw rice and skinned black gram, soaked, ground, salted, and fermented, then spread on a hot griddle. Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, and Kerala all keep that ferment and change the sides.

Cultural background

A home batter is judged by smell and bubbles. A warm night may finish it by morning. A cool kitchen needs more time near the stove. The sourness should stay gentle. Harsh smell or visible mold means the bowl is discarded.

Why it tastes this way

Rice supplies the starch that dries against iron and turns crisp. Urad dal brings protein and a soft stickiness so the sheet can spread without tearing. Fermentation adds a mild tang and the gas that opens lacy holes at the rim. A few fenugreek seeds, traditional in many batters, leave a faint bitter aroma under the toast.

How it is made

Soak rice and urad separately, grind them to different textures, ferment until the batter is lively, and cook thin rounds on a lightly oiled griddle.

Authentic touch. Spread from the center while the batter still flows. A pan that is too hot tears the sheet. A pan that is too cool glues it down and never crisps.

How it is used

Eat it at once with coconut chutney and sambar. A covered dosa steams itself and loses the crisp edge.

Field notes

  • Skinned black gram is the urad dal in this batter, sometimes sold as white urad.
  • The same fermented batter can be steamed as idli. The griddle is what makes the dosa.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup idli rice or other short-grain raw rice
  • 1/3 cup skinned urad dal (black gram)
  • 1/4 teaspoon fenugreek seeds
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
  • Water for soaking and grinding
  • Neutral oil for the griddle

Steps

  1. Rinse the rice. Rinse the urad with the fenugreek. Soak them in separate bowls for 4 to 6 hours.
  2. Drain and grind the urad with cold water to a light, fluffy paste. Grind the rice with water to a slightly grainy batter. Stir them together with the salt. The mix should thickly coat a spoon.
  3. Cover the bowl and ferment until it is bubbly and mildly sour, often 8 to 14 hours. In a cool room give it longer. This wait is not part of the active prep time. Discard the batter if it smells putrid or grows mold.
  4. Stir the batter down and loosen it with a spoon or two of water if it is too stiff to spread. Heat a griddle until a drop of water skitters. Grease it lightly.
  5. Pour a ladle in the center and spiral outward. Drizzle a little oil around the edge. Cook until the underside is spotted gold and the top looks dry. Fold and serve immediately.

Ferment in a clean bowl. Discard batter that smells rotten, turns an odd color, or shows mold.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Ghee on the rim instead of oil
  • A spoon of cooked rice in the rice grind for a softer crumb

Masala dosa

A fermented rice-and-urad sheet wrapped around a turmeric potato filling, with curry leaf and green chile.

45 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 4 · 6 stuffed dosas

History

Masala dosa uses the same fermented rice and urad batter as a plain dosa. The potato masala is a filling, not a second grain and not a different ferment. Restaurant kitchens in Karnataka, including Bengaluru's hotel dosas, made the potato-stuffed fold a city breakfast. The filling itself is a home potato palya that already belonged on the plate beside a plain dosa.

Cultural background

The potato should be dry enough to sit inside the roll without soaking the crisp side. Onion cooked in oil with mustard seed, curry leaf, and turmeric is the usual base. A wet curry would belong in a bowl, not in the fold.

Why it tastes this way

The batter still tastes of fermented rice and urad. The masala adds an earthy, lightly bitter turmeric note, the sweetness of onion, and the pop of mustard seed. Because the potato is spiced and then wrapped, each bite mixes a sour crisp sheet with a soft, salted center. The green chile is there for aroma more than burn.

How it is made

Ferment a rice and urad batter, cook a dry spiced potato, spread a thin dosa, lay the potato in a line, and roll.

Authentic touch. Spread the potato in a strip on the set dosa, not in a tall mound. Too much filling cracks the sheet before it can be rolled.

How it is used

Cut the roll only if you must. Most tables eat it with coconut chutney and sambar, starting at the crisp tail.

Field notes

  • The batter is still rice and urad. Potato never replaces either grain.
  • A masala dosa that sits too long steams the crust from the inside out.

Ingredients

  • 1 batch fermented rice and urad dosa batter, about 3 cups
  • 3 medium potatoes, boiled, peeled, and crumbled
  • 1 small onion, sliced
  • 1 green chile, slit
  • 1/2 teaspoon mustard seeds
  • 8 curry leaves
  • 1/4 teaspoon turmeric
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil, plus more for the griddle
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt, or to taste

Steps

  1. Use a bubbly fermented rice and urad batter, ground and rested as for a plain dosa. Active prep here is the filling and the cooking, not a second grind.
  2. Heat 2 tablespoons of oil. Add mustard seeds. When they pop, add curry leaves, onion, and green chile. Cook until the onion softens.
  3. Add turmeric and the crumbled potato. Season with salt. Mash lightly and cook until the mixture looks dry. Cool a little.
  4. Heat a griddle, grease it, and spread a ladle of batter in a thin round. Oil the edge and cook until the underside is golden.
  5. Lay a strip of potato across the center. Fold the dosa over the filling. Serve at once with chutney.

Use batter that smells pleasantly sour. Discard it if it is moldy or putrid.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of ghee on the potato, which adds dairy
  • A little cooked green pea in the masala

Rava dosa

A lacy, cracker-crisp semolina sheet scented with cumin, black pepper, and curry leaf.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 crisp dosas

History

Rava dosa is a semolina batter, usually stirred and cooked without the long rice-and-urad ferment. Wheat semolina, often called rava or sooji, is the grain that builds the crisp web. Rice flour is there to help the sheet set. It became a restaurant standard because a cook can mix it to order when a fermented batter has run out. It is a different dosa from the fermented rice and urad kind.

Cultural background

The batter is meant to be thin and a little lumpy. Those lumps and the water around them are what leave the holes. A thick batter makes a pancake, which is not the point of this page.

Why it tastes this way

Semolina browns faster than a wet fermented rice batter and tastes toasted and wheat-y. Cumin and crushed black pepper sit on the surface instead of disappearing into a ferment. Curry leaf, dropped into hot oil or onto the batter, adds a sharp green note. The lack of a sour ferment is the flavor clue that this is rava dosa.

How it is made

Whisk semolina, rice flour, and water into a thin batter, season it, and pour it loosely onto a hot griddle so it laces.

Authentic touch. Pour the thin batter from the ladle in separate streams and let the holes stay open. Do not spiral it into a solid disk.

How it is used

Lift it off while it is still brittle. Coconut chutney is the usual partner. It softens if it waits in a stack.

Field notes

  • Rava here is wheat semolina, so this dosa is not gluten-free.
  • The lacy holes are a pouring method. They are not a sign of fermentation.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup fine wheat semolina (rava or sooji)
  • 1/3 cup rice flour
  • 1 1/2 cups cold water, plus more if needed
  • 1/2 teaspoon cumin seeds
  • 1/4 teaspoon crushed black pepper
  • 1 green chile, finely chopped
  • 8 curry leaves, torn
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Neutral oil for the griddle

Steps

  1. Whisk semolina, rice flour, salt, and cold water into a thin batter. Rest it 15 minutes so the semolina hydrates. It should stay pourable.
  2. Stir in cumin, pepper, green chile, and curry leaves. Thin with a splash of water if it has thickened.
  3. Heat a griddle until very hot. Grease it lightly and pour the batter in separate ribbons rather than one tight spiral.
  4. Fill obvious gaps with a little more batter, then drizzle oil around the lace. Cook until the sheet is golden and rigid.
  5. Loosen the edge and lift the dosa off. Cook the rest, stirring the batter each time so the spices do not sink.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A few cumin seeds extra on the surface
  • Buttermilk in place of part of the water, which adds dairy

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Snack
  • Coconut chutney
  • Allam-style ginger chutney
  • Sambar

Onion rava dosa

Crisp semolina lace crowded with sweet onion, green chile, and curry leaf.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 onion dosas

History

Onion rava dosa keeps the unfermented semolina batter and puts onion in the sheet itself. Udupi hotel kitchens, which sent many South Indian breakfasts to other cities, treat it as its own dosa on the board, next to a plain rava dosa. The onion is mixed through or scattered on the pour so it fries into the lace. It is still wheat semolina, not a fermented rice and urad dosa with onions on the side.

Cultural background

Cut the onion small enough to cook in the time the lace crisps. Large raw chunks steam and leave pale pockets. A pinch of ginger is common in Udupi batters and keeps the onion from tasting flat.

Why it tastes this way

Onion fries in the oil at the edge of the batter and turns sweet. Semolina still supplies the wheat toast and the brittle holes. Chile and curry leaf cut that sweetness. Because there is no overnight souring, the onion stays bright instead of fading into a fermented tang.

How it is made

Mix a thin semolina and rice-flour batter, fold in plenty of onion, and pour it loosely on a hot griddle.

Authentic touch. Scatter the onion as you pour, then leave the pan alone until the rim lifts. Stirring after that pulls the lace apart.

How it is used

Serve open-faced so the onion stays visible, with coconut chutney. Folding is fine once the sheet is crisp.

Field notes

  • Onion rava dosa is still a semolina batter. The onion does not change the grain.
  • Udupi restaurants often list it apart from both plain dosa and plain rava dosa.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup fine wheat semolina
  • 1/3 cup rice flour
  • 1 1/2 cups cold water
  • 1 medium onion, finely chopped
  • 1 green chile, minced
  • 1 teaspoon grated ginger
  • 10 curry leaves, chopped
  • 1/2 teaspoon cumin seeds
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Neutral oil for the griddle

Steps

  1. Stir semolina, rice flour, salt, and water until smooth and thin. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Fold in onion, chile, ginger, curry leaves, and cumin. The batter should look busy with onion and still pour from a ladle.
  3. Heat a griddle well. Oil it. Pour the batter in ribbons so holes remain, carrying onion along with each pour.
  4. Add extra onion to any bare spot. Drizzle oil and cook until the onion browns and the sheet snaps at the edge.
  5. Remove it whole. Stir the bowl before the next ladle so the onion does not settle at the bottom.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A teaspoon of grated ginger in the batter
  • A few cashews on one dosa, which adds tree nut

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • Coconut chutney
  • Sambar
  • A small bowl of sugar and ghee for people who want a sweet bite
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Neer dosa

A soft, pale, almost translucent rice roll with a clean cereal taste and no sour ferment.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 10 soft dosas

History

Neer dosa belongs to Mangaluru and the Tulu coast of Karnataka. Neer means water, which matches the batter: soaked rice ground with enough water to look like thin rice milk. No fermentation is required. The dosa is cooked quickly, stays flexible, and is rolled rather than crisped into a wafer. It is a coastal rice bread, separate from the fermented rice-and-urad dosa of the inland hotel menu.

Cultural background

Tulu and other coastal kitchens serve it with coconut chutney, a chicken or fish curry, or a simple scrape of jaggery and grated coconut. This recipe keeps the dosa itself free of fish and meat so the tags match the written batter.

Why it tastes this way

Without urad and without a ferment, there is no tang and no lacy wheat crust. Freshly ground rice tastes sweet and mild. The extra water lets the sheet set thin and pliable instead of brittle. Coconut, if you add a little to the grind, rounds the flavor the way coastal cooking often does, with the fat of the nut rather than with ghee.

How it is made

Soak rice, grind it with plenty of water to a watery batter, and cook soft rounds that can be rolled at once.

Authentic touch. Use a warm, not screaming-hot, pan and spread with the back of the ladle in one or two strokes. The dosa is done when the top turns from shiny to matte. Browning it hard dries it out.

How it is used

Stack them under a clean cloth so they stay flexible. Serve with coconut chutney. A fish curry is a common Mangaluru partner and would add a fish allergen.

Field notes

  • Neer dosa does not need an overnight ferment. The batter is cooked the day it is ground.
  • The sheet is supposed to bend. A cracker-crisp result is a different dosa.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup raw rice
  • 1 1/4 cups water, plus more for grinding
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon grated coconut, optional
  • Neutral oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Rinse the rice and soak it in water for 3 to 4 hours. Soaking is waiting, not active prep.
  2. Drain and grind the rice with the coconut, if using, and fresh water until the batter is completely smooth and as thin as light cream.
  3. Stir in the salt. The batter should pour in a steady stream. Add water if the grind is thick.
  4. Heat a nonstick or well-seasoned pan over medium heat. Grease it lightly. Pour a ladle of batter and tilt the pan, or spread once, to make a thin round.
  5. Cover for a few seconds if the top looks wet. When the surface is matte and the edge lifts, roll the dosa off. Keep the rolls covered while you finish the batch.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A small piece of coconut ground with the rice
  • A pinch of salt only, which is the plainest home version

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Coconut chutney
  • Jaggery and fresh coconut
  • A vegetable coconut curry

Set dosa

Thick, soft, and mildly sour, with a spongy face and a pale golden underside.

40 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 3 · 8 thick dosas, about 3 sets

History

Set dosa is a Karnataka breakfast dosa made from fermented rice and urad, cooked thick and served in a set, usually two or three cakes on one plate. The name comes from that serving, not from a new grain. A little flattened rice in the grind is a common way to keep the crumb soft. Hotel kitchens in Bengaluru and Mysuru plate it with a potato palya and chutney, and homes do the same with a smaller pan.

Cultural background

The batter is close to an idli batter, sometimes a touch looser. Cooks cover the pan so steam finishes the top. Leaving it uncovered and thin would turn it into an ordinary crisp dosa, which misses the set.

Why it tastes this way

A thicker pool of fermented batter keeps moisture in the center, so the sourness reads as soft and bready instead of as a toasted edge. Flattened rice lightens the crumb. Oil on the underside adds a little browning, but the cake is not chased to a cracker. The mild ferment is the main flavor.

How it is made

Ferment a soft rice and urad batter, pour small thick rounds, cover them, and serve two or three together.

Authentic touch. Cook on medium-low and cover the pan. If you spread the batter thin, you have left set dosa behind.

How it is used

Bring the set to the table stacked, with potato palya and coconut chutney. They are eaten with the hands, torn, not cut like a cake.

Field notes

  • A set is the portion: two or three thick dosas, not a different cereal.
  • Covering the pan is what keeps the center spongy.

Ingredients

  • 3/4 cup idli rice
  • 1/4 cup skinned urad dal
  • 2 tablespoons flattened rice (poha)
  • 1/8 teaspoon fenugreek seeds
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Water for soaking and grinding
  • Neutral oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Rinse and soak the rice in one bowl. Soak the urad, fenugreek, and flattened rice in another for 4 to 5 hours.
  2. Grind the urad mixture to a fluffy paste and the rice to a fine batter. Combine with salt. The batter should be thick, like a soft idli batter.
  3. Ferment covered until risen and mildly sour, often 8 to 12 hours. Do not count the ferment as active prep. Discard it if it spoils.
  4. Stir gently. Heat a pan on medium-low, grease it, and pour a small ladle without spreading it to the rim. The round should stay about a finger thick.
  5. Cover and cook until the top is set and the underside is pale gold. Repeat. Serve three cakes as one set.

Discard fermented batter that smells rotten or shows mold.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A pinch of sugar in the batter, common in some Karnataka homes
  • Ghee on the griddle, which adds dairy

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Potato palya
  • Coconut chutney
  • A thin vegetable sagu

Paper dosa

A very large, very thin fermented rice sheet that shatters at the edge and tastes toasted.

40 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 very large dosas

History

Paper dosa is a size and a cook, not a different grain. The batter is the fermented rice and urad batter of a plain dosa. The cook spreads it across a wide griddle until the sheet is almost translucent, then keeps it on the heat until the rim turns brittle. Restaurant menus sometimes call that spread a paper roast. The spectacle is the diameter. The cereal is unchanged.

Cultural background

Homes with a smaller pan still make a paper-style dosa by using less batter and a hotter surface. The restaurant version is larger because the griddle is larger. Either way, the test is thinness and a dry crackle, not a new recipe of flours.

Why it tastes this way

More of the batter touches iron, so toast and a light ferment sourness dominate. There is almost no soft center to cushion the taste. Oil at the edge fries the lace. Because the grain is still rice and urad, the aroma matches a plain dosa that has been cooked further and thinner.

How it is made

Spread fermented rice and urad batter very thin on a broad hot griddle and cook it until the sheet is crisp enough to crack.

Authentic touch. Use less batter than pride suggests, and spread in one confident spiral. A second pass piles batter back onto the center and ruins the paper thickness.

How it is used

Bring it to the table whole, standing or folded once, with chutney and sambar. It is a shared breakfast more than a stuffed one.

Field notes

  • Paper dosa does not introduce wheat, rava, or millet. It is a thin cook of rice and urad batter.
  • The wide restaurant griddle is why the famous ones look longer than a home dosa.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup idli rice
  • 1/3 cup skinned urad dal
  • 1/4 teaspoon fenugreek seeds
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Water for soaking and grinding
  • Neutral oil for a wide griddle

Steps

  1. Soak rice, and soak urad with fenugreek, in separate bowls for 4 to 6 hours. Grind the urad fluffy and the rice fine. Mix with salt.
  2. Ferment until bubbly and mildly sour, commonly 8 to 14 hours. Active prep does not include that rest. Discard spoiled batter.
  3. Loosen the batter with water until it spreads easily in a thin film. Heat a wide griddle until a water drop dances.
  4. Grease lightly. Pour a small ladle and spread it at once to the full width of the pan, as thin as the batter will allow.
  5. Oil the edge and cook without flipping until the sheet is pale gold, dry, and brittle at the rim. Fold once and serve.

Discard batter that is moldy or smells putrid.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A brush of oil only, no filling
  • Ghee on the last ten seconds of cooking, which adds dairy

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • Street food
  • Coconut chutney
  • Sambar
  • Molaga podi with sesame oil

Uttapam

A thick, soft fermented cake topped with onion, tomato, and green chile.

35 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 3 · 6 uttapams

History

Uttapam is the thick member of the South Indian fermented rice and urad family. Instead of a thin spread, the cook pours a deeper round and presses onion, tomato, or chile into the wet top. Tamil homes and vegetarian hotels both claim it as everyday breakfast food. It uses the dosa ferment, then chooses toppings and thickness rather than a new flour.

Cultural background

The toppings go on while the batter is still soft so they sink and hold. A crisp paper dosa cannot carry tomato pieces. Uttapam can. Green chile keeps the heat mild if you leave the seeds out.

Why it tastes this way

A deeper batter stays moist, so the ferment tastes round and a little sweet. Onion and tomato steam on the covered pan and add juice. Chile and curry leaf keep it savory. The underside still toasts against the pan, which is why uttapam is a griddle cake and not a steamed idli with vegetables.

How it is made

Pour thick rounds of fermented rice and urad batter, press onion, tomato, and chile into the top, cover, and flip once.

Authentic touch. Press the toppings in with a spatula as soon as you pour. If you wait until the top dries, they fall off when you flip.

How it is used

Serve open so the vegetables show, with coconut chutney. Two small uttapams make a breakfast.

Field notes

  • Uttapam is thick on purpose. Spreading it thin turns it back into a dosa.
  • Onion, tomato, and chile are toppings on the ferment, not a change of grain.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups fermented rice and urad dosa batter
  • 1 small onion, finely chopped
  • 1 small tomato, seeded and chopped
  • 1 green chile, minced
  • 2 tablespoons chopped cilantro
  • Neutral oil for the pan
  • Salt if the batter needs it

Steps

  1. Start with bubbly fermented rice and urad batter. If it is stiff, stir in a spoon of water. It should hold a thick round.
  2. Mix onion, tomato, chile, and cilantro in a bowl. Salt them lightly if the batter is already salted.
  3. Oil a medium-hot pan. Pour a ladle of batter and let it spread only into a cake about a centimeter thick.
  4. Scatter a handful of the vegetable mix on top and press gently. Drizzle oil around the edge. Cover for about 2 minutes.
  5. Flip once and cook until the vegetable side takes a little color. Serve the vegetable side up.

Use fermented batter that smells clean and sour, not rotten.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Grated carrot in place of tomato
  • A few curry leaves pressed into the top

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Coconut chutney
  • Sambar
  • Corriander leaves
  • Vegetarian

Pesarattu

A green-gold moong dal pancake, herbal from ginger and green chile, with a soft crisp edge.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 pesarattu

History

Pesarattu is the moong-dal pancake of Andhra cooking. Whole green gram is soaked and ground. Many kitchens cook it after that soak, with no long ferment, or after only a short rest. It is often eaten with ginger chutney, and a famous plate pairs it with upma. The batter is lentil, not the rice-and-urad ferment of a classic dosa, even though both are griddled breakfasts in South India.

Cultural background

Andhra tables treat pesarattu as its own order, especially when upma is tucked inside or served beside it. This recipe is the plain moong pancake. Upma is a pairing, and it is usually wheat semolina, so adding it would change the gluten-free tag.

Why it tastes this way

Cultural background

Kerala breakfasts pair appam with stew: vegetable, egg, or meat. The hopper itself is rice and coconut. A yeast ferment will not taste identical to a toddy ferment, and the page does not pretend it will. The bowl shape is non-negotiable.

Why it tastes this way

Rice and coconut milk make the center sweet and tender. Fermentation, whether from toddy or from yeast, adds a light sourness that keeps the coconut from tasting flat. The thin batter that climbs the hot wall of the pan dries into lace and picks up a toasted edge. Salt is the only savory note in the cake. The stew around it does the rest.

How it is made

Ferment a rice and coconut-milk batter and swirl it in a covered curved pan so it finishes bowl-shaped.

Authentic touch. Swirl the pan as soon as you pour, so a puddle remains in the center and only a film coats the sides. Then cover the pan. An open flat griddle cannot make an appam.

How it is used

Serve the bowls at once, center side up, with a mild vegetable stew. Meat stew is a common pairing and would add a meat allergen.

Why it tastes this way

Jaggery and fresh coconut are the winter sweet of Bengal: molasses, perfume, and fat. Cardamom lifts both. The crepe tastes of rice first and of wheat second, soft rather than crisp, so it wraps the filling without shattering. Because the filling is cooked until the coconut looks dry and glossy, the roll slices instead of leaking syrup.

How it is made

Cook a coconut-jaggery filling, make thin rice-and-wheat crepes, and roll them while warm.

Authentic touch. Cook the coconut filling until it leaves the sides of the pan. A wet filling splits the crepe. Roll patishapta while the crepe is still flexible.

How it is used

Serve warm or at room temperature on a Poush Sankranti plate, with other pithas if you have them. They keep for a day, covered.

Field notes

  • Patishapta is a Poush Sankranti pitha, shared by West Bengal and Bangladesh.
  • Coconut filling and kheer filling are both traditional. This recipe writes the coconut one.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup rice flour
  • 1/4 cup whole wheat flour
  • 1 cup water, plus more if needed
  • A pinch of salt
  • 1 cup freshly grated coconut
  • 1/2 cup jaggery, chopped
  • 1/4 teaspoon cardamom powder
  • Neutral oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Warm the jaggery in a pan until it loosens. Add the coconut and cardamom and cook, stirring, until the filling is glossy and holds together. Cool it.
  2. Whisk rice flour, wheat flour, salt, and water into a thin crepe batter. Rest 10 minutes.
  3. Oil a nonstick pan lightly over medium heat. Pour a small ladle and swirl a thin crepe.
  4. Cook until the top is set and the underside is pale gold. Do not brown it hard or it will crack.
  5. Spread a spoon of filling along one side and roll. Continue with the rest. Serve the same day.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

  • All rice flour if you can roll it without cracking, which would drop the wheat

Malpua

A fried sweet pancake, fennel-scented, soaked in sugar syrup until the edges lacquer.

15 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 5 · 10 malpuas

History

Malpua is a fried sweet pancake of North India and Bengal, served at Holi and at other festivals. Bangladesh shares the Bengali feast table, so it is listed here as well. The batter is wheat flour and milk, often with fennel, fried in ghee and bathed in syrup. Some Bengali versions are richer with khoya. This Lucknow-leaning recipe keeps the fennel and the syrup and does not invent a courtly origin story.

Cultural background

Holi tables expect fried sweets after the colors. Malpua sits with gujiya in many northern houses and with other festival sweets in Bengal. It is not a breakfast crepe. The fry and the syrup are the method.

Why it tastes this way

Fennel is the spice that makes malpua taste like itself rather than like a plain doughnut. Milk in the batter browns and softens the crumb. Syrup cooked to a sticky thread clings to the lacy edge. Ghee, or oil if you must, carries that aroma. The contrast is crisp fringe and a soaked, fragrant center.

How it is made

Mix a loose fennel batter with milk and flour, fry small pancakes in ghee, and soak them briefly in cardamom syrup.

Authentic touch. Fry at a moderate heat so the center cooks before the fringe blacks. Dip into warm syrup, not cold syrup, or the crust sheds the sugar.

How it is used

Serve warm at a festival meal, a few to a plate. They are a dessert, rich enough that two each is plenty.

Field notes

  • Malpua is festival food, especially Holi, in North India and Bengal.
  • The cake is fried and then syruped. It is a fritter, not a dry griddle dosa.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup whole wheat flour or all-purpose flour
  • 1 cup milk
  • 1 teaspoon fennel seeds
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • A pinch of salt
  • 3/4 cup sugar for the syrup
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 2 cardamom pods
  • Ghee for frying

Steps

  1. Whisk flour, milk, fennel, the tablespoon of sugar, and salt into a smooth batter about as thick as cream. Rest 10 minutes.
  2. Simmer 3/4 cup sugar, water, and cardamom until the syrup is sticky and coats a spoon. Keep it warm.
  3. Heat a finger of ghee in a wide pan over medium heat.
  4. Pour a small ladle of batter. Fry until the underside is deep gold and the edge looks lacy, then flip and fry the other side.
  5. Dip each malpua in the warm syrup for a few seconds and lift it out. Fry the rest, adding ghee as needed. Serve warm.

Fry where you can see the pan. Do not leave hot ghee unattended.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Khoya mashed into a Bengali-style batter
  • A pinch of black pepper with the fennel

Pairings and uses

  • Feast or fast day
  • Dessert
  • A bowl of chilled rabri, which is more dairy
  • Plain tea
  • A handful of pomegranate seeds
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Goan alebele

A thin, tender crepe rolled around fresh coconut and jaggery, with cardamom in the filling.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 filled crepes

History

Alebele, also spelled alle belle, are Goan crepes filled with coconut and jaggery. Goan Catholic kitchens serve them at Christmas and at other festive tables. The coast has traded flour, sugar, and coconut for a long time. This page does not name one inventor or a European author for the crepe. The dish as written is the Goan rolled pancake: a wheat crepe, an egg and milk batter, and a fresh coconut center.

Cultural background

Panaji bakeries and home kitchens both turn them out in the festive season. The crepe should be thin enough to roll and pale rather than browned like a dessert pancake from a diner. The filling is coconut cooked with jaggery until it clumps, the same idea as a Bengal coconut pitha filling, in a Goan batter.

Why it tastes this way

Fresh coconut is sweeter and wetter than dried coconut, and jaggery gives it a dark, rum-like molasses note without any rum required. Cardamom is enough spice. The crepe tastes of egg, milk, and wheat, neutral so the filling leads. A little salt in the coconut keeps the roll from cloying.

How it is made

Cook a coconut-jaggery filling, make thin egg crepes, and roll them while they are still soft.

Authentic touch. Grind or grate the coconut fresh if you can. Dry sweetened coconut will taste like a candy bar and throw off the jaggery.

How it is used

Dust nothing on top. Serve the rolls warm on a festive afternoon, two to a person.

Field notes

  • Alebele is the Goan name for these coconut-filled crepes.
  • Christmas tables in Goan Catholic homes are one place they appear, not the only one.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup all-purpose wheat flour
  • 1 egg
  • 3/4 cup milk
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • A pinch of salt
  • 1 cup freshly grated coconut
  • 1/3 cup jaggery, chopped
  • 1/4 teaspoon cardamom
  • Butter or neutral oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Cook coconut, jaggery, cardamom, and a pinch of salt in a dry pan until the jaggery melts and the filling holds a shape. Cool it.
  2. Blend or whisk flour, egg, milk, sugar, and a pinch of salt into a thin crepe batter. Rest 10 minutes.
  3. Butter a crepe pan over medium heat. Pour a small ladle and swirl a thin crepe.
  4. Cook until the underside is pale gold and the top is dry. Turn briefly if needed.
  5. Spread filling in a strip and roll. Keep the rolls covered while you finish the batter.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A splash of coconut milk in the batter
  • A few drops of rose water in the filling

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Feast or fast day
  • Snack
  • Black coffee
  • A slice of ripe mango when the season overlaps
  • Vanilla ice cream, a later cafe habit
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Amboli

A soft, spongy, lightly sour rice pancake from the Konkan coast.

30 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 3 · 6 ambolis

History

Amboli is a fermented rice pancake of the Konkan, especially Malvani kitchens in Maharashtra and the coast down toward Goa. Rice is soaked and ground, then left to sour. Some cooks add a little urad. This recipe uses a spoon of flattened rice for softness and keeps the batter wheat-free. It is served with coconut curries and chutneys, a monsoon and breakfast food of the coast rather than a crisp hotel dosa.

Cultural background

Ratnagiri and Malvan cook amboli as a sponge that soaks up a spicy side. The pancake on this page is not itself a hot dish. The heat lives in the fish or vegetable curry beside it. A fish curry would change the allergens, so the pairing listed here is a coconut chutney.

Why it tastes this way

A long rice ferment in humid coastal air turns sweet soaked rice into something mildly sour and bready. The cake is thick, so the sourness stays soft instead of toasting away. Coconut chutney adds fat and sweetness the batter does not have. The coast's humidity is not a myth here: it is why an overnight bowl sours reliably without a starter.

How it is made

Grind soaked rice, ferment it overnight, and cook thick covered rounds on a moderate griddle.

Authentic touch. Cook it covered and do not chase a deep brown crust. Amboli is done when the top is set and the crumb looks spongy, like a pancake that never tried to be a wafer.

How it is used

Serve in a stack with coconut chutney. On the Konkan it also meets spicy curries. Those are separate dishes.

Field notes

  • Amboli is a Konkan fermented rice cake, soft on purpose.
  • This batter has no wheat, so the pancake is gluten-free.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup raw rice
  • 2 tablespoons flattened rice (poha)
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Water for soaking and grinding
  • Neutral oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Rinse the rice and soak it with the flattened rice for 4 hours.
  2. Drain and grind to a smooth batter a little thicker than crepe batter. Stir in the salt.
  3. Ferment covered overnight, until bubbly and mildly sour. Do not count those hours as active prep. Discard the bowl if it spoils.
  4. Oil a pan over medium-low. Pour a thick round and cover it.
  5. Cook until the top is set and spongy and the underside is pale gold. Flip only if the center needs it. Repeat.

Discard batter that smells rotten or shows mold.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A tablespoon of soaked urad ground with the rice
  • A pinch of sugar if the ferment went very sour

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • Coconut chutney
  • A dry potato bhaji
  • Sweetened coconut milk
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan

Jowar dosa

A pale sorghum dosa, mildly sweet and peppery, with a dry crisp edge.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 jowar dosas

History

Jowar dosa is a sorghum griddle cake from the dry-grain belts of Karnataka and Maharashtra, where jowar is a daily flour. This recipe ferments jowar flour with a spoon of rice flour and no wheat, so it can be tagged gluten-free. Sorghum rotis are the unleavened cousin. The dosa is the watered, soured, griddled one. The page does not pin the habit to a century.

Cultural background

Hubballi sits in a sorghum-growing region, and the flour is ordinary pantry stock there. A jowar dosa is a breakfast use of that flour, not a novelty millet. Cooks who skip the ferment and add yogurt are making the instant kind. This bowl is the fermented one.

Why it tastes this way

Sorghum tastes mildly sweet and a little grassy, less bitter than finger millet. Fermentation brings a gentle sour note that keeps the sweetness from tasting raw. Rice flour helps the fragile sorghum starch hold together on the pan. Cumin and green chile are surface seasoning, so the grain still leads.

How it is made

Ferment a sorghum and rice-flour batter, then cook it in small thin rounds with cumin and chile.

Authentic touch. Spread gently. Jowar batter tears if you press the ladle the way you would on a glossy urad batter. A nonstick or very well-seasoned pan is the practical tool.

How it is used

Eat it hot with coconut chutney or a dry peanut chutney. Peanut chutney would add a peanut allergen, so the written cake stays nut-free.

Field notes

  • Jowar is sorghum, not millet and not wheat.
  • This recipe stays gluten-free by using sorghum and rice flour only.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup jowar flour (sorghum), with no wheat added
  • 2 tablespoons rice flour, with no wheat added
  • 1 1/2 cups water
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon cumin seeds
  • 1 green chile, minced
  • Neutral oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Whisk the flours, salt, and water into a lump-free batter.
  2. Ferment covered 8 to 12 hours until slightly sour. Count only the mixing as active prep. Discard batter that spoils.
  3. Stir in cumin and green chile. Loosen with water if needed.
  4. Oil a medium-hot pan. Spread a small round with a light hand.
  5. Cook until the underside is spotted and the edge is dry. Flip for a short minute. Repeat, oiling the pan each time.

Discard fermented batter that smells rotten or shows mold.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Chopped spinach stirred into the batter
  • Black pepper instead of green chile

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Coconut chutney
  • A sprinkle of chopped onion
  • Sambar

Palak cheela

A green spinach and gram-flour pancake, savory with ginger and cumin, soft rather than crisp.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 3 · 6 green cheelas

History

Palak cheela is a North Indian gram-flour pancake with spinach ground or finely chopped into the batter. Besan is still the structure. The spinach is the vegetable, not a dye from somewhere else. Kitchens from Punjab through Delhi make it as a breakfast that uses a bunch of greens. It is unfermented. It is not the plain onion besan cheela, and it is not a moong dal cake.

Cultural background

Chandigarh markets sell spinach through the cool months, which is when this cheela tastes sweetest. The leaf should be washed well. Grit in the batter is the usual failure, not the spice. A mild green chile is enough.

Why it tastes this way

Spinach sweetens and loosens besan, and it turns the crumb green and moist. Ginger keeps the green from tasting grassy in a raw way. Cumin is the savory line. Because the cake is thicker and wetter than a plain cheela, the chickpea flavor is softer and the spinach reads in every bite.

How it is made

Blend spinach with besan, ginger, and spices, then cook small green pancakes on a moderate pan.

Authentic touch. Squeeze the cooked or very finely chopped spinach so the batter does not become a soup. Extra leaf water steams the cake and stops it browning.

How it is used

Serve hot with a spoon of pickle or a dairy-free chutney. It is a savory breakfast.

Field notes

  • Palak is spinach. The flour is still besan.
  • The cake is mixed and cooked at once, with no ferment.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups spinach leaves, washed
  • 1 cup besan (chickpea flour), with no wheat added
  • 1 inch ginger
  • 1 green chile
  • 1/2 teaspoon cumin seeds
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Water only if the batter is stiff
  • Neutral oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Blend spinach, ginger, and green chile to a coarse puree.
  2. Stir in besan, cumin, and salt. Add a spoon of water only if you cannot spread the batter. Rest 10 minutes.
  3. Oil a pan over medium heat. Spread a small round.
  4. Cook until the underside browns and the top loses its raw shine. Flip and cook through so the besan is done.
  5. Repeat. The green darkens as it cooks, which is expected.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A handful of fenugreek leaves with the spinach
  • Grated carrot for sweetness

  • Lime
  • A side of yogurt, which is dairy

Sri Lankan hoppers

A crisp-edged rice bowl with a soft coconut center and a light ferment tang.

35 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 hoppers

History

Hoppers are Sri Lanka's bowl-shaped rice cakes, kin to Kerala appam and still their own daily bread. Fermented rice and coconut milk are poured into a small curved pan, swirled, and covered. Coconut toddy is a traditional ferment. Yeast is the home substitute in this recipe. A hopper plate in Colombo might come with lunumiris or seeni sambol. Egg hoppers, with an egg broken into the center, are a separate page.

Cultural background

Hopper shops pour from a bowl of batter that has worked overnight. The edge laces where it is thin, and the middle stays pale and spongy. String hoppers, which are pressed rice noodles, are a different food and not this pan.

Why it tastes this way

Coconut milk sweetens the rice and keeps the center custardy. Fermentation adds a clean sour note that toddy makes fruitier and yeast makes milder. The hot curved wall fries the thin film into a cracker while the covered center steams. Salt is spare. The sambol beside the hopper carries chile and, often, dried fish.

How it is made

Ferment rice ground with coconut milk, swirl it in a hot hopper pan, and cover until the bowl sets.

Authentic touch. Swirl once and cover. If you leave the batter pooled without climbing the sides, you have a thick pancake, not a hopper.

How it is used

Serve immediately with onion sambol. Some sambols include dried Maldive fish, which would add a fish allergen. The hopper batter here does not.

Field notes

  • Sri Lankan hoppers and Kerala appam share a pan shape and keep different names and tables.
  • An egg cracked into the center is an egg hopper, written on its own page.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup raw rice
  • 1/4 cup cooked rice
  • 3/4 cup coconut milk
  • 1/4 teaspoon dry yeast
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Water as needed
  • Neutral oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Soak the raw rice for 3 hours, drain, and grind it with the cooked rice and a little water until smooth.
  2. Stir in coconut milk, yeast, sugar, and salt. Aim for a batter that pours in a ribbon.
  3. Ferment covered until bubbly, often 6 to 12 hours. Exclude that rest from active prep.
  4. Heat a hopper pan, grease it, pour a ladle, and swirl so the sides film and the center stays thick.
  5. Cover and cook on medium-low until the rim is crisp and the center is spongy and set. Repeat.

Discard batter that smells rotten or grows mold.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Toddy in place of yeast where it is sold fresh and safe
  • A sweeter center with an extra spoon of coconut milk

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Onion sambol without dried fish
  • Seeni sambol
  • A mild coconut gravy
