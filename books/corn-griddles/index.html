Appalachian Cornmeal Cakes

Toasted meal, tangy milk, and a sturdy round.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 cakes

History

Cornmeal cakes were ordinary bread in the southern mountains, where corn was the grain people grew and milled. Scots-Irish and English settlers brought griddle and oat habits. Cherokee and other Native nations of these mountains had grown corn long before those settlements. This cake is later mountain home cooking. It is not presented here as a Cherokee recipe.

Cultural background

A mix of meal, buttermilk, and egg cooked in a skillet was breakfast or the bread for soup beans. It is humbler than spoonbread and closer to a hoe cake.

Why it tastes this way

Mountain farms stored corn as meal because the grain dried and kept. Buttermilk from a family cow softened it. The taste is toasted meal and acid, not vanilla. Cooks who had no wheat still had a bread. That economy is the flavor.

How it is made

Cornmeal is soaked briefly with buttermilk, bound with an egg, and cooked as thick rounds.

Authentic touch. Use a well-seasoned skillet and a moderate fire. A blazing griddle burns the meal before the center sets.

How it is used

Butter, sorghum, or beside soup beans. Both uses are honest.

Field notes

  • This book does not assign the cake to the Cherokee. Corn in these mountains is older than this recipe.
  • Soup beans and cornbread are a documented mountain plate. The cake can play the bread’s part.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup plain cornmeal
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1 cup buttermilk
  • 1 large egg
  • 1 tablespoon melted butter or oil

Steps

  1. Stir meal, salt, and soda. Add buttermilk, egg, and fat.
  2. Rest 10 minutes.
  3. Cook on a greased medium griddle as thick rounds.
  4. Flip when the underside is brown and the top is set.
  5. Cook through.
  6. Serve with beans or sorghum.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of wheat flour softens them and means they are no longer gluten-free.
  • Water instead of buttermilk is the leaner, older cake.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Bread course
  • sorghum
  • soup beans
  • butter
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Arepa Antioqueña

A corn arepa griddled and often finished with a little butter, served beside the meal.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 arepas

History

In Antioquia and the Paisa region, the arepa is a corn cake served with meals, often not split and stuffed the Venezuelan way. It is griddled, sometimes with a pat of butter at the end. The bandeja paisa and simpler breakfasts both include it. This recipe is the plain corn cake.

Cultural background

Masarepa and water, salt, and a griddle. Butter at the end is common and adds dairy. The cake itself can be vegan if you skip that pat.

Why it tastes this way

The Paisa arepa is a side bread, so it stays mild and corn-sweet, a contrast to beans, eggs, and meat on the same plate. Griddling toasts it. Butter browns on the hot surface. The choice not to stuff it is cultural, not a missing step. Venezuela’s stuffed arepa is the neighbor, not the template.

How it is made

Masarepa disks are cooked on a griddle until the crust is set.

Authentic touch. Make them a bit thinner than a stuffed Venezuelan arepa if you are serving them beside a full plate. Cook until the crust is dry and the center is set.

How it is used

Hot, beside eggs or beans. Butter is optional.

Field notes

  • Paisa arepas are often a side, not a stuffed sandwich.
  • Butter at the end is optional and adds dairy.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups masarepa
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 1/2 cups warm water
  • Butter, optional

Steps

  1. Mix masarepa, salt, and water. Rest 5 minutes.
  2. Shape disks a bit thinner than a stuffed arepa.
  3. Cook on a dry or lightly oiled griddle until the crust is dry and the center is set.
  4. Optional: rub with butter.
  5. Serve beside eggs or beans.
  6. Do not split them unless you want to.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A cheese arepa presses cheese into the dough and adds dairy.
  • Venezuelan arepas are more often split.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Bread course
  • eggs
  • beans
  • butter
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan

  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Arepa Boyacense

A toasted corn arepa from Boyacá, often sweetened and served with cheese.

10 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 arepas

History

Arepa boyacense is the arepa of Boyacá in the Colombian highlands, often made with corn flour and a little wheat in some houses, griddled, and eaten with cheese or a touch of sweetness. Recipes differ. This version stays with masarepa and a spoon of sugar, plus cheese on the side, and does not add wheat.

Cultural background

Highland kitchens toast the cake more than they stuff it. Cheese is the partner. The crumb is denser than a fresh-corn arepa.

Why it tastes this way

A drier corn dough toasts deeply on a griddle, which is the flavor of a highland arepa meant to keep and to eat with salty cheese. A little sugar is a regional sweetness, not a dessert frosting. The altitude and the habit of griddling rather than frying are why it tastes toasted instead of oily.

How it is made

A firm masarepa dough with a little sugar is cooked dry on a griddle.

Authentic touch. Cook it longer than a soft cachapa, until the face is well spotted. It should still be moist inside. Do not oil it heavily.

How it is used

With cheese.

Field notes

  • This highland arepa is toasted more than it is stuffed.
  • This version stays with corn and does not add wheat.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups masarepa
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 2 cups warm water
  • Cheese, to serve

Steps

  1. Mix a firm dough. Add a spoon more water only if it cracks.
  2. Shape thick disks.
  3. Cook on a dry medium griddle until well spotted and cooked through.
  4. Serve with cheese.
  5. Do not oil them heavily.
  6. The face should toast.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some Boyacá recipes add wheat flour. This one does not.
  • Arepa de choclo is the fresh-corn cake.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Snack
  • cheese
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Arepa de Choclo

A sweet fresh-corn arepa, often with cheese, folded or topped.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 6 cakes

History

Arepa de choclo is a Colombian fresh-corn cake, especially associated with places that sell it with cheese as a sweet-salty breakfast. Choclo is fresh corn. It is closer to a cachapa than to a masarepa arepa. Cheese is part of the usual plate, so this recipe includes it and is not vegan.

Cultural background

Street vendors cook them on a griddle. The corn is sweet. The cheese melts. They are eaten with the hands.

Why it tastes this way

Fresh corn’s sugar browns on the griddle, and a salty fresh cheese is the contrast Colombians expect. That sweet-salt pair is the dish. It is a different corn from the dried masarepa of a plain arepa: juicy, seasonal, and closer to a pancake. The griddle, not an oven, is the tool.

How it is made

Fresh corn is blended, cooked as thick cakes, and topped with cheese.

Authentic touch. Blend the corn coarse. If the batter is watery, a spoon of masarepa or cornmeal helps. Put the cheese on while the cake is hot so it softens.

How it is used

Folded or open, hot.

Field notes

  • Choclo means fresh corn, not dried masarepa.
  • Cheese on top is the usual sweet-salt bite.

Ingredients

  • 4 ears sweet corn
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon masarepa, if needed
  • 1 cup crumbled fresh cheese
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Blend the corn and salt. Add masarepa if watery.
  2. Cook thick cakes on a medium griddle.
  3. Top with cheese while hot.
  4. Fold or serve open.
  5. Eat immediately.
  6. The corn should taste sweet.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Without cheese it is closer to a cachapa and can be vegan.
  • Cachapas are the Venezuelan sister.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Street food
  • queso fresco
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Basque Talo

A toasted corn flatbread, often wrapped around chistorra or chocolate.

20 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 talo

History

Talo is a Basque corn flatbread, cooked on a griddle, traditionally wrapped around chistorra sausage or, for a sweet, around chocolate. Corn arrived in the Basque Country and became talo flour, a coarser meal. The bread is festival and cider-house food as well as home food. This recipe is the bread. The sausage is a separate meat.

Cultural background

It should be flexible enough to wrap and toasted enough to taste corn. It is thicker than a crepe and thinner than a loaf.

Why it tastes this way

Coarse cornmeal tastes toasted and slightly sweet, a wrap that does not fight a salty sausage or a piece of chocolate. The griddle is the Basque tool for this bread. Corn replaced earlier grains in this particular cake after maize spread in Europe. The two fillings, sausage and chocolate, show how the same bread works as street food and as a treat.

How it is made

Cornmeal, salt, and water are patted into rounds and cooked on a griddle.

Authentic touch. Rest the dough so the meal hydrates. Cook it on a dry or lightly oiled griddle until it spots and is cooked through, still pliable.

How it is used

Warm, wrapped around a cooked sausage or a piece of chocolate, or simply buttered.

Field notes

  • Talo is a Basque corn flatbread for sausage or chocolate.
  • The sausage is a filling, not part of the dough.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups cornmeal
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 1/4 cups boiling water

Steps

  1. Stir cornmeal, salt, and boiling water. Rest 10 minutes.
  2. Pat thin rounds.
  3. Cook on a medium dry griddle until spotted and cooked through, still flexible.
  4. Wrap and serve.
  5. A sausage or chocolate is a filling, not part of the dough.
  6. Add a spoon of water if the dough cracks.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A wheat flour addition makes them easier and removes the gluten-free label.
  • They are a bread, not a syrup pancake.

Pairings and uses

  • Bread course
  • Street food
  • chistorra
  • chocolate
  • Vegetarian

Cachapas

A sweet-corn pancake, soft and irregular, often folded with cheese.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 6 cachapas

History

Cachapas are Venezuelan pancakes of fresh ground sweet corn, cooked on a griddle and often folded around cheese, queso de mano or similar. They are street and home food. The batter is the corn itself, sometimes loosened with a little milk or water. This recipe keeps the batter dairy-free and serves cheese as an optional fold, so the cake can be vegan and the cheese is a choice.

Cultural background

The corn must be sweet and juicy. Canned corn, drained and blended, is a workable off-season substitute and tastes less alive. Say which you used.

Why it tastes this way

Fresh corn is sweet because the kernels are immature and full of sugar, which is why a cachapa tastes like corn and dessert at once even when the filling is salty cheese. The griddle browns the natural sugar. No wheat is required. The cheese, when used, is salty and squeaky, the contrast. Street griddles make them irregular. That rustic edge is normal.

How it is made

Fresh corn is blended with a little salt and cooked as thick pancakes.

Authentic touch. Blend the corn until it is a coarse batter, not a juice. Cook on medium and cover briefly so the center sets. It will not look like a smooth wheat pancake.

How it is used

Folded, with cheese if you eat dairy, or plain.

Field notes

  • Cachapas are fresh-corn pancakes. Cheese is optional here.
  • Canned corn works and tastes less alive than fresh.

Ingredients

  • 4 ears sweet corn, kernels cut off
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon cornmeal, if needed
  • Oil
  • Cheese, optional

Steps

  1. Blend the corn and salt to a coarse batter. Add cornmeal if it is watery.
  2. Cook thick rounds on a medium oiled griddle, covered briefly.
  3. Flip when the underside is spotted.
  4. Fold with cheese if you eat dairy.
  5. Serve hot.
  6. They will look rustic.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of cornmeal helps a watery blend hold.
  • Costa Rican chorreadas are the close cousin.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Street food
  • Savory meal
  • queso de mano
  • butter
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Carolina Corn Griddle Cakes

Sweet-corn meal, buttermilk, and a crisper edge than a wheat cake.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 cakes

History

Corn was the daily grain of the Carolinas long before wheat pancakes filled a diner menu. Indigenous farmers in the region grew corn, and later Black and white Southern cooks turned meal into breads, mush, and griddle cakes. This cake is the breakfast form: cornmeal loosened with buttermilk and an egg, cooked in rounds.

Cultural background

It belongs with breakfast or with a plate of vegetables. It is closer to a hoe cake than to a fluffy flour pancake, with enough egg and buttermilk to make it tender.

Why it tastes this way

Coarse meal keeps a sandy, toasted flavor that fine wheat flour cannot. Buttermilk softens that grit and adds acid. The coastal and piedmont South could rely on corn when wheat was a bought luxury, which is why so many Carolina breads start with meal.

How it is made

Cornmeal, buttermilk, egg, and a little chemical leavener are cooked as small thick cakes.

Authentic touch. Let the batter stand 10 minutes so the meal swells. If you cook it at once, the center tastes raw.

How it is used

Butter, honey, or a savory plate of eggs and greens. They do not need a heavy sweet syrup.

Field notes

  • Resting the meal is the step that keeps the center from tasting like dry polenta.
  • Plain cornmeal keeps the cake gluten-free. Corn muffin mix usually does not.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup plain cornmeal
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/4 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1 cup buttermilk
  • 1 large egg
  • 1 tablespoon melted butter

Steps

  1. Stir the dry ingredients. Whisk in buttermilk, egg, and butter.
  2. Rest 10 minutes.
  3. Cook on a buttered medium griddle as small thick rounds.
  4. Flip when the edges look dry.
  5. Cook until the center is set.
  6. Serve hot.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of flour makes them softer and removes the gluten-free label.
  • Cracklings in the batter are an older savory turn and add meat.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Bread course
  • Side dish
  • butter
  • honey
  • eggs
  • greens
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Chorreadas

Sweet fresh-corn pancakes, cousins of cachapas.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 cakes

History

Chorreadas are Costa Rican fresh-corn pancakes, cooked on a griddle and served with sour cream or a crumbly cheese. They are the local sister of Venezuelan cachapas and Nicaraguan güirilas. The batter is corn. This written cake is vegan. Sour cream is the usual partner and adds dairy at the table.

Cultural background

They are a bit irregular. Sweet corn is the requirement. They are breakfast or a snack, not a stuffed dinner.

Why it tastes this way

Fresh corn’s sugar browns on a griddle and tastes like the season. A Costa Rican table often meets that sweetness with sour cream, the same sweet-salt idea as a cheese cachapa. No wheat is needed. The cake is a Central American corn pancake with its own name.

How it is made

Fresh corn is blended with a pinch of salt and cooked as small pancakes.

Authentic touch. Blend to a coarse batter and cook on medium. Cover for a minute if the center is slow. They should be spotted, not burnt.

How it is used

With sour cream or cheese if you eat dairy, or plain.

Field notes

  • Chorreadas are Costa Rica’s fresh-corn pancakes.
  • Sour cream is a table partner, not required in the batter.

Ingredients

  • 4 ears sweet corn
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon masa harina, if needed
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Blend corn and salt to a coarse batter.
  2. Cook small cakes on a medium oiled griddle until spotted and set.
  3. Serve plain or with sour cream.
  4. They should taste of corn.
  5. Cover briefly if the center is slow.
  6. Eat them the same day.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of milk in the blend adds dairy and richness.
  • Cachapas and güirilas are the neighboring cakes.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Snack
  • sour cream
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan

  • Dairy-free

Corn Tortillas

Soft nixtamal corn flatbreads, toasted on a comal.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 tortillas

History

Corn tortillas are the foundational griddle bread of Mexico, made from masa of nixtamalized maize. Nixtamal, cooking dried corn with calcium hydroxide, is an Indigenous technique that makes the grain nutritious and able to form a dough. Tortillas are cooked on a comal. They are not pancakes, and they are in this journal because they are the corn cake that explains a great deal of Mexican food. This recipe uses masa harina, the flour of that process, not a raw-corn shortcut.

Cultural background

They are the daily bread: tacos, enchiladas, a meal beside beans. A tortilla press or a flat plate and a bag help you flatten them.

Why it tastes this way

Nixtamalized corn tastes toasted and a little mineral, different from sweet cornmeal, because the alkali changes the grain. A hot comal dries the surface and keeps the center flexible. Wheat never had to be the daily bread where masa was the technology. The flavor is corn, salt, and the comal.

How it is made

Masa harina and water are pressed into thin rounds and cooked on a hot dry comal.

Authentic touch. Keep the dough as soft as clay, not crumbly. If it cracks at the edges, knead in a teaspoon of water. Cook about a minute a side, until they puff a little. Stack them in a towel.

How it is used

Warm, as a bread, the same day if you can.

Field notes

  • Masa harina is nixtamalized corn. It is not the same as cornmeal.
  • A soft dough that cracks needs a spoon more water.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups masa harina
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 1/2 cups warm water, plus more if needed

Steps

  1. Mix masa, salt, and water to a soft clay-like dough. Rest 10 minutes.
  2. Press thin rounds between plastic.
  3. Cook on a hot dry comal about a minute a side, until they speckle and puff a little.
  4. Stack in a towel.
  5. Serve warm.
  6. Add water if the edges crack.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Blue corn masa makes blue tortillas and is the same method.
  • Flour tortillas are the northern wheat bread.

Pairings and uses

  • Bread course
  • Supper
  • beans
  • salsa
  • eggs
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Gorditas

Thick corn cakes, griddled, split, and filled.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 gorditas

History

Gorditas are thick masa cakes, cooked on a comal and often split and filled with beans, cheese, or stewed meat. The name means little fat ones. They are street and home food across central and northern Mexico, with many local fillings. This recipe is the masa cake and a bean filling, so the bread itself stays vegan.

Cultural background

They should puff a little. The pocket is pinched open after cooking. Fillings are salty and savory against the plain corn.

Why it tastes this way

Thicker masa stays soft inside and toasted outside, a container rather than a flexible tortilla. Nixtamal is still the corn flavor. A filling of beans makes a meal from the same staples. Regional gorditas differ. The thick comal cake is the shared idea.

How it is made

Masa is patted into thick rounds, cooked on a comal, and filled with beans.

Authentic touch. Keep the masa soft and the cakes about a half inch thick. Cook them until they are no longer raw in the center and the outside is spotted. Split them carefully while warm.

How it is used

Hot, filled.

Field notes

  • Gorditas are thick enough to split and fill.
  • The written filling is beans, so the cake can stay meatless.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups masa harina
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 1/2 cups warm water
  • 1 cup refried beans

Steps

  1. Mix a soft masa dough.
  2. Shape 6 thick disks.
  3. Cook on a medium comal until spotted and cooked through, turning.
  4. Split and fill with beans.
  5. Serve hot.
  6. The center should not be raw masa.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A potato and cheese filling is another meatless option and adds dairy.
  • Fried gorditas exist and are a different, heavier cake.

Pairings and uses

  • Street food
  • Supper
  • Stuffed or filled
  • refried beans
  • salsa
  • Vegetarian

Güirilas

Soft fresh-corn cakes, traditionally eaten with cuajada cheese.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 cakes

History

Güirilas are Nicaraguan fresh-corn cakes, close to chorreadas and cachapas, traditionally paired with cuajada, a fresh cheese. They are breakfast and a side. The corn cake is vegan here. The cheese is the traditional partner and is dairy.

Cultural background

The texture is soft. The corn is sweet. They are cooked on a griddle and eaten the same morning.

Why it tastes this way

Young corn tastes sweet and moist, and a fresh salty cheese is the Nicaraguan contrast, the same logic as the other Central American corn pancakes with different names. Keeping the name güirila matters so Nicaragua is not only a footnote on the cachapa page. The griddle toasts the milk sugars in the corn.

How it is made

Fresh corn is blended and cooked as thick soft cakes.

Authentic touch. Do not add much liquid. The kernels supply it. Cook until the center is set and the face is spotted gold.

How it is used

With cuajada or another fresh cheese, if you eat dairy.

Field notes

  • Güirilas are Nicaragua’s fresh-corn cakes.
  • Cuajada, a fresh cheese, is the traditional partner.

Ingredients

  • 4 ears sweet corn
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Oil
  • Fresh cheese, to serve

Steps

  1. Blend the corn and salt.
  2. Cook thick soft cakes on a medium griddle until spotted and set.
  3. Serve with cuajada or another fresh cheese.
  4. Do not add much water.
  5. The cakes should stay soft.
  6. Eat them warm.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Chorreadas are the Costa Rican name in the same family.
  • A savory herb in the batter is a home change.

Pairings and uses

Hoe Cakes

Toasted cornmeal, salt, and the taste of a hot greased griddle.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 cakes

History

Hoe cakes are a Southern cornmeal bread cooked on a griddle. The usual explanation of the name is that cooks fried the batter on the blade of a field hoe. Enslaved African American cooks, and later Black Southern cooks, kept corn griddle breads in daily use. West African millet and corn cakes cooked on a griddle are part of the wider history. The hoe story is a traditional account, and the bread itself is well documented.

Cultural background

Hoe cakes were field food and kitchen food: portable, cheap, and made from the corn that dominated Southern rations and farms. They still show up next to greens, beans, and fish.

Why it tastes this way

Corn, not wheat, is the flavor, and that corn is tied to Indigenous agriculture in the Americas and to the rations and fields of slavery. A thin batter and a hot pan toast the meal instead of steaming it, which is why a hoe cake tastes browned and a boiled dumpling from the same meal does not. Little or no sugar keeps it a bread.

How it is made

Cornmeal is stirred with boiling water or cold water, salt, and a little fat, then cooked as thin rounds on a greased griddle.

Authentic touch. Spread the batter thin and cook it slower than a fluffy pancake. The cake is done when the corn smells toasted and the center is set, not when it puffs.

How it is used

Hot Water Cornbread

Crisp corn crust and a soft, steaming corn center.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 cakes

History

Hot-water cornbread is a Southern skillet bread: cornmeal scalded with boiling water, shaped, and fried in fat. It is a cousin of hoe cakes, not a sweet bakery corn muffin. Cookbooks from Black Southern and broader Southern kitchens both record it as a bread for beans, greens, and fish.

Cultural background

The boiling water partly cooks the meal before it fries, which is why the inside stays tender while the outside crisps. It is weeknight food, made when the pot of beans is already on.

Why it tastes this way

Scalding gelatinizes some of the starch, so the cake holds together without wheat or egg. The flavor is corn plus whatever fat you fry in. Bacon drippings taste meaty. Neutral oil tastes plainer and keeps this version free of meat. Climate comes in through the corn itself: the South grew dent corn for meal, grits, and bread because wheat was less reliable in much of the region.

How it is made

Boiling water is stirred into cornmeal and salt. The mash is shaped into small cakes and fried in a skillet.

Authentic touch. The mash should be hot when you shape it. Cold meal fried in a skim of oil steams instead of crusting. Use enough fat to come a little up the sides of each cake.

How it is used

Put the cakes beside beans, greens, or fried fish. They are not usually syrup cakes.

Field notes

  • Hot-water cornbread is fried, which is why it is crisper than a hoe cake cooked in a thin film of fat.
  • It should not contain sugar. Sweet corn muffins are a different bread.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup plain cornmeal
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
  • 3/4 cup boiling water
  • 1/3 cup neutral oil, for frying

Steps

  2. Heat the oil in a skillet over medium.
  3. Shape the hot dough into small patties with wet hands.
  4. Fry until deep golden on both sides, turning once.
  5. Drain briefly on a rack.
  6. Serve at once beside a savory plate.

Fry over steady heat and do not leave the pan. Let the oil cool before you discard it.

History

Why it tastes this way

Blue corn tastes earthier and a little sweeter than yellow meal because of the variety, not because of food coloring. It is an Indigenous crop adapted to high, dry fields. The pancake format is new. How it is made

Blue cornmeal is mixed with milk, egg, and a little leavener and cooked as small cakes.

Honey or butter. A side of red chile is a New Mexican option and adds heat the batter does not have. Piñon nuts add a tree-nut allergen.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 tlayudas

History

Tlayudas are large Oaxacan corn tortillas, often cooked until they are partly dry and crisp, then topped with refried beans, quesillo or asiento, cabbage, and salsa. They are a night-market food of Oaxaca. The tortilla is the base. This recipe uses large corn tortillas you cook further on a comal, with beans and cheese. Asiento, unrefined pork fat, is traditional and is not in this version.

Cultural background

They are a meal. The tortilla should be toasted, not soft like a fresh taco tortilla. Quesillo is the stringy Oaxacan cheese. Mozzarella is only a stand-in and should be named as such if you use it.

Why it tastes this way

A large masa tortilla toasted on a comal tastes nuttier and crisper than a fresh soft tortilla, which is why it can hold beans without falling apart. Oaxaca’s corn diversity is in the masa. The toppings are the market meal. Cheese adds salt and stretch. Leaving out asiento keeps the plate free of pork and changes it from some street versions. That change is stated.

How it is made

Large corn tortillas are toasted on a comal, spread with beans and cheese, and heated until the cheese softens.

Authentic touch. Toast the tortilla until it is dry and blistered but not burnt to carbon. Spread beans thinly. Too much weight cracks it.

How it is used

Folded or open, with salsa and shredded cabbage.

Field notes

  • Tlayudas are the large toasted tortillas of Oaxaca.
  • This version leaves out pork asiento and says so.

Ingredients

  • 4 large corn tortillas
  • 1 cup refried beans
  • 1 cup shredded quesillo or mozzarella
  • 1 cup shredded cabbage
  • Salsa
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Toast the tortillas on a comal until dry and blistered but not burnt.
  2. Spread a thin layer of beans.
  3. Add cheese and heat until it softens.
  4. Top with cabbage and salsa.
  5. Serve immediately.
  6. Do not overload them.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Asiento is the traditional pork fat and adds meat.
  • A fully soft tortilla is a different, taco-like bite.

Pairings and uses

Venezuelan Arepas

Thick corn cakes of precooked maize flour, griddled, then split.

15 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 arepas

History

Arepas are a daily bread of Venezuela and Colombia, with roots in Indigenous maize cakes of the region. The modern Venezuelan arepa is usually made from precooked corn flour, masarepa, shaped into thick disks, griddled, and often finished in the oven, then split and filled. Fillings are a whole cuisine. This page is the corn cake. Reina pepiada and other meat fillings are variations, not the dough.

Cultural background

The dough should feel like clay. A griddle browns the face. A few minutes in a hot oven, if you have one, sets the center. They are split while warm.

Why it tastes this way

Precooked corn flour is a twentieth-century product that made a much older maize cake fast to cook at home. The taste is toasted corn, mild and a little sweet, a container for salty fillings. The thickness is why an arepa is not a tortilla. Venezuela’s habit of stuffing them turned the bread into breakfast and dinner.

How it is made

Masarepa and water are shaped into thick disks and cooked on a griddle, then split.

Authentic touch. Use masarepa, not masa harina for tortillas and not cornmeal. The dough should be moist and not crack. Cook the crust, then finish the center. A raw white line in the middle means they need more time.

How it is used

Split, with cheese, beans, or eggs. Meat fillings are a separate choice.

Field notes

  • Use masarepa, the precooked corn flour, not tortilla masa.
  • Stuffing them is the Venezuelan habit.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups masarepa
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 1/2 cups warm water
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Stir masarepa, salt, and water. Rest 5 minutes. The dough should be moist and not crack.
  2. Shape 6 thick disks.
  3. Cook on a medium griddle until a crust forms, then bake at 400°F for 10 minutes or continue on the griddle until the center is set.
  4. Split.
  5. Fill with beans, cheese, or eggs.
  6. Do not use tortilla masa harina.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Colombian arepas are often left unsplit and served beside the meal.
  • Andean arepas use wheat and are a different bread.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Stuffed or filled

  • beans
  • eggs

Virginia Corn Cakes

White cornmeal, a little sugar, and a soft crumb.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 cakes

History

Virginia cooks have used white cornmeal for breads and griddle cakes for generations. Corn was an Indigenous crop in the Chesapeake, and English colonists adopted it early. Later Virginia tables, Black and white, kept corn cakes as breakfast and as a bread beside salt-cured ham. A spoon of sugar in some house recipes marks them as breakfast rather than a purely savory hoe cake.

Cultural background

White meal is preferred in much of Virginia when it is available. The cake is thinner than a corn muffin and thicker than a cracker.

Why it tastes this way

White cornmeal tastes cleaner and less sweet than yellow meal, which is why a Virginia cake can take a little sugar and still taste like corn rather than cake. Ham on the same table is salty and smoky, so the bread stays mild. The Chesapeake’s long corn agriculture is the reason meal, not wheat, is the traditional starch.

How it is made

White cornmeal, milk, egg, and a little sugar and leavener are cooked on a griddle.

Authentic touch. Rest the batter. White meal can be fine, and a short rest keeps the center from tasting powdery.

How it is used

Butter, honey, or beside eggs and ham. The ham is a separate salty dish.

Field notes

  • White cornmeal is a Virginia preference, not a rule of chemistry.
  • A little sugar marks this as the breakfast cake rather than a hoe cake.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup white cornmeal
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1/4 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup milk or buttermilk
  • 1 large egg
  • 1 tablespoon melted butter

Steps

  1. Stir everything together and rest 10 minutes.
  2. Cook on a buttered medium griddle.
  3. Flip when the underside browns and the top sets.
  4. Cook until the center is done.
  5. Serve hot.
  6. Leftovers split and toast well the next day.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Leave the sugar out for a bread to eat with beans.
  • Yellow meal makes a sweeter, deeper cake.
