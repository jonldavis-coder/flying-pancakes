Belarusian draniki

Belarusian potato pancakes bound with potato starch and egg, sour cream on the side.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 pancakes

History

Draniki are the potato pancakes of Belarus, grated and fried. They are a national home dish, served with sour cream. A pork gravy called machanka is a traditional accompaniment and is not in this recipe, so the cakes stay vegetarian. This batter uses the potato's own starch and an egg, not wheat flour, so it is gluten-free. Sour cream is a topping. The cakes are fried in oil and the recipe is dairy-free until you add that topping.

Cultural background

Potato is the staple. Draniki are everyday food, not a single holiday. They are cousins of deruny, placki, and latkes, with a Belarusian name and this flourless method.

Why it tastes this way

Crisp fried potato and onion, salted, with a soft center. Egg binds the shreds without flour. The flavor is direct. Sour cream cools the crust if you add it. Machanka, if you serve it, makes a heavier meat plate. The oil should be hot enough to crust the cake before it absorbs grease.

How it is made

Potatoes and onion are grated, mixed with their starch, an egg, and salt, and fried in oil.

Authentic touch. Do not add wheat flour if you want this gluten-free version. Keep the starch that settles out of the grated potato. Sour cream is the usual condiment and is optional if the meal must stay dairy-free.

How it is used

Hot, with sour cream. Machanka, a pork gravy, is the meat accompaniment and is not part of this batter.

Field notes

  • Draniki are the Belarusian potato pancakes.
  • This recipe uses no wheat flour.
  • Sour cream is the usual topping. Machanka is a pork gravy served beside them, not in this batter.

Ingredients

  • 700 g starchy potatoes, peeled
  • 1 small onion
  • 1 large egg
  • 1 tsp fine salt
  • Neutral oil, for frying

Steps

  1. Grate the potatoes and onion. Save the liquid, let the starch settle, pour off the water, and mix the starch back in.
  2. Stir in the egg and salt.
  3. Fry flattened rounds in hot oil until both sides are brown and the centers are cooked.
  4. Serve hot. Add sour cream at the table if you want it.

The potato must cook through. Do not leave the frying pan.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of wheat flour, which removes the gluten-free diet
  • Ukrainian deruny, which use flour on their page

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Sour cream
  • Machanka, a pork gravy, for a meat meal
  • Salt
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free

Chilote milcao

Pan-fried cakes of raw and cooked potato, salty, crisp at the edge, and dense inside.

30 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 cakes

History

Milcao is a potato cake from Chiloé, where potato diversity is old and specific. The dough mixes grated raw potato with mashed cooked potato. The starch from the raw half is what holds the cake together. Some Chilote cooks fold in pork cracklings. This recipe leaves the pork out, so the cakes stay vegetarian and vegan. A curanto, the pit cook of the archipelago, can include milcaos. The method here is the home skillet, not a pit.

Cultural background

On Chiloé, milcaos are a staple next to fish stews and in the curanto, and they are also simply pan-fried for a meal. Castro is a fair place-name for a food that belongs to the whole archipelago. The cake is potato and salt. Wheat never enters the bowl.

Why it tastes this way

Cooked potato brings a soft, familiar mash. Raw potato brings sticky starch and a fresher, sharper taste that mellows as it fries. Salt is the seasoning. Oil browns the outside. Because there is no egg and no wheat, the cake tastes only of potato, with a crisp face and a dense center. Pork cracklings would add salt and fat. They are a variation, not this batch.

How it is made

Grate raw potatoes and save the settled starch. Mash an equal weight of cooked potatoes. Mix them with salt, form cakes, and pan-fry until both sides are crisp and the raw potato is cooked through.

Authentic touch. Grate the raw potatoes, squeeze the liquid into a bowl, and let the starch settle. Pour off the water and put that starch back into the potatoes. Losing it is how the cake falls apart. Use a starchy potato, and mix half raw and half cooked by weight.

How it is used

Serve them hot beside a stew, or with butter if you are willing to add dairy. They are a bread as much as a side dish on a Chilote table.

Field notes

  • Milcao needs both raw and cooked potato. The raw starch is the binder.
  • Let the potato liquid settle and return the starch. Pouring it all away weakens the cake.
  • This batch has no pork. Cracklings are a separate, not-vegetarian variation.

Ingredients

  • 500 g starchy potatoes, peeled and left raw
  • 500 g starchy potatoes, cooked and mashed
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil for the skillet

Steps

  1. Grate the raw potatoes. Squeeze the liquid into a bowl and let it sit 10 minutes. Pour off the water and keep the starch that settled.
  2. Mix the grated potato, the reserved starch, the mashed potato, and the salt into a firm dough.
  3. Form 8 flat cakes.
  4. Pan-fry in oil over medium heat, turning once, until both sides are crisp and the center is cooked through, about 5 minutes a side. Break one open to check that the raw potato has turned tender.
  5. Serve hot.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Chicharrón folded into the dough is traditional in many houses. It adds meat and is not vegetarian.
  • Milcaos can be cooked in a curanto. This recipe stays in a skillet.
  • A little cooked pumpkin in the mash is a home addition on some islands.

Pairings and uses

  • Snack
  • Accompaniment to a meal
  • A fish stew, which adds fish
  • Pebre
  • Butter, which adds dairy

Deruny

Ukrainian grated-potato pancakes, fried crisp and served with sour cream.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 pancakes

History

Deruny are Ukrainian potato pancakes made from grated raw potato, onion, egg, and flour. They are everyday food, served with sour cream. They share a technique with Polish placki, Belarusian draniki, and Ashkenazi latkes, and they are not the same plate. Latkes on this journal are the Hanukkah cake, with that history. Deruny are the Ukrainian home pancake. Flour means they are not gluten-free.

Cultural background

A cast-iron pan and a fine grater are the tools. Some cooks add a little salo, cured pork fat. This recipe fries in oil and keeps the cake vegetarian, with sour cream at the table.

Why it tastes this way

Onion and potato fry together so the edge is sweet and crisp. Egg and flour hold the shreds. Sour cream cools the crust. Salt and pepper are the seasoning. If the batter sits, the potato darkens and the flavor dulls, so cook it promptly.

How it is made

Potato and onion are grated, mixed with egg, flour, and salt, and fried in oil until brown.

Authentic touch. Grate onion into the potato and fry the cakes thin enough to crisp. Sour cream is the serving. A pork-fat fry is a real variation and is not this vegetarian recipe.

How it is used

Hot, with sour cream, as a meal. They are savory.

Field notes

  • Deruny are the Ukrainian grated-potato pancakes.
  • They are related to draniki, placki, and latkes, and the histories are not identical.
  • This recipe is fried in oil and served with sour cream.

Ingredients

  • 600 g starchy potatoes, peeled
  • 1 small onion
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tbsp plain wheat flour
  • 1 tsp fine salt
  • 1/4 tsp black pepper
  • Neutral oil, for frying
  • Sour cream, for serving

Steps

  1. Grate the potatoes and onion. Squeeze lightly, leaving them moist. Stir in the egg, flour, salt, and pepper.
  2. Heat a shallow layer of oil over medium-high heat.
  3. Fry flattened rounds until deep brown on both sides and cooked through.
  4. Drain briefly and serve with sour cream.

Stay with the hot oil. Cut one cake open to be sure the potato is cooked.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Fried in salo, which adds meat and removes the vegetarian diet
  • Belarusian draniki, the next-door potato cake with its own page

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Sour cream
  • Dill
  • A mushroom sauce
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

South Korea · East Asia

Gamjajeon

A pan-fried potato cake, crisp at the lace and soft inside, with scallion and sesame.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 3 · 6 potato pancakes

History

Gamjajeon is Korea's potato pancake. Gamja means potato. Shredded potato is salted, squeezed, and fried, sometimes with a little potato starch so it holds. Wheat pancake mix is common in quick recipes. This one uses only potato and potato starch, so it stays gluten-free. It is a home side dish and a snack, plainer than seafood pajeon and sharper in texture than a mung-bean cake.

Cultural background

Potato starch or the starch that leaks from the potato itself is the binder. Rinsing every drop away makes the cake fall apart, so the method keeps that liquid. A soy dip is usual. This recipe uses gluten-free tamari so the dip does not smuggle wheat back in.

Why it tastes this way

Potato fries into a sweet, toasty lace where the shreds hit the oil, and the center stays steaming. Scallion adds a green bite. Sesame oil is the Korean pantry note that keeps it from tasting like a plain hash brown. There is no ferment and no chile in the cake. Salt and the dip do the seasoning.

How it is made

Shred potato, bind it with its own starch and a little potato starch, and pan-fry thin cakes.

Authentic touch. Squeeze the potato, then let the liquid sit and pour off the water, keeping the starch that settles. Stir that starch back in.

How it is used

Cut while hot and dip lightly. It is a side with rice or a snack. It softens as it cools.

Field notes

  • Gamja is potato. This is a potato cake, not a wheat jeon with a few cubes on top.
  • The dip uses gluten-free tamari so the plate matches the gluten-free tag.

Ingredients

  • 3 medium starchy potatoes, peeled
  • 1 tablespoon potato starch
  • 2 scallions, chopped
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Neutral oil
  • 1 teaspoon sesame oil
  • Gluten-free tamari and rice vinegar for dipping

Steps

  1. Shred the potatoes. Salt them and squeeze hard over a bowl. Let the liquid stand 5 minutes, pour off the water, and keep the starch at the bottom.
  2. Mix the shreds with that starch, the potato starch, and the scallions.
  3. Heat neutral oil with the sesame oil in a pan. Spread small thin rounds.
  4. Fry until both sides are golden and the centers are tender.
  5. Serve with a dip of gluten-free tamari and rice vinegar.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A little carrot shred for color
  • Wheat buchimgaru instead of potato starch, which adds wheat

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Snack
  • Gluten-free tamari and vinegar dip
  • Steamed rice
  • A cabbage salad
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Irish boxty

Pan boxty: raw grated potato and mashed potato, fried as cakes.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 pan cakes

History

Boxty is an Irish potato cake. There are three forms: pan boxty, loaf boxty, and dumpling boxty. This page is only the pan cake. The batter mixes raw grated potato with mashed potato and flour, then the cakes are fried. Potatoes reached Irish kitchens and became a staple. The cake is one of the ways a raw potato was cooked without waiting for a roast. Flour means this recipe is not gluten-free.

Cultural background

Pan boxty is breakfast and supper food. It is associated with the north and the midlands as much as with any single county. This page does not assign it to one village.

Why it tastes this way

Raw potato brings starch and a clean flavor. Mash brings softness. Flour binds the two. Frying browns the starch. If you rinse all the starch off the grated potato, the cake falls apart and tastes watery. Salt is the seasoning. A crisp edge and a soft center is the point.

How it is made

Equal parts raw grated potato and mashed potato are mixed with flour, salt, and a little milk, then fried as flat cakes in butter or oil.

Authentic touch. Grate the raw potato and keep the liquid that settles into starch. Pour off the water and put the starch back. That paste helps the cake hold.

How it is used

Hot, with butter, eggs, or bacon. The cake on this page is vegetarian. Bacon is a serving choice, not part of the batter.

Field notes

  • Pan, loaf, and dumpling boxty are three methods. This is the pan cake.
  • The mix is raw grated potato plus mashed potato, not mash alone.
  • The starch from the grated potato helps bind the cake.

Ingredients

  • 250 g raw potatoes, peeled
  • 250 g cold mashed potato
  • 100 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tsp fine salt
  • 60 ml milk
  • 30 g unsalted butter, for frying

Steps

  1. Grate the raw potatoes onto a clean cloth. Squeeze the liquid into a bowl. Let it sit so the starch settles, pour off the water, and keep the starch.
  2. Mix the squeezed potato, the starch, the mash, flour, salt, and enough milk to make a stiff batter.
  3. Heat the butter in a frying pan. Shape flat cakes and fry over medium heat until both sides are brown and the raw potato is cooked through, about 4 minutes a side.
  4. Rest them a minute and serve with butter or eggs.

The raw potato must cook through. Cut one cake open. The center should be soft and hot, not glassy.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Boxty loaf, baked as a bread, which is a different method
  • Boxty dumpling, boiled, which is also a different method

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • Butter
  • Fried eggs
  • Bacon, if you want a meat breakfast
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Irish potato farls

Mashed-potato farls, cut in quarters and cooked on a griddle.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 farls

History

Potato farls are a mashed-potato bread cooked on a griddle and cut into quarters. Farl means a fourth. They are an Ulster breakfast bread eaten in Ireland, which is why this page is pinned to Ireland. The wheat soda farl, a different dough, is on the Ulster soda farl page under the United Kingdom. Potato farls use mash, flour, and fat. They do not use the raw potato that defines pan boxty.

Cultural background

They are weekend breakfast food beside eggs, bacon, or soda bread. The dough should be soft and the cakes thin enough to cook through on a dry griddle.

Why it tastes this way

Mashed potato makes a mild, stretchy crumb once it meets a hot griddle. Flour keeps the shape. Butter in the dough tastes nutty when the surface toasts. They are saltier and drier than a fried potato cake, because the pan is only lightly greased.

How it is made

Cold mash is mixed with butter, salt, and flour, pressed into rounds, quartered, and cooked on a hot griddle until spotted brown.

Authentic touch. Cut the round into four before you cook it. Do not grate in raw potato. If you do, you have started a boxty, and that recipe is the other page.

How it is used

At breakfast, with butter, eggs, or a fry. The farls themselves are vegetarian.

Field notes

  • A farl is a quarter of the round.
  • This cake is mashed potato. Boxty also uses raw potato.
  • The pin is Ireland. Ulster soda farls are the wheat cake on the United Kingdom page.

Ingredients

  • 500 g cold mashed potato
  • 40 g unsalted butter
  • 1 tsp fine salt
  • 100 g plain wheat flour, plus more for pressing

Steps

  1. Beat the mash with the butter and salt. Work in the flour until you have a soft dough that does not stick.
  2. Divide in two. Press each half into a round about 1 cm thick on a floured board and cut into quarters.
  3. Cook on a hot dry griddle until brown spots show, then turn and cook the other side.
  4. Serve warm with butter.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of chopped spring onion
  • Tattie scones, the Scottish thin triangle of the same family

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • Butter
  • Eggs
  • Bacon or sausage on a meat plate
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Kartoffelpuffer

Shredded German potato pancakes, crisp, with applesauce.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 pancakes

History

Kartoffelpuffer are German shredded-potato pancakes, fried until crisp. In the Rhineland the same family is often called Reibekuchen. They are street and kitchen food, served with applesauce or with a savory garnish. This recipe binds the potato with egg and potato starch, not wheat flour, and fries it in oil, so it is gluten-free and dairy-free. Flour is a common substitute and would add a wheat allergen. The cake is vegetarian.

Cultural background

Christmas markets and home kitchens both sell them. Applesauce is the sweet side that people expect. A savory plate with herbs or smoked fish is the other direction. This page writes the applesauce plate.

Why it tastes this way

Raw potato shreds brown and crisp because the starch fries in oil. Onion, if you add it, sweetens the edge. Salt matters. Applesauce is cold, soft, and tart against a hot crust. Egg binds without making it an omelet. Squeeze out some water and keep enough starch or the cake will not hold.

How it is made

Potatoes and onion are grated, squeezed, mixed with egg, potato starch, and salt, and fried in shallow oil until crisp.

Authentic touch. Grate just before frying. Potato starch in this recipe keeps it free of wheat. If you use wheat flour instead, drop the gluten-free label. Serve applesauce on the side, not in the batter.

How it is used

Hot, with applesauce, as a snack or a light meal. Sour cream is a dairy addition and would change the diet tags.

Field notes

  • Reibekuchen is the Rhineland name in this family.
  • Applesauce is the usual sweet side.
  • This version uses potato starch, not wheat flour.

Ingredients

  • 700 g starchy potatoes, peeled
  • 1 small onion
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tbsp potato starch
  • 1 tsp fine salt
  • Neutral oil, for frying
  • Applesauce, for serving

Steps

  1. Grate the potatoes and onion. Squeeze them in a cloth, leaving them damp rather than bone-dry. Stir in the egg, potato starch, and salt.
  2. Heat a shallow layer of oil in a frying pan over medium-high heat.
  3. Fry flattened rounds until deep golden and crisp, turning once. The potato must be cooked through.
  4. Drain on a rack and serve with applesauce.

Do not leave the oil unattended. The center of a thick cake should not taste raw.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Reibekuchen, the Rhineland name for the same kind of cake
  • Wheat flour instead of potato starch, which is no longer gluten-free

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Snack
  • Street food
  • Applesauce
  • Salt
  • Chives, for a savory plate
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Lancashire potato cakes

Thick mashed-potato cakes, fried, from Lancashire breakfasts.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 cakes

History

Lancashire potato cakes are mashed potato bound with flour and fried. They are a breakfast cake, often made when there is mash left from the day before. They are not Irish boxty, which uses raw grated potato as well as mash, and they are not Scottish tattie scones, which are rolled thin and cooked on a dry girdle. This version is a thicker round, fried in butter.

Cultural background

Potato cakes of this sort belong to the industrial breakfast as much as to a farmhouse. They use a staple that stores. The flavor depends on the potato, so a watery mash makes a weak cake.

Why it tastes this way

Flour and a little butter keep mashed potato from falling apart, and frying browns the outside. The inside stays soft and salty. Pepper is the usual seasoning. There is no egg in this recipe, so the cake tastes of potato rather than of omelet.

How it is made

Cold mashed potato is worked with flour, butter, and salt, shaped into thick rounds, and fried in butter until crusted.

Authentic touch. Dry the mash. A cake that weeps in the pan was too wet. Flour is the binder here, which means the cake is not gluten-free.

How it is used

At breakfast, with eggs or bacon, or with butter and salt alone. Bacon makes the plate a meat meal.

Field notes

  • These use mashed potato only. Boxty also uses raw grated potato.
  • Leftover mash is the usual reason to make them.
  • They are fried rounds, not the thin triangles of a tattie scone.

Ingredients

  • 500 g cold mashed potato, made without much liquid
  • 80 g plain wheat flour, plus more for shaping
  • 30 g unsalted butter, melted, plus more for frying
  • 1/2 tsp fine salt
  • 1/4 tsp black pepper

Steps

  1. Mix the mash, flour, melted butter, salt, and pepper into a soft dough. Add a spoon more flour if it sticks.
  2. Shape eight thick rounds on a floured board.
  3. Fry in butter over medium heat until both sides are brown and the center is hot.
  4. Serve at once with pepper, eggs, or bacon.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of chopped chives in the mash
  • Scottish tattie scones, which are thinner and cooked more dry

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • Fried eggs
  • Bacon
  • Butter and black pepper
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Latvia · Eastern Europe

Latvian potato pancakes

Latvian pancakes of half mashed and half raw potato, with dill, fried in butter.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 pancakes

History

Latvian potato pancakes, kartupeļu pankūkas, are grated potato cakes served with sour cream. This page uses half raw grated potato and half mashed potato, with dill, so the texture is softer than an all-raw draniki and different from the Lithuanian kefir batter. Flour binds them. They are home food, not a copy of the Belarusian recipe. Butter in the pan means the cakes contain dairy.

Cultural background

Potato is a staple, and pancakes are a supper that uses it up. Dill is the herb. Sour cream is the sauce. A meat gravy is a possible side and is not required.

Why it tastes this way

Mashed potato makes the center soft. Raw potato still crisps at the edge and supplies starch. Dill tastes green and slightly sharp. Butter browns and tastes sweeter than a neutral oil. They are less brittle than a fully raw pancake and more herb-scented than the Lithuanian page.

How it is made

Raw grated potato and cold mash are mixed with egg, flour, dill, and salt, then fried in butter.

Authentic touch. Keep the half-and-half potato mix. All raw potato is a different cake. Dill goes into the batter, not only on the plate as a garnish you might forget.

How it is used

Hot, with sour cream, as a savory meal.

Field notes

  • This Latvian recipe mixes raw grated potato with mashed potato.
  • Dill is in the batter.
  • The Lithuanian page uses kefir and all raw potato. The Belarusian page uses no wheat flour.

Ingredients

  • 300 g raw potatoes, peeled
  • 300 g cold mashed potato
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tbsp plain wheat flour
  • 2 tbsp chopped fresh dill
  • 1 tsp fine salt
  • 30 g unsalted butter, for frying
  • Sour cream, for serving

Steps

  1. Grate the raw potatoes and squeeze them lightly. Mix with the mash, egg, flour, dill, and salt.
  2. Fry flattened cakes in butter over medium heat until both sides are brown and the raw potato is cooked.
  3. Keep the heat moderate so the butter browns instead of burning.
  4. Serve with sour cream.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • All raw potato, closer to draniki
  • Bacon fat for frying, which adds meat and removes the vegetarian diet

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Sour cream
  • More dill
  • A beet salad
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Lithuania · Eastern Europe

Lithuanian bulviniai blynai

Lithuanian potato pancakes with kefir in the batter, fried and served with sour cream.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 pancakes

History

Bulviniai blynai means potato pancakes in Lithuanian. They are grated potato cakes, a different dish from the Ashkenazi farmer-cheese latke pinned to Lithuania on that page. Kefir in the batter is the method here. A little wheat flour binds them, so they are not gluten-free. They are everyday food, served with sour cream or with bacon on a meat plate. Bacon is not in this recipe.

Cultural background

Potato pancakes are a home supper. The cheese latke is a holiday dairy cake with a separate history. Keeping the names apart matters, because both pages use Lithuania and they are not the same food.

Why it tastes this way

Kefir tangs the potato slightly and loosens the batter. Onion fries sweet at the edge. The crust is crisp and the center soft. Sour cream doubles the dairy note. They taste fresher and less purely starchy than a flour-only bind, because the kefir is sour.

How it is made

Grated potato and onion are mixed with kefir, egg, flour, and salt, then fried in oil.

Authentic touch. Use kefir, not sweet milk. Do not call these cheese latkes. Those are the Hanukkah page. Flour in this batter means it is not gluten-free.

How it is used

Hot, with sour cream, as a savory meal. Bacon on the side makes it a meat plate.

Field notes

  • Bulviniai blynai are potato pancakes. The cheese latke page is a different food.

Ecuador · South America

Llapingachos

Pan-fried potato cakes with cheese inside and a mild peanut sauce over the top.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 cakes

History

Llapingachos are pan-fried potato cakes from the Ecuadorian Andes, especially the central highlands around Ambato. Mashed potato is stuffed with cheese, browned in a skillet, and often served with salsa de maní, a peanut sauce. Peanuts are an Andean crop. This recipe includes the sauce, so the plate contains peanut and is not nut-free. The cheese makes it a dairy dish. A fried egg or a sausage on the side is common and is not in the written steps.

Cultural background

They are breakfast and lunch in highland towns, sold in markets and cooked at home when potatoes are the meal. Annatto colors some batches a soft orange. The sauce is onion, roasted peanut, and milk, not a fiery chile paste. Ají can be served beside it. It is not mixed in here.

Why it tastes this way

Potato and salt are the body, fried so the outside browns and the cheese in the center softens. The peanut sauce tastes toasted and slightly sweet, loosened with milk and onion. Annatto adds color and a faint earthy note, not heat. Because there is no chile in the sauce, the dish stays at no chili heat. The peanut is obvious. Anyone who avoids peanuts should not make this version.

How it is made

Mash boiled potatoes with salt and a little annatto. Seal cubes of cheese inside cakes and pan-fry them. Cook onion and ground roasted peanuts with milk into a sauce and spoon it over.

Authentic touch. Chill the mashed potato before you stuff it, or the cheese leaks before a crust can form. Seal the cakes well. Simmer the peanut sauce until it is thick enough to spoon, not a broth, and salt it.

How it is used

Serve two cakes with sauce for breakfast or lunch. A fried egg on top adds the egg allergen. Chorizo adds meat and is not vegetarian. Neither is in this pan.

Field notes

  • The peanut sauce means this recipe is not nut-free.
  • Cheese inside the potato cake softens. It does not have to stretch like a pizza.
  • Ambato and the central highlands are the landscape people name with llapingachos.

Ingredients

  • 700 g potatoes, peeled
  • 1 teaspoon salt, divided
  • Pinch of ground annatto
  • 120 g fresh white cheese, in 8 cubes
  • 80 g roasted peanuts
  • 1 small onion, chopped
  • 120 ml milk
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil

Steps

  1. Boil the potatoes until tender. Drain, mash, and season with 1/2 teaspoon salt and the annatto. Cool until you can handle the mash.
  2. Divide into 8 portions. Seal a cube of cheese inside each and flatten into a thick cake.
  3. Pan-fry in the oil over medium heat until both sides are browned and the center is hot.
  4. Meanwhile, cook the onion in a spoon of oil, add the peanuts, milk, and remaining salt, and simmer. Mash or blend the sauce until thick.
  5. Spoon the peanut sauce over the cakes and serve.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Skip the peanut sauce and the allergen list loses peanut, and the plate can be nut-free. It is a plainer dish.
  • A fried egg is a usual topping and adds egg.
  • Chorizo on the side adds meat and is not vegetarian.

  • Breakfast
  • Street food
  • Ají on the side, which adds heat
  • A small salad of onion and tomato
  • Black coffee

Norwegian lefse

Potato lefse, rolled thin and griddled, soft enough to fold with butter and sugar.

1 hr 30 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 8 · 8 large sheets

History

This page is potato lefse, the soft Norwegian flatbread of boiled potato, butter, and flour, rolled thin and cooked on a dry griddle. There are other lefse, some without potato. Lompe is a smaller potato cousin often wrapped around a hot dog. Norwegian-American tables, especially in the Upper Midwest, are a later home of potato lefse, not the origin. The flour means this recipe is not gluten-free.

Cultural background

Lefse is holiday food and family food. Rolling it thin is a skill, often done with a grooved rolling pin and a cloth-covered board. The cakes are stacked under a cloth so they stay flexible.

Why it tastes this way

Potato keeps the sheet soft and mild. Butter in the dough tastes warm when the griddle spots it. Flour is there for strength, and too much of it makes the lefse tough and pale. Sugar and more butter at the table are the usual sweet serving. The cake itself is only lightly salted.

How it is made

Potatoes are boiled, riced, and mixed with butter, salt, and flour. The dough is chilled, rolled very thin, and griddled until spotted.

Authentic touch. Chill the potato dough before you roll it. Cook on a dry, hot griddle until brown spots appear, and stack the finished sheets in a towel. A cloth under the dough helps you turn the thin round.

How it is used

Cooled slightly, spread with butter and sugar, folded, as a snack or a holiday table bread. Savory uses exist. This page writes the butter-and-sugar fold.

Field notes

  • This is potato lefse, rolled thin and griddled.
  • Tables in the American Upper Midwest are a later home, not the origin.
  • Lompe is the smaller potato wrap. It is related and not the same sheet.

Ingredients

  • 700 g starchy potatoes, peeled and cut into chunks
  • 40 g unsalted butter
  • 1 tsp fine salt
  • 200 g plain wheat flour, plus more for rolling
  • Butter and sugar, for serving

Steps

  1. Boil the potatoes until tender. Drain well and rice or mash them dry. Stir in the butter and salt. Cool completely. Chilling the mash for an hour makes rolling easier.
  2. Work in the flour until you have a soft dough that does not stick badly. Do not add more flour than the dough needs.
  3. Roll a ball very thin on a floured cloth. Cook on a hot dry griddle until brown spots appear, then turn and spot the other side.
  4. Stack in a clean towel. Serve with butter and sugar, folded.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Lefse without potato, a different bread
  • Lompe, smaller and thicker, used as a wrap

Pairings and uses

  • Snack
  • Accompaniment to a meal
  • Feast or fast day
  • Butter and sugar
  • Cinnamon sugar
  • A hot dog inside lompe, which is the smaller cousin and not this sheet

Pennsylvania Dutch potato pancakes

Shredded potato, onion, and pepper, crisp at the edge and soft in the middle.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 potato cakes

History

Pennsylvania Dutch potato pancakes are shredded-potato cakes griddled by German-speaking communities in Pennsylvania. Pennsylvania Dutch means Deutsch, not a recipe from the Netherlands. The cakes are cooked on a griddle, with crisp edges and a soft center. Lancaster County is the map pin.

Cultural background

Potato cakes traveled with German-speaking cooks and became everyday food, not only a holiday plate. They are related to other shredded-potato cakes and are not the same page as a Jewish latke.

Why it tastes this way

Potato starch that stays in the bowl is part of what holds the cake together. Rinsing the shreds until the water runs clear can make them fall apart. Onion browns at the edge and keeps the potato from tasting flat. The griddle, with only a thin film of oil, fries the surface without deep-frying the center.

How it is made

Shredded potato and onion griddled in a thin film of oil.

Authentic touch. Griddle them, do not deep-fry them. Squeeze excess water from the potatoes and leave the white starch in the bowl.

How it is used

Hot, with applesauce or sour cream if you want dairy at the table. The cakes themselves have no milk or butter.

Field notes

  • Pennsylvania Dutch comes from Deutsch, meaning German, not from the Netherlands.
  • These cakes are griddled. The funnel cake from the same communities is the fried one.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 pounds russet potatoes
  • 1 small yellow onion
  • 2 large eggs
  • 3 tablespoons all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/4 teaspoon black pepper
  • 3 tablespoons neutral oil

Steps

  1. Peel the potatoes and grate them on the large holes of a box grater. Grate the onion the same way.
  2. Squeeze the mixture in a towel over a bowl. Pour off the water that collects and keep the white starch at the bottom of the bowl.
  3. Stir the potato, onion, starch, eggs, flour, salt, and pepper together.
  4. Heat a thin film of oil in a skillet over medium heat.
  5. Drop 1/4 cup mounds, flatten them, and cook until the underside is deep brown, about 3 to 4 minutes. Flip and cook until the center is tender.
  6. Add oil as needed. Serve hot.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Add a spoon of minced parsley for a greener cake.
  • Flour can be reduced if the potatoes are very starchy, but do not rinse the starch away.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • Accompaniment to a meal
  • Applesauce
  • Sour cream, if you eat dairy
  • Chives
  • Vegetarian
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Placki ziemniaczane

Polish potato pancakes, shredded and fried, with sugar or sour cream.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 pancakes

History

Placki ziemniaczane are Poland's everyday shredded-potato pancakes. They are fried crisp and eaten with sugar, sour cream, or a savory stew. They use the same raw-potato technique as deruny and as Ashkenazi latkes. The latke page is the Hanukkah history. This page is the Polish home and street pancake, not a holiday claim. Flour and egg bind them. They are fried in oil, so the cake itself is dairy-free. Sour cream is a topping, not an ingredient.

Cultural background

They are sold at counters and made at home, often from a bowl of grated potato that has to be cooked before it turns gray. Sugar on a potato pancake surprises people who expect only sour cream. Both are served in Poland.

Why it tastes this way

The crust is salty, browned starch. Onion makes it savory. Sugar on top is a direct contrast, grainy and sweet against the salt. Sour cream is the softer contrast. The inside should be tender, not raw. Too much flour makes them bready.

How it is made

Potato and onion are grated, mixed with egg, flour, and salt, and fried in oil until crisp.

Authentic touch. Offer sugar and sour cream as two real options, not as a mistake. Keep the cakes thin. This is not the Hanukkah latke page, even though a cook could mistake one pan for the other.

How it is used

Hot, with sugar or with sour cream. A meat goulash beside them is a meal and adds meat to the plate.

Field notes

  • Placki ziemniaczane are the Polish potato pancakes of ordinary days.
  • Sugar and sour cream are both served on them.
  • The Hanukkah latke has its own page and its own history.

  • 1 small onion
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tbsp plain wheat flour
  • 1 tsp fine salt
  • Neutral oil, for frying
  • Sugar or sour cream, for serving

Steps

  1. Grate the potatoes and onion, squeeze lightly, and mix with the egg, flour, and salt.
  2. Heat a shallow layer of oil over medium-high heat.
  3. Fry thin rounds until both sides are brown and the potato is cooked.
  4. Serve at once with sugar or sour cream.

Potato latkes

Ashkenazi potato pancakes for Hanukkah, fried in oil, with the older cheese custom named beside them.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 latkes

History

Potato latkes are the Ashkenazi Hanukkah pancake of grated potato, onion, egg, and flour or matzo meal, fried in oil. Potatoes became common in Eastern Europe in the 19th century. Cheese pancakes are the older Hanukkah dairy custom, tied in Ashkenazi teaching to Judith, who in the Book of Judith fed an enemy general salty cheese and wine. That cheese cake has its own page. This one is the potato cake, and it is not gluten-free, because flour or matzo meal is wheat. The pin is Poland. The same cakes are cooked in Ukraine, the United States, and Israel.

Cultural background

Hanukkah food is fried, a reminder of oil in the Temple story. A dairy-free latke fried in oil can sit on a meat meal. Sour cream is a dairy topping and belongs on a dairy meal, not beside meat. Applesauce keeps the plate neutral.

Why it tastes this way

Oil frying makes the crust crisp and savory. Onion sweetens it. The inside stays soft if the shreds were not ground to a paste. Salt and pepper are the seasoning. Applesauce is tart and cool. Because there is no baking powder, the flavor is potato, onion, and browned oil, not a diner pancake.

How it is made

Potato and onion are grated, squeezed, mixed with egg, flour, and salt, and fried in oil until crisp.

Authentic touch. Use flour or matzo meal and say so: this is not gluten-free. Fry in oil, not butter, if the latkes need to stay dairy-free for a meat meal. Keep the starch that settles from the grated potato.

How it is used

Hot at Hanukkah, with applesauce, or with sour cream when the meal is dairy. They are a feast food.

Field notes

  • Cheese latkes are the older Ashkenazi Hanukkah dairy custom. Potato latkes spread after potatoes did.
  • Potatoes became common in Eastern Europe in the 19th century.
  • Flour or matzo meal means these latkes are not gluten-free.

Ingredients

  • 700 g starchy potatoes, peeled
  • 1 onion
  • 1 large egg
  • 3 tbsp plain wheat flour or matzo meal
  • 1 tsp fine salt
  • 1/4 tsp black pepper
  • Neutral oil, for frying
  • Applesauce, for serving

Steps

  1. Grate the potatoes and onion. Collect the liquid, let the starch settle, pour off the water, and return the starch to the potato.
  2. Stir in the egg, flour or matzo meal, salt, and pepper.
  3. Fry flattened spoonfuls in hot oil until both sides are deep golden and the centers are cooked.
  4. Drain on a rack and serve with applesauce, or with sour cream if the meal is dairy.

Fry in enough oil to brown the cakes and do not leave the pan. Drain them so they stay crisp.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • The older farmer-cheese latke, on its own page
  • Matzo meal instead of flour, still wheat

Pairings and uses

  • Feast or fast day
  • Savory meal
  • Applesauce
  • Sour cream, on a dairy meal
  • Salt

Raggmunk

Crisp Swedish potato pancakes from raw grated potato, with lingonberries.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 pancakes

History

Raggmunk is a Swedish potato pancake made with raw grated potato in a batter of flour, egg, and milk. It fries crisp at the edge. The usual plate adds fried pork and lingonberries. This page keeps the pancake vegetarian and names the pork as the traditional accompaniment, not as an ingredient. It is not äggakaka, which is an egg cake, and it is not lefse.

Cultural background

Potato pancakes of this kind are weekday food in Sweden. The raw potato has to be grated just before cooking or it darkens. A crisp raggmunk and a soft one are both defended. This recipe aims for a lacy, crisp edge.

Why it tastes this way

Potato starch browns and crisps. Milk and egg make the middle tender. Salt and white pepper season it. Lingonberry jam is the sour-sweet contrast, the same idea as applesauce beside a German potato pancake, and not the same plate. The wheat flour binds and means this is not gluten-free.

How it is made

Raw potato is grated into a batter of flour, egg, and milk, then fried thin in butter until the edges are brown.

Authentic touch. Grate the potato on a fine side and do not wash all the starch away. Spread the batter thin if you want the crisp version. Serve the jam on the side, not cooked into the batter.

How it is used

Hot, with lingonberry jam. Fried pork is the traditional meat side and is not in this batter.

Field notes

  • Raggmunk uses raw grated potato in a batter, then fries crisp.
  • Lingonberries, and often pork, are the plate. This recipe leaves the pork as a choice.
  • It is not the Scanian äggakaka and not Norwegian lefse.

Ingredients

  • 500 g raw potatoes, peeled
  • 1 large egg
  • 100 ml whole milk
  • 60 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tsp fine salt
  • 1/4 tsp white pepper
  • 40 g unsalted butter, for frying
  • Lingonberry jam, for serving

Steps

  1. Grate the potatoes. Stir in the egg, milk, flour, salt, and pepper at once so the potato does not sit and darken.
  2. Heat butter in a frying pan over medium-high heat.
  3. Spread thin rounds and fry until the edges are brown and crisp. Turn and cook through.
  4. Serve hot with lingonberry jam.

Swiss rösti

A shredded potato cake from Switzerland, pan-fried as one round. Not a batter pancake.

40 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 1 large cake, 4 wedges

History

Rösti is a Swiss shredded-potato cake, fried as a round and served whole or in wedges. It is not a batter pancake. There is no flour and no egg in this recipe. The potatoes are parboiled, chilled, grated, and fried in butter. Bern's version often includes bacon. This page writes the plain cake and names bacon as a variation, so the main recipe stays vegetarian. The word Röstigraben is a common name for the cultural line between German-speaking and French-speaking Switzerland, taken from this potato cake.

Cultural background

Rösti is a meal beside fried eggs or a supplement beside meat and cheese. It is everyday food in German-speaking cantons, not a festival-only dish. The crust is the point.

Why it tastes this way

Parboiled potato gratings brown into a crust while the inside steams and softens. Butter tastes nutty when it browns. Salt is the seasoning. There is no egg to make it cakey and no flour to make it bready. A raw-potato rösti exists in some kitchens. This page uses the chilled, parboiled method because it shreds cleanly and crisps.

How it is made

Waxy potatoes are boiled in their skins, chilled, peeled, grated, seasoned, and fried in butter as one cake until a deep crust forms on both sides.

Authentic touch. Say plainly that this is a potato cake, not a poured pancake. Press the shreds into one round and leave them alone long enough to crust before you turn the cake. Bacon is the Bern variation, not a requirement.

How it is used

As a savory meal or a side, with eggs, cheese, or a salad. Bacon on top is the Bern-style variation and adds meat.

Field notes

  • Rösti is shredded potato fried as a cake. It is not a batter pancake.
  • This recipe has no flour and no egg.
  • Bacon is typical of Bern's version. The plain cake is written here.

Ingredients

  • 800 g waxy potatoes
  • 1 tsp fine salt
  • 40 g unsalted butter
  • 1 tbsp neutral oil

Steps

  1. Boil the potatoes in their skins until just tender. Drain, chill until cold, then peel and grate coarsely.
  2. Toss the gratings with salt.
  3. Heat the butter and oil in a wide nonstick pan. Add the potato and press it into an even round. Cook over medium heat until the bottom is a deep crust, about 10 minutes.
  4. Turn the cake onto a plate and slide it back to brown the other side. Cut in wedges.

Turning a large rösti is easier with a plate. The pan handle will be hot.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Bärner Rösti, with bacon, which adds a meat allergen and is not vegetarian
  • Raw grated potato instead of parboiled, a different and also real method

  • Accompaniment to a meal

Tattie scones

Thin potato triangles from a Scottish girdle, eaten at breakfast.

15 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 triangles

History

Tattie scones are Scottish potato scones: mashed potato, a little flour and fat, rolled thin, cut into triangles, and cooked on a girdle. Tattie means potato. They are part of a fried breakfast or a weekend plate with eggs. They are not Lancashire's thicker fried rounds, and they are not boxty. Flour in the dough means they are not gluten-free.

Cultural background

Cold mash from the night before is the usual start. The scones are cooked in a dry or lightly greased pan so the outside toasts without becoming a fritter.

Why it tastes this way

Potato makes them mild and soft. A thin roll means the heat reaches the center and the surface dries. Salt and pepper matter because there is little else. Browning at the edge is the flavor. Butter on the hot scone melts into the face that was against the girdle.

How it is made

Mashed potato is kneaded with butter and flour, rolled into a thin round, quartered, and cooked on a hot girdle until spotted.

Authentic touch. Cut the round into quarters. A farl here is a practical cut, not the Ulster soda farl. Keep them thin enough that you can see the girdle color through the dough at the edge.

How it is used

At breakfast, with eggs, bacon, or butter. They are a savory cake.

Field notes

  • Tattie is potato. The scones are potato, not a sweet oven scone.
  • They are rolled thin and cut in triangles or quarters.
  • They belong on a Scottish breakfast plate.

Ingredients

  • 400 g cold mashed potato
  • 30 g unsalted butter, plus more for serving
  • 80 g plain wheat flour, plus more for rolling
  • 1/2 tsp fine salt
  • 1/4 tsp black pepper

Steps

  1. Mash the potato with the butter, salt, and pepper until smooth. Work in the flour to a soft dough.
  2. Roll into two thin rounds on a floured board and cut each into quarters.
  3. Cook on a hot dry girdle until brown spots appear, then turn and cook the other side.
  4. Serve warm with butter, eggs, or bacon.
