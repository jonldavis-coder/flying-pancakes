Boxty

Half grated raw potato, half mashed potato, browned in a pan.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 cakes

History

Boxty is an Irish potato bread especially associated with Ulster, Connacht, and the northwest. A traditional rhyme says boxty on the griddle and boxty in the pan. There are pan cakes, baked loaves, and boiled dumplings. This page is the pan cake: raw grated potato and mashed potato together. Potatoes became a central Irish food in the eighteenth century, and boxty is one of the breads that came out of that dependence, including through the years around the Famine.

Cultural background

It is a meal with butter, or with bacon and eggs. The raw potato keeps it from tasting like a leftover-mash patty.

Why it tastes this way

Grated raw potato brings water and a clean starch that crisps. Mashed potato brings a soft, cooked sweetness. Together they taste more potato than a flour pancake and more tender than a latke made only of shreds. The wheat flour is only a binder. Ireland’s cool, wet climate favored the potato as a staple, which is why a bread was built from it.

How it is made

Equal parts squeezed raw potato and mashed potato are mixed with a little flour, egg, and milk, then fried as cakes.

Authentic touch. Squeeze the grated potato dry, and keep the starch that settles from the liquid. Stir the batter just before frying. It darkens if it waits.

How it is used

Butter, or beside bacon and eggs. Some cooks serve it with a fried egg on top.

Field notes

  • Pan, griddle, and boiled boxty are three methods. This recipe is the pan cake.
  • Use a floury potato for the mash and a dry grate. A waxy potato stays gluey.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups grated raw potato, squeezed dry
  • 1 cup cold mashed potato
  • 1/2 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 large egg
  • 1/4 cup milk
  • 3/4 teaspoon salt
  • Butter or oil for frying

Steps

  1. Squeeze the grated potato in a towel. Add back any settled starch.
  2. Mix with mash, flour, egg, milk, and salt.
  3. Heat a buttered skillet over medium.
  4. Fry flattened rounds until brown on both sides and cooked through.
  5. Keep the heat moderate so the raw potato cooks before the crust burns.
  6. Serve hot with butter.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Boxty bread is the same idea baked as a round loaf.
  • Boxty dumplings are boiled. They are a different dish from the pan cake.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Supper
  • Side dish

  • bacon

Bramboráky

Potato pancakes seasoned with marjoram and garlic.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 cakes

History

Bramboráky are Czech potato pancakes, and the seasoning is the point: marjoram and garlic, sometimes a little caraway. They are not plain latkes. They are served as a side or a meatless main, and they are a recognized national potato dish.

Cultural background

Marjoram is the herb Czech cooks reach for in potato dishes and soups. The cake should taste of it, not of a generic fry.

Why it tastes this way

Marjoram and garlic make the potato savory and slightly perfumed, which is why bramboráky do not need applesauce to have a point. The herb is dried in winter, when potatoes are the staple. Flour and egg bind a cake that is thicker than a lace cookie and crisp at the edge. The flavor is potato plus that herb, a combination worth keeping distinct from German puffer.

How it is made

Grated potato is mixed with egg, flour, garlic, and marjoram and fried.

Authentic touch. Use dried marjoram if that is what you have, and crush it so it does not stay in needles. Fry the cakes through. Raw garlic and raw potato in the center are the failure.

How it is used

Hot, as a side or a main, with a salad or with meat.

Field notes

  • Marjoram is the characteristic seasoning of bramboráky.
  • They are a different dish from palačinky.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 pounds potatoes
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 teaspoon dried marjoram
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons flour
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • Oil for frying

Steps

  1. Grate and squeeze the potatoes.
  2. Mix with garlic, marjoram, egg, flour, and salt.
  3. Fry flattened cakes until deep brown and cooked through.
  4. Drain.
  5. Serve hot.
  6. The center should not taste starchy-raw.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A pinch of caraway is a regional addition.
  • Without marjoram they are just potato cakes. Keep the herb.

Pairings and uses

  • Side dish
  • Supper
  • salad
  • sour cream
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Bulviniai Blynai

Potato pancakes, crisp, served with sour cream or bacon.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 10 cakes

History

Bulviniai blynai are Lithuanian potato pancakes. Like their neighbors’ potato cakes, they are grated potato fried in fat, served with sour cream, bacon, or sometimes a sweet accompaniment. They are a staple supper.

Cultural background

Bacon on the side is common and adds meat. The written cake is vegetarian. Sour cream is the usual sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Potato, fried, is the calorie and the flavor of a northern winter diet. A little flour keeps the cake together for a family skillet. Sour cream cools it. The Baltic potato pancake is a family of dishes. The Lithuanian name is worth keeping so it is not folded into a single “Eastern European latke.”

How it is made

Grated potato, egg, and a little flour are fried as cakes.

Authentic touch. Squeeze, then fry in a real film of oil. Pale, soft cakes are underfried bulviniai blynai.

How it is used

Sour cream, hot.

Field notes

  • These are the potato blynai, not the yeasted wheat ones.
  • Sour cream is the partner to write down first.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 pounds potatoes
  • 1 small onion
  • 1 large egg
  • 1 tablespoon flour
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • Oil for frying

Steps

  1. Grate and squeeze potato and onion.
  2. Mix with egg, flour, and salt.
  3. Fry until crisp on both sides.
  4. Drain.
  5. Serve with sour cream.
  6. Keep cakes in a single layer so they stay crisp.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Bacon fat is a traditional frying fat and adds meat.
  • Wheat blynai are the yeasted pancakes.

Pairings and uses

  • Supper
  • Side dish
  • sour cream
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Deruny

Crisp shredded-potato pancakes with onion.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 10 cakes

History

Deruny are Ukrainian potato pancakes, grated potato and onion fried until crisp. Belarusian draniki and Jewish latkes are relatives, and the dishes are not identical in seasoning, holiday, or community. Deruny are everyday food, served with sour cream. They are also called terchuny in some places.

Cultural background

They are a meal with sour cream or mushrooms, not a Hanukkah symbol on this page. The potato itself is the shared eastern European fact.

Why it tastes this way

Grated potato fried in oil tastes sweet and browned because the starch contacts the pan. Onion adds savor. Ukraine’s reliance on the potato as a staple crop is why a pancake, not a wheat loaf, was the fast meal. Sour cream is the dairy of the region, cooling the crust. This version uses no wheat, only egg and the potato’s own starch.

How it is made

Grated potato and onion are squeezed, mixed with egg and salt, and fried.

Authentic touch. Squeeze the potato dry. Fry thin enough to crisp. Salt after they leave the pan so the crust stays dry.

How it is used

Sour cream. Mushroom sauce is a fair partner and is extra cooking.

Field notes

  • Deruny, draniki, and latkes are related potato cakes with different tables.
  • Sour cream is the usual Ukrainian topping.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 pounds potatoes
  • 1 small onion
  • 1 large egg
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • Oil for frying

Steps

  1. Grate potato and onion and squeeze dry.
  2. Mix with egg and salt.
  3. Fry flattened spoonfuls in hot oil until crisp on both sides.
  4. Drain and salt.
  5. Serve with sour cream.
  6. Do not crowd the pan.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of flour is common and means the cakes are not gluten-free.
  • Draniki are the Belarusian cousin.

Pairings and uses

  • Side dish

Draniki

Crisp potato pancakes, a Belarusian national dish, with sour cream.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 10 cakes

History

Draniki are Belarusian potato pancakes and one of the country’s best-known dishes. Grated potato is fried in cakes and served with sour cream. Pork cracklings appear in some versions. Belarus’s cooking is deeply tied to the potato, and draniki are the pancake of that fact.

Cultural background

They are home food and restaurant food. Sour cream is the sauce. A mushroom topping is also common. This recipe is the plain cake.

Why it tastes this way

The flavor is potato pushed to a crisp, because the cake is often made with little or no flour so the starch of the potato does the binding. Sour cream is the cool dairy against the fry. A potato-centered cuisine, in a cold climate where the tuber stored through winter, produced a national pancake that is not a dessert.

How it is made

Grated potato and onion are mixed with salt and egg and fried crisp.

Authentic touch. Squeeze very dry and fry in hot oil. Draniki should shatter a little at the edge. If they are soft, they are not done.

How it is used

Sour cream, immediately.

Field notes

  • Draniki are a Belarusian national potato dish.
  • Sour cream is the standard partner.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 pounds potatoes
  • 1 small onion
  • 1 large egg
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • Oil for frying

Steps

  1. Grate and squeeze dry.
  2. Mix with egg and salt.
  3. Fry thin cakes in hot oil until deep golden.
  4. Drain.
  5. Serve with sour cream.
  6. Do not cover them while they wait or they soften.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Cracklings in the batter are a meat version.
  • Deruny are the Ukrainian relative.

Pairings and uses

South Korea · East Asia

Gamjajeon

A Korean potato pancake, crisp, with a soy dip.

20 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 cakes

History

Gamjajeon is a Korean potato pancake, gamja meaning potato. Grated potato is mixed with a little flour and egg and fried, then dipped in soy sauce. It is a jeon, in the same broad family as pajeon, and a cousin of latkes with a Korean seasoning and sauce.

Cultural background

It is a side or a snack. The potato should be squeezed. The sauce is salty and vinegary.

Why it tastes this way

Potato starch crisps, and a little wheat flour helps the cake hold in a jeon style. Soy and vinegar are the Korean finish, not applesauce or sour cream, which is the clearest difference from the European potato cakes in this journal. Onion, if you add it, is the same savory note those cakes use. The pan and the dip are what make it gamjajeon.

How it is made

Grated potato, egg, and a little flour are fried and served with a soy dip.

Authentic touch. Squeeze the potato and fry thin. Salt the dip, not the cake too heavily, or it will be briny twice.

How it is used

Hot, with the dipping sauce.

Field notes

  • Gamja means potato. Jeon is the pancake category.
  • The soy dip, not sour cream, is the Korean plate.

Ingredients

  • 2 medium potatoes
  • 1 egg
  • 2 tablespoons flour
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Oil
  • 2 tablespoons soy sauce
  • 1 tablespoon vinegar

Steps

  1. Grate and squeeze the potatoes. Mix with egg, flour, and salt.
  2. Fry thin cakes until crisp.
  3. Mix soy and vinegar for the dip.
  4. Serve immediately.
  5. A few sesame seeds on the dip are optional and add sesame.
  6. Do not cover the cakes.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A few slices of chile on top are a home addition.
  • Latkes are the Ashkenazi cousin with a different sauce and holiday.

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Side dish
  • soy dipping sauce
  • Vegetarian
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Irish Potato Farls

Soft potato flatbread, spotted brown, with a floury face.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 farls

History

Potato farls are a northern Irish griddle bread made from mashed potato and flour, cut into quarters. “Farl” means a quarter. They are an Ulster breakfast bread, cousins of boxty but smoother, because the potato is cooked first. Scottish tattie scones are a close relative. The bread belongs to the potato diet of Ireland and to the griddle that also cooked soda farls.

Cultural background

An Ulster fry often includes a farl alongside soda bread. The farl itself can be vegetarian. The fry around it usually is not.

Why it tastes this way

Mashed potato and flour, cooked dry on a griddle, taste mild and slightly sweet, with a fried spot where the surface dries. There is no egg and often no milk, so the flavor is potato rather than batter. The north’s breakfast griddle, used for several breads in one morning, is why farls are flat and quick instead of oven-risen.

How it is made

Hot mashed potato is mixed with flour and salt, rolled into a round, quartered, and cooked on a dry or lightly floured griddle.

Authentic touch. The dough should be soft and rolled thicker than a cracker, about a half inch. A dry dough cracks. Cook it on a moderate griddle so the center heats before the flour scorches.

How it is used

Butter, or as part of a breakfast fry. They are a bread.

Field notes

  • Farl means a quarter of the round.
  • Potato farls and boxty both use potato. Farls start with mash only.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups warm mashed potato, plain
  • 1/2 cup all-purpose flour, plus more for rolling
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Mix the warm mash, flour, and salt into a soft dough.
  2. Roll on a floured board into a round about 1/2 inch thick. Cut into quarters.
  3. Heat a dry griddle over medium.
  4. Cook the farls until spotted brown, a few minutes a side.
  5. They should be hot in the center.
  6. Serve with butter.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A knob of butter in the mash is richer and adds dairy.
  • Tattie scones in Scotland are the close cousin.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Bread course
  • butter
  • eggs
  • bacon
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan

Germany · Western Europe

Kartoffelpuffer

Crisp potato pancakes served with applesauce.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 10 cakes

History

Kartoffelpuffer are German potato pancakes, also called Reibekuchen in the Rhineland. They are Christmas-market food and home food, served with applesauce. Grated potato, onion, egg, and a little flour are fried flat. Pennsylvania Dutch potato pancakes come from this family.

Cultural background

At a market they are eaten standing up, with a cup of applesauce. At home they are a side or a meatless supper. Sour cream is less universal here than applesauce.

Why it tastes this way

Potato starch browns into a sweet crust, and applesauce is the tart partner that makes the richness make sense. A little flour keeps the market cake in one piece while you walk. The Rhineland name Reibekuchen points at grating, which is the whole method. Onion is the savor. Christmas markets did not invent the cake. They kept it in public.

How it is made

Grated potato and onion are mixed with egg and flour and fried crisp.

Authentic touch. Squeeze the potato, and fry in enough fat that the edge laces. Press them thin. Thick puffer steam.

How it is used

Applesauce. Sugar is a lesser habit. Applesauce is the one to write down.

Field notes

  • Applesauce is the classic partner, including at Christmas markets.
  • Reibekuchen and Kartoffelpuffer are regional names, not two unrelated foods.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 pounds potatoes
  • 1 small onion
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons flour
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • Oil for frying

Steps

  1. Grate and squeeze the potato and onion.
  2. Mix with egg, flour, and salt.
  3. Fry thin cakes in hot oil until deep brown.
  4. Drain.
  5. Serve with applesauce.
  6. Salt a little more if the applesauce is very sweet.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Reibekuchen is the Rhineland name for the same idea.
  • Pennsylvania Dutch potato pancakes are the American descendant.

Pairings and uses

  • Side dish
  • Supper
  • Street food
  • applesauce
  • Vegetarian

Lancashire Potato Cakes

Buttery mashed potato and flour, griddled in spots.

15 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 cakes

History

Lancashire potato cakes are mashed potato, flour, and butter, rolled and cooked on a griddle. They are a northwest English breakfast bread, close to Irish potato farls and Scottish tattie scones. Butter in the dough is typical, which makes them richer than a lean farl.

Cultural background

They are eaten hot with more butter, or with bacon and eggs. Leftover mash is the usual reason to make them.

Why it tastes this way

Butter in the dough browns and tastes sweet against the potato. Flour makes a rollable bread rather than a fried shred cake. The northwest of England, like Ireland, put potatoes on the breakfast griddle because the tuber was the cheap staple and the griddle was already hot.

How it is made

Mashed potato, butter, and flour are rolled, cut, and cooked on a griddle.

Authentic touch. Use dry mash. Buttery, wet mash needs more flour and then tastes doughy. Cool it slightly so the butter does not melt into a slick before you roll.

How it is used

More butter, while they are hot.

Field notes

  • Leftover mash is the traditional start. Freshly boiled potatoes should be dried in the pan first.
  • They are a bread, not a syrup pancake.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups plain mashed potato
  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • 3/4 cup all-purpose flour, plus more to roll
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Work the butter and salt into the mash, then add flour to a soft dough.
  2. Roll about 1/4 inch thick and cut rounds or squares.
  3. Cook on a medium dry griddle until spotted brown on both sides.
  4. The center should be hot.
  5. Serve with butter.
  6. They are best the same day.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A pinch of thyme is a savory home addition.
  • Irish potato farls are the close relative, often with less butter in the dough.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Bread course
  • butter
  • bacon
  • eggs

Latvian Potato Pancakes

Simple grated-potato cakes with sour cream.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 10 cakes

History

Latvian potato pancakes, kartupeļu pankūkas, are the local member of the Baltic potato-cake family. They are home food, served with sour cream or a meat sauce. This version stays with egg and potato so it is gluten-free, which some Latvian recipes that add flour are not.

Cultural background

They are a weeknight supper in potato country. The method matches the neighbors. The page exists so Latvia is not represented only by a footnote.

Why it tastes this way

The flavor is fried potato and onion, the same agricultural fact as in Lithuania and Poland: the tuber became the staple. Sour cream is the dairy sauce. Leaving out wheat makes the potato taste clearer and makes a cake that is a little more fragile. That is an honest trade.

How it is made

Grated potato and onion are fried in thin cakes with egg as the binder.

Authentic touch. Grate finely for a tighter cake or coarsely for a lacier one. Squeeze either way. Do not stack them while they are still steaming or the crust dies.

How it is used

Sour cream.

Field notes

  • Kartupeļu pankūkas are Latvia’s potato pancakes.
  • This written recipe uses no wheat.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 pounds potatoes
  • 1 small onion
  • 1 large egg
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • Oil for frying

Steps

  1. Grate, squeeze, and mix with egg and salt.
  2. Fry thin cakes until brown and crisp.
  3. Drain.
  4. Serve with sour cream.
  5. Salt to taste.
  6. Eat them promptly.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Flour-bound versions are common and are not gluten-free.
  • A meat gravy is a heartier plate.

Pairings and uses

  • Supper
  • Side dish

Lefse

A soft potato flatbread, lightly browned in spots.

40 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 8 · 8 rounds

History

Lefse is a Norwegian potato flatbread cooked on a griddle. It traveled with Norwegian immigrants to the Upper Midwest of the United States, where church basements still roll it for holidays. There are several lefse types in Norway. This is the potato lefse most Norwegian-Americans mean: mashed potato, flour, a little fat, rolled thin.

Cultural background

It is served with butter and sugar, or butter and cinnamon sugar, folded or rolled. It is a bread and a holiday food, not a syrup stack. The Midwest page of the journal is this same bread, cooked far from Norway.

Why it tastes this way

Potato keeps the bread moist and mild, and a dry griddle spots it brown without frying it hard. Butter and sugar on a warm lefse melt into the cloth-soft crumb. Immigrants kept the bread because it marked Christmas and church, and because potatoes and flour were available on the prairie. The taste is potato, wheat, and browned spots, then whatever you spread.

How it is made

Cold mashed potato is mixed with butter, cream, and flour, rolled into rounds, and cooked on a dry griddle.

Authentic touch. Chill the mashed potato before you add flour, and roll it thin enough to see the board almost through it. Thick lefse tastes like dough. A sock-covered rolling pin is the traditional anti-stick tool. Parchment and cold dough also work.

How it is used

Butter and sugar, folded, while still flexible.

Field notes

  • Upper Midwest church lefse is this Norwegian bread in a new country.
  • Roll it thin. Thickness is the usual failure.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups riced cold mashed potato
  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • 2 tablespoons cream
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup all-purpose flour, plus more for rolling

Steps

  1. Mix potato, butter, cream, and salt. Work in enough flour to make a soft dough.
  2. Divide into 8 pieces and roll very thin on a floured cloth or board.
  3. Cook on a dry medium-hot griddle until spotted, about a minute a side.
  4. Stack under a towel so they stay flexible.
  5. Spread with butter and sugar and fold.
  6. Unused rounds can be frozen.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Lompe is a smaller potato lefse often wrapped around a hot dog.
  • Hardanger lefse and other regional Norwegian lefses differ. This is the soft potato one.

Pairings and uses

  • Bread course
  • Holiday table
  • Breakfast

  • sugar
  • cinnamon
  • Vegetarian

Ecuador · No chili heat · 6 cakes

Flying Pancakes of the World

Ecuador · South America

Llapingachos

Pan-fried potato cakes stuffed with cheese, served with peanut sauce.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 6 cakes

History

Llapingachos are Ecuadorian potato cakes, pan-fried, often stuffed with cheese and served with a peanut sauce and salad or sausage. They are highland food. The potato cake is the page. Peanut sauce is traditional and is a peanut allergen, so this written plate serves the cakes with cheese inside and puts the peanut sauce as a named variation you add only if you eat peanuts.

Cultural background

The cakes are thick. The cheese melts inside. A pickled onion salad is a common side and can be made without peanuts.

Why it tastes this way

Highland potatoes fry into a crisp cake with a soft center, a staple turned into a meal. Cheese in the middle salts them. Peanut sauce, when served, is the richer, sweeter note of the classic plate and is also a serious allergen, which is why it is not silently included. The cake without it is still a llapingacho. The sauce is the choice.

How it is made

Mashed potato is stuffed with cheese, shaped into thick cakes, and pan-fried.

Authentic touch. Season the mash, enclose a small piece of cheese, and chill the cakes before frying so they hold. Fry in a little oil until both sides are crusty.

How it is used

With a small salad. Add peanut sauce only if everyone at the table can eat peanuts.

Field notes

  • Llapingachos are Ecuadorian stuffed potato cakes.
  • Peanut sauce is traditional and optional here because it is a major allergen.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 pounds potatoes, boiled and mashed
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 4 ounces cheese, cut in 6 pieces
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Season the mash and divide into 6.
  2. Enclose a piece of cheese in each and shape thick cakes. Chill 15 minutes.
  3. Fry in a little oil until both sides are crusty and the center is hot.
  4. Serve with salad.
  5. Add peanut sauce only if everyone can eat peanuts.
  6. Do not use a wet mash.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

History

Cultural background

Why it tastes this way

Raw potato brings starch that binds, and cooked potato brings a soft, earthy flavor, the same logic as boxty in a completely different potato culture. Chiloé’s dozens of potato varieties are why a potato bread, not a wheat loaf, is the local starch. A pan toasts the outside. The inside stays dense. It is a bread.

How it is made

Grated raw potato and mashed potato are mixed, shaped, and cooked in a skillet.

How it is used

Warm, as a bread.

Pennsylvania Dutch Potato Pancakes

Shredded potato and onion, bound with egg and a little flour.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 10 cakes

History

German-speaking immigrants, later called Pennsylvania Dutch, settled in Pennsylvania in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries and brought potato pancakes with them. The cakes are everyday farm food, served with applesauce or sour cream, and they are part of the same family as German Kartoffelpuffer. They are not the same dish as Jewish latkes, though a hungry person could mistake the plate. The communities and the holiday meanings differ.

Cultural background

They show up at breakfast with apple butter and at supper beside meat. Church suppers still fry them by the dozen.

Why it tastes this way

A potato pancake tastes of starch browned in fat. German-speaking farmers in Pennsylvania had the potatoes, the onions, and the skillets. Flour in this version is the binder those recipes often use, which is why it is not gluten-free. Apple butter and applesauce on the side are the region’s fruit, preserved for winter, cutting the fry with acid.

How it is made

Grated potato and onion are mixed with egg and flour and fried as flat cakes.

Authentic touch. Squeeze the potatoes dry. Fry in a generous film of fat, not a dry nonstick pan, or the shreds never lace.

How it is used

Applesauce, apple butter, or sour cream.

Field notes

  • Pennsylvania Dutch means German-speaking settlers, from Deutsch, not a separate Dutch nation in this name.
  • These are relatives of German potato pancakes and are a different community’s food from Hanukkah latkes.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 pounds russet potatoes
  • 1 small onion
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons all-purpose flour
  • 3/4 teaspoon salt
  • Black pepper
  • Oil or butter for frying

Steps

  1. Grate potatoes and onion and squeeze dry.
  2. Mix with egg, flour, salt, and pepper.
  3. Heat a thin layer of oil over medium.
  4. Fry flattened spoonfuls until brown and crisp on both sides.
  5. Drain and salt.
  6. Serve with applesauce.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Leftover mashed potato makes a denser cake, closer to some boxty methods, and is a different texture.
  • Bacon fat is a farmhouse fat and adds meat.

Potato Latkes

Shredded potato, onion, and a lacy fried edge.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · about 12 latkes

History

Latkes are Ashkenazi potato pancakes associated with Hanukkah. Potato pancakes became everyday food in eastern Europe after potatoes were widely grown there. The Hanukkah link is the oil: the holiday remembers the oil of the Temple, and fried foods became the table. Jewish immigrants brought latkes to the United States, where they are now a December staple far beyond immigrant kitchens. Cheese pancakes were an earlier Hanukkah food in some communities. The potato latke is the one most Americans mean.

Cultural background

They are served with sour cream or applesauce. The argument between those two sauces is a family argument, not a rule. In Israel, sufganiyot, jelly donuts, share the holiday. This page is the pancake.

Why it tastes this way

Potato and onion, squeezed dry and fried in oil, taste sweet and savory at once because the potato’s starch browns and the onion’s sulfur softens. Hanukkah practice explains the fry: oil is the point of the food, not an accident of the method. Eggs bind the shreds. A little starch replaces the matzo meal some families use, and keeps this version gluten-free. The American Northeast did not invent the latke. It became a place where the holiday cake is cooked in huge skillets for a crowd.

How it is made

Russet potatoes and onion are shredded, squeezed, bound with egg and starch, and fried in oil until lacy.

Authentic touch. Squeeze the shredded potato and onion until the liquid stops dripping, then let that liquid sit and pour off the water. The starch that settles can go back in. Wet shreds steam and never crisp.

How it is used

Applesauce, sour cream, or both. They are a holiday side, not a syrup breakfast.

Field notes

  • Oil is the Hanukkah reason for frying, tied to the Temple story as Jewish tradition tells it.
  • Sour cream and applesauce are both served. Neither one cancels the other.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds russet potatoes
  • 1 small onion
  • 2 large eggs
  • 2 tablespoons potato starch
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/4 teaspoon black pepper
  • Neutral oil for frying

Steps

  1. Grate the potatoes and onion. Squeeze them hard in a towel.
  2. If a lot of liquid came out, let the starch settle and add that paste back.
  3. Mix in eggs, potato starch, salt, and pepper.
  4. Heat 1/4 inch of oil over medium.
  5. Fry flattened spoonfuls until deep golden on both sides.
  6. Drain on a rack and salt them. Serve hot.

Shallow-fry over steady heat. Do not leave the pan.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Matzo meal instead of potato starch is common and is not gluten-free.
  • A sweet potato latke is a later American variation.

History

Raggmunk is a Swedish potato pancake made with raw grated potato in a batter, as opposed to rårakor, which are grated potato fried without much batter. Both are served with salted pork and lingonberries. The combination is a classic Swedish everyday meal.

Cultural background

The cake is crisp at the edge. The berries and the pork do the sweet-and-salt work. This page includes the pork as the traditional plate and marks the meat allergen.

Why it tastes this way

Raw potato fries up sweeter and lacier than mashed potato. The batter of flour, egg, and milk holds the shreds into a cake you can flip. Lingonberry cuts the fry. Salt pork is the preserved meat of a winter kitchen. Together they are a balanced plate from a cold climate that stored potatoes, berries, and cured pork.

How it is made

Raw potato is stirred into a thin batter and fried as cakes, served with pork and lingonberry.

Authentic touch. Squeeze some water from the potato, not all of the starch. A wet batter steams. Fry in a generous film of fat until the cake is actually crisp.

How it is used

With fried salt pork or bacon and lingonberry jam.

Field notes

  • Raggmunk has batter. Rårakor are mostly potato.
  • Lingonberry and pork are the documented partners.

Ingredients

  • 4 medium potatoes, peeled and grated
  • 1/2 cup flour
  • 1 large egg
  • 1/2 cup milk
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • Oil or butter for frying
  • 8 ounces bacon or salt pork
  • Lingonberry jam

Steps

  1. Squeeze the grated potato lightly and mix with flour, egg, milk, and salt.
  2. Fry the pork until browned and set it aside.
  3. Fry spoonfuls of batter in the fat until crisp on both sides.
  4. Drain.
  5. Serve with the pork and jam.
  6. Keep the heat moderate so the potato cooks through.

Rösti is a Swiss potato cake, originally a farmer’s breakfast in German-speaking cantons, now a national side dish. Grated potato is fried in one cake and flipped. How it is made

Cooked potatoes are grated, seasoned, and fried as one cake in a skillet, then flipped.

Authentic touch. Parboil the potatoes and chill them, then grate. Press the cake and leave it alone until it releases. A nervous spatula breaks it. Flip with a plate if you need to.

How it is used

As a side, or with a fried egg and cheese. The cake itself is only potato, salt, and fat.
