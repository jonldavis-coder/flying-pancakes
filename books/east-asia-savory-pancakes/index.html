Pajeon

A scallion pancake, crisp at the edge and full of green onion.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 2 pancakes

History

Pajeon is a Korean scallion pancake. Pa is scallion, jeon is a pancake or fritter. It is rainy-day food in a popular saying, and it is also restaurant and home food any day, served with a dipping sauce of soy, vinegar, and chile. This batter is flour, water, and egg, with enough scallions that they are the body of the cake.

Cultural background

Haemul pajeon adds seafood and is a different page. Kimchi jeon is another. Plain pajeon can be vegetarian if the dipping sauce has no fish sauce. This recipe uses soy and vinegar.

Why it tastes this way

Scallions taste sweet when they fry and sharp when they are raw, so a pancake that is mostly scallion tastes green and browned at once. A thin batter only binds them. The crisp edge comes from oil and a hot pan, not from baking powder. Vinegar in the sauce is the acid that makes the fry make sense. Rainy-day custom is culture. The cake is still the same on a dry day.

How it is made

A thin batter is mixed with a mass of scallions and fried in oil until crisp, then cut.

Authentic touch. Use a lot of scallions, cut on a long bias, and press them into the batter so they touch the pan. Do not stack the cakes. The crust dies under steam.

How it is used

Cut into wedges, with a soy-vinegar dipping sauce.

Field notes

  • Pajeon is the scallion pancake. Jeon is the broader category.
  • A dipping sauce of soy and vinegar is the usual partner. This one has no fish sauce.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup flour
  • 3/4 cup cold water
  • 1 egg
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 8 scallions, cut into 2-inch lengths
  • 3 tablespoons oil
  • 2 tablespoons soy sauce
  • 1 tablespoon rice vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon sugar

Steps

  1. Stir flour, water, egg, and salt into a thin batter. Fold in the scallions.
  2. Heat oil in a skillet and spread half the batter into a round.
  3. Cook until the underside is deep golden, then flip and cook through.
  4. Repeat.
  5. Mix soy, vinegar, and sugar for the sauce.
  6. Cut into wedges and serve with the sauce.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Haemul pajeon adds seafood.
  • A spoon of rice flour makes the edge crisper and is still not gluten-free because of the wheat.

Kimchi Jeon

A savory pancake sour and hot from chopped kimchi.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 2 pancakes

History

Kimchi jeon is a pancake that uses ripe kimchi, the fermented cabbage, mixed into a flour batter and fried. It is a home dish for kimchi that has become sour, which is when it works best in a pancake. Kimchi itself is a preserved vegetable central to Korean meals.

Cultural background

The pancake is cut and dipped. Sour kimchi, not a fresh salad, is the ingredient. The juice of the kimchi goes into the batter.

Why it tastes this way

Fermented cabbage brings acid, chile, and umami, so the pancake tastes tangy and warm rather than like plain fried batter. Older kimchi is sourer because the ferment has continued, which is why the pancake is a thrifty use of the jar. Wheat batter binds the leaves. Oil crisps the chile paste at the edge. The heat is the gochugaru already in the kimchi.

How it is made

Chopped sour kimchi is mixed into a flour-and-egg batter and fried as pancakes.

Authentic touch. Squeeze the kimchi a little so the batter is not a soup, and keep some of the juice. Chop it small. Cook the cakes through so the cabbage is hot and the center is not gummy.

How it is used

With a light soy-vinegar dip, or on its own if the kimchi is already salty.

Field notes

  • Sour, ripe kimchi makes a better pancake than freshly made kimchi.
  • The chile heat comes from the kimchi.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup chopped sour napa kimchi, plus 2 tablespoons of its juice
  • 1 cup flour
  • 1 egg
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • Oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Stir kimchi, juice, flour, egg, water, and salt into a thick batter.
  2. Fry two rounds in a filmed skillet until both sides are browned and the center is set.
  3. Cut into pieces.
  4. Serve hot.
  5. Add a pinch of salt only if the kimchi is mild.
  6. Do not use sweet fresh kimchi if you can use sour.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of gochujang in the batter makes it hotter.
  • Seafood kimchi jeon adds fish or squid.

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Snack
  • soy dipping sauce
  • Vegetarian

Haemul Pajeon

A scallion pancake studded with squid or shrimp.

20 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 2 pancakes

History

Haemul pajeon is seafood and scallion pancake, a restaurant dish as much as a home one. Haemul means seafood. Squid and shrimp are the usual pieces. It is an elaborated pajeon, not a different cuisine. The dipping sauce is the same soy and vinegar family.

Cultural background

The seafood should be cooked through in the pancake, cut small so it heats with the batter. This recipe uses shrimp and marks shellfish.

Why it tastes this way

Shrimp tastes sweet and saline against the green scallion and the fried batter. The oil crisps the flour while the seafood steams inside if the pieces are thin. It is a coastal and market elaboration of a vegetable pancake. The sauce’s vinegar is what keeps the richness in check.

How it is made

Pajeon batter is mixed with scallions and shrimp and fried crisp.

Authentic touch. Pat the shrimp dry and cut them small. Wet seafood makes a soggy center. Press the pancake so the pieces contact the pan.

How it is used

Cut, with soy-vinegar sauce.

Field notes

  • Haemul means seafood. The pancake is pajeon with that addition.
  • Shrimp should be cut small so they cook in the time the batter does.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup flour
  • 3/4 cup water
  • 1 egg
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 6 scallions, cut up
  • 6 ounces shrimp, peeled and chopped
  • Oil
  • 2 tablespoons soy sauce
  • 1 tablespoon rice vinegar

Steps

  1. Mix batter, scallions, and shrimp.
  2. Fry two pancakes in oil until the shrimp are opaque and both sides are crisp.
  3. Mix the sauce.
  4. Cut and serve.
  5. Do not crowd the shrimp in one lump.
  6. The center should be hot.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Squid instead of shrimp is traditional and is still seafood.
  • Plain pajeon is the seafood-free page.

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Supper
  • soy dipping sauce
  • Dairy-free

Bindaetteok

A crisp mung-bean pancake with cabbage and kimchi.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 pancakes

History

Bindaetteok, also nokdujeon, are mung-bean pancakes. Soaked mung beans are ground, mixed with kimchi or vegetables, and fried. They are a popular snack and drinking food, and an older grain cake than the wheat pajeon. This version keeps them free of wheat and meat.

Cultural background

The edge should be lacy. The bean flavor is earthy. Kimchi adds acid. A dipping sauce is optional because the cake is already seasoned.

Why it tastes this way

Mung beans taste earthy and fry crisp when the batter is coarse, not a smooth milk. Kimchi juice seasons the batter without a long spice list. The cake is a bean pancake in a wheat-pancake culture, which is why it has its own name. Oil and a hot pan make the lace. A blender that purees it too fine makes a dense cake.

How it is made

Soaked mung beans are ground coarse, mixed with kimchi and cabbage, and fried as thick pancakes.

Authentic touch. Grind the beans until they are gritty, not baby-food smooth. Squeeze a little of the water off if the batter is a puddle. Fry in enough oil that the edge frills.

How it is used

Hot, in wedges. A vinegar dip is optional.

Field notes

  • Bindaetteok are mung-bean pancakes, also called nokdujeon.
  • A coarse grind fries lacier than a smooth puree.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup dried mung beans, soaked 8 hours and drained
  • 1/2 cup chopped kimchi
  • 1/2 cup chopped napa cabbage
  • 1 clove garlic
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Grind the beans with garlic and a splash of water until coarse and spreadable.
  2. Stir in kimchi, cabbage, and salt.
  3. Fry four thick cakes in a filmed skillet until both sides are crisp and the center is cooked.
  4. Drain.
  5. Serve hot.
  6. Add water only if the batter will not spread.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pork in the batter is a common version and adds meat.
  • A smooth grind is a different, heavier texture.

Pairings and uses

Gamjajeon

A Korean potato pancake, crisp, with a soy dip.

20 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 cakes

History

Gamjajeon is a Korean potato pancake, gamja meaning potato. Grated potato is mixed with a little flour and egg and fried, then dipped in soy sauce. It is a jeon, in the same broad family as pajeon, and a cousin of latkes with a Korean seasoning and sauce.

Cultural background

It is a side or a snack. The potato should be squeezed. The sauce is salty and vinegary.

Why it tastes this way

Potato starch crisps, and a little wheat flour helps the cake hold in a jeon style. Soy and vinegar are the Korean finish, not applesauce or sour cream, which is the clearest difference from the European potato cakes in this journal. Onion, if you add it, is the same savory note those cakes use. The pan and the dip are what make it gamjajeon.

How it is made

Grated potato, egg, and a little flour are fried and served with a soy dip.

Authentic touch. Squeeze the potato and fry thin. Salt the dip, not the cake too heavily, or it will be briny twice.

How it is used

Hot, with the dipping sauce.

Field notes

  • Gamja means potato. Jeon is the pancake category.
  • The soy dip, not sour cream, is the Korean plate.

Ingredients

  • 2 medium potatoes
  • 1 egg
  • 2 tablespoons flour
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Oil
  • 2 tablespoons soy sauce
  • 1 tablespoon vinegar

Steps

  1. Grate and squeeze the potatoes. Mix with egg, flour, and salt.
  2. Fry thin cakes until crisp.
  3. Mix soy and vinegar for the dip.
  4. Serve immediately.
  5. A few sesame seeds on the dip are optional and add sesame.
  6. Do not cover the cakes.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A few slices of chile on top are a home addition.
  • Latkes are the Ashkenazi cousin with a different sauce and holiday.

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Side dish
  • soy dipping sauce
  • Vegetarian

Osaka Okonomiyaki

A thick cabbage pancake, savory, finished with sauce, mayonnaise, and bonito flakes.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 2 pancakes

History

Okonomiyaki is a savory Japanese pancake. In Osaka it is mixed: cabbage, batter, and other ingredients cooked together as one cake. The name is often explained as “grilled as you like it.” Hiroshima’s version is layered and includes noodles, and it has its own page. The Osaka cake is the thick mixed one. Bonito flakes and a sweet-savory sauce are the usual finish.

Cultural background

It is restaurant food and home food. Pork is common and is left out of this recipe so the written cake has no meat. The fish is the bonito and the dashi.

Why it tastes this way

Cabbage wilts into the batter and tastes sweet, which is why the pancake is mostly vegetable and still rich. Dashi, made here with instant bonito stock, is the sea savor. The brown sauce is sweet and salty, a cousin of Worcestershire-style sauces adapted in Japan. Mayonnaise is the cool fat. The griddle browns the flour. Osaka mixes everything. That mix is the regional method.

How it is made

Cabbage is stirred into an egg batter and cooked as a thick pancake, then topped with sauce, mayonnaise, and bonito.

Authentic touch. Use more cabbage than you think, and do not press the cake into a thin crepe. Cook it covered so the cabbage softens before the crust burns. Flip with confidence, or use two spatulas.

How it is used

Cut, on the griddle or a plate, immediately so the bonito still moves in the heat.

Field notes

  • Osaka okonomiyaki is mixed. Hiroshima okonomiyaki is layered.
  • Bonito flakes are fish. A kombu dashi and no bonito would be a different, meatless-sea version.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups finely shredded cabbage
  • 1 cup flour
  • 2 eggs
  • 3/4 cup dashi or water plus 1 teaspoon instant dashi
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • Okonomiyaki sauce or a mix of ketchup and Worcestershire
  • Mayonnaise
  • Bonito flakes

Steps

  1. Stir flour, eggs, dashi, and salt. Fold in the cabbage.
  2. Oil a skillet and spread half the batter into a thick cake.
  3. Cook covered on medium until the bottom browns and the cabbage softens, then flip.
  4. Cook until the center is set.
  5. Spread sauce and mayonnaise. Top with bonito.
  6. Cut and serve.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pork slices on the cake are a common addition and add meat.
  • Hiroshima style is layered with noodles.

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Supper
  • Street food
  • okonomiyaki sauce
  • mayonnaise
  • bonito flakes
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Hiroshima Okonomiyaki

A layered savory pancake: crepe, cabbage, noodles, and egg.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 2 · 2 layered pancakes

History

Hiroshima okonomiyaki is built in layers on the griddle: a thin crepe, a large pile of cabbage, noodles, often pork, and an egg, then flipped together and sauced. It is the city’s version, distinct from Osaka’s mixed batter. This home version skips the pork and keeps the layers, including yakisoba-style noodles.

Cultural background

The cabbage pile looks excessive and cooks down. The crepe is only a base. Noodles are what make a visitor recognize the Hiroshima plate.

Why it tastes this way

Layering keeps the cabbage sweeter and fresher-tasting than a fully mixed batter, and the noodles make it a full meal. The same sauce and bonito finish tie it to other okonomiyaki. Hiroshima’s shops specialize in the stack. The method is the local identity. Pork is common and omitted here so the allergens stay with egg, wheat, fish, and soy.

How it is made

A thin crepe is topped with cabbage, then noodles and egg, flipped, and sauced.

Authentic touch. Cook the crepe thin, pile the cabbage and cover it until it wilts, then add noodles and egg. Flip the whole stack onto the egg. Do not stir it into one batter.

How it is used

With okonomiyaki sauce, mayonnaise, and bonito flakes.

Field notes

  • Hiroshima okonomiyaki is defined by layers and usually by noodles.
  • The cabbage pile shrinks. Start with more than looks reasonable.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup flour
  • 1 egg, plus 2 eggs for topping
  • 1/3 cup water
  • 4 cups shredded cabbage
  • 2 cups cooked noodles
  • Oil
  • Okonomiyaki sauce
  • Mayonnaise
  • Bonito flakes
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Make a thin batter with flour, 1 egg, water, and a pinch of salt. Cook a thin crepe.
  2. Pile half the cabbage on it, cover, and cook until wilted.
  3. Add half the noodles. Crack an egg onto a clear spot of the griddle, break the yolk, and flip the stack onto the egg.
  4. Cook until the egg sets. Repeat for the second cake.
  5. Sauce, mayonnaise, and bonito.
  6. Serve at once.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pork slices are the usual market addition.
  • Osaka style mixes the batter instead of layering.

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Supper
  • okonomiyaki sauce
  • mayonnaise
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Monjayaki

A loose, savory batter, scraped and crisped on the griddle, runnier than okonomiyaki.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 2 · 2 pans

History

Monjayaki is Tokyo’s loose savory pancake, associated with Tsukishima in Chuo ward. The batter is much wetter than Osaka okonomiyaki and is cooked by spreading it thin, letting it crisp, and scraping it together. It is a restaurant griddle food. Cabbage and other bits are in it. This version uses cabbage and a dashi batter.

Cultural background

Diners often cook it themselves on a table griddle. At home, a skillet works if you accept a less smoky result. It is eaten with small spatulas as it crisps.

Why it tastes this way

A thin batter crisps at the edge and stays soft in places, so the texture is part pancake and part savory pudding. Dashi is the flavor. Tokyo’s monja shops made a local answer to the thicker pancakes of the west of Japan. The looseness is not a mistake. It is the dish.

How it is made

A very loose cabbage batter is spread on a hot oiled griddle until the bottom crisps, then folded.

Authentic touch. Spread it thin and wait. If you flip it like a pancake too soon, you get a scrambled puddle. Let a crust form, then fold it over itself.

How it is used

Straight from the pan, in bites.

Field notes

  • Monjayaki is supposed to be runny and then crisp at the edges.
  • Tsukishima in Tokyo is the neighborhood most tied to it.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup flour
  • 1 cup dashi
  • 1 egg
  • 2 cups finely shredded cabbage
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • Oil
  • Okonomiyaki sauce

Steps

  1. Stir a loose batter and fold in the cabbage.
  2. Oil a skillet and spread the batter thin.
  3. Cook until the bottom crisps and the top is just set.
  4. Fold it over and crisp the new surface.
  5. Cut into bites and sauce lightly.
  6. Do not expect a neat round pancake.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Cheese or mochi are modern additions.
  • Okonomiyaki is thicker and is not a failed monja.

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Street food
  • okonomiyaki sauce
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Jianbing

A crisp savory crepe with egg, scallion, sauce, and a cracker.

15 min prep · 10 min cook · Serves 2 · 2 large wraps

History

Jianbing is a northern Chinese street breakfast. A thin batter, often mung-bean or wheat, is cooked on a round griddle, brushed with egg, and topped with scallion, cilantro, fermented bean sauce or hoisin, chile, and often a crispy cracker, the guozi, or a fried dough stick. Tianjin and Shandong both have strong claims. This is a home wheat-and-egg version with bean sauce and a store-bought cracker.

Cultural background

It is wrapped and eaten on the way to work. The sauce is savory and slightly sweet. The herbs are raw. The crepe is crisp at the edge.

Why it tastes this way

Mung-bean batter, where it is used, tastes cleaner and crisper than wheat. Wheat is the easier home flour and is what this recipe uses, so the page does not pretend otherwise. Egg fries onto the crepe. Fermented bean sauce is the deep savory note, a northern pantry flavor. Cilantro and scallion are fresh against that. The cracker is there for noise and texture. Street griddles run very hot, which is why the edge shatters.

How it is made

A thin batter is cooked, topped with egg, sauce, herbs, and a cracker, then folded.

Authentic touch. Spread the batter paper-thin on a hot pan and crack the egg on immediately so it bonds. Wrap it while it is still flexible enough to fold and crisp enough to crackle.

How it is used

Wrapped, hot, as breakfast.

Field notes

  • Jianbing is a northern street breakfast. Tianjin and Shandong are the places most often named.
  • The cracker is traditional texture. The crepe and egg are the meal if you cannot find one.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup flour
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 2 eggs
  • 2 tablespoons fermented bean sauce or hoisin
  • 2 scallions, sliced
  • 2 tablespoons chopped cilantro
  • Chile sauce, to taste
  • 2 crackers or a piece of fried dough
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Whisk flour and water into a thin batter.
  2. Oil a hot skillet and pour a paper-thin crepe.
  3. Beat an egg and spread it on the crepe. Cook until just set.
  4. Spread sauce, scallion, cilantro, and a little chile. Press on a cracker.
  5. Fold and serve.
  6. Repeat.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A mung-bean flour batter is closer to some Tianjin stalls and can be gluten-free if the sauce is too.
  • Hoisin instead of plain bean sauce is sweeter.

Pairings and uses

  • Street food
  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • fermented bean sauce
  • cilantro
  • scallion
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Scallion Pancake

A flaky pan-fried bread layered with scallion and oil.

40 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 pancakes

History

Scallion pancakes, cong you bing, are a Chinese pan bread: a dough rolled with oil and scallions into a coil, then fried until flaky. They are eaten across China and in Taiwan as a snack and a side. They are not a batter pancake. They are included because they are a griddled bread people reasonably meet next to jianbing.

Cultural background

The flake comes from oil and the coil, the same idea as other coiled flatbreads in this journal, with scallion as the flavor. A dipping sauce of soy and vinegar is common.

Why it tastes this way

Hot oil between thin layers of dough separates into flakes, and scallions steam and sweeten inside. Wheat dough is the structure. The pan fry, rather than an oven, browns the face. Salt and sesame oil, if you use a few drops, make it savory. It is a wheat snack of the north and of teahouses, not a sweet cake.

How it is made

A soft dough is coiled with oil and scallions and pan-fried.

Authentic touch. Roll, oil, sprinkle scallion and salt, coil, and roll again. Fry on medium so the inside cooks before the outside is black. A high heat only burns the scallion.

How it is used

Cut into wedges, with a soy-vinegar dip.

Field notes

  • Cong you bing is a laminated pan bread, not a poured batter.
  • The coil is how the layers are made.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups flour
  • 3/4 cup hot water
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 3 tablespoons oil, plus more for the pan
  • 6 scallions, chopped
  • Soy sauce and vinegar, to dip

Steps

  1. Mix flour and hot water into a dough. Rest 20 minutes.
  2. Roll each piece, brush with oil, sprinkle scallion and salt, and roll into a coil. Flatten.
  3. Fry on a medium oiled pan until both sides are golden and the center is cooked.
  4. Cut and serve.
  5. The layers should separate when torn.
  6. Dip in soy and vinegar.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A cracked egg on the frying pancake is a breakfast variation.
  • More oil means more flake and a heavier bread.

Pairings and uses

  • Side dish
  • Snack
  • Savory meal
  • soy sauce
  • vinegar

Mandarin Pancakes

Thin, steamed-then-pan wheat pancakes for wrapping Peking duck or stir-fries.

30 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 pancakes

History

Mandarin pancakes, or duck pancakes, are thin wheat rounds used to wrap Peking duck with scallion and hoisin. A related spring pancake, chunbing, is eaten at the start of spring with vegetables. The two are often described as relatives. This recipe is the thin wrapper. It does not include a whole roast duck. The pancake is cooked in pairs so the faces stay soft.

Cultural background

They should be flexible, not brown and crisp. Steam or a dry pan with a cover finishes them. Restaurants buy them. Home cooks roll them.

Why it tastes this way

A hot-water dough stays soft, which is why the wrapper bends around crisp duck skin instead of cracking. Cooking two rounds together and peeling them apart makes one side especially tender. The pancake is almost tasteless on purpose. Hoisin, scallion, and cucumber, or duck, are the flavor. Spring pancakes use the same idea for a seasonal vegetable meal.

How it is made

A hot-water dough is rolled into thin rounds, cooked briefly in pairs, and peeled apart.

Authentic touch. Roll them thin and do not brown them hard. Keep the stack under a towel. If they dry out, a few seconds in a covered steamer brings them back.

How it is used

Warm, as wrappers. Hoisin, scallion, and cucumber are the classic duck accompaniments and are not all in the dough.

Field notes

  • These are wrappers, not a sweet or savory filled cake.
  • Cooking them in pairs is the usual way to keep a tender side.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup flour
  • 1/2 cup boiling water
  • 1 teaspoon oil
  • Pinch of salt

Steps

  1. Stir flour, salt, and boiling water into a dough. Add the oil. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Divide into 8, pair them up with a drop of oil between, and roll each pair thin.
  3. Cook on a dry medium pan until the surface looks dry and a few spots appear, under a minute a side.
  4. Peel the pairs apart.
  5. Stack under a towel.
  6. Serve warm as wrappers.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Spring pancakes are the seasonal cousin for vegetables.
  • A crisp scallion pancake is a different bread.

Taiwanese Danbing

A thin breakfast crepe with egg, scallion, and a salty-sweet sauce.

10 min prep · 10 min cook · Serves 2 · 2 rolls

History

Danbing is a Taiwanese breakfast crepe: a thin batter cooked on a griddle with egg, often a cruller or youtiao inside, and a savory sauce. Breakfast shops cook them to order. It is related to northern jianbing and is its own breakfast-shop food. This version uses a wheat crepe, egg, and sauce, with the cruller as an optional variation.

Cultural background

It is wrapped and eaten with soy milk at a breakfast shop. The crepe is soft-crisp. The egg is fully cooked.

Why it tastes this way

Egg on a thin crepe is the protein of a fast breakfast, and the sauce, often a sweet soy or a miso-like bean paste, makes it salty-sweet. Scallion is the fresh note. Taiwanese breakfast shops standardized the wrap. Wheat batter is what those shops usually pour. The speed of the griddle is why the edge crisps before the egg dries out.

How it is made

A thin batter is cooked with egg and scallion, sauced, and rolled.

Authentic touch. Spread the batter thin, add the egg at once, and roll the crepe as soon as the egg sets. Overcooking makes a stiff tube.

How it is used

Hot, as breakfast.

Field notes

  • Danbing is a Taiwanese breakfast-shop crepe.
  • The egg should be cooked through in the roll.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup flour
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 2 eggs
  • 2 scallions, sliced
  • 1 tablespoon soy sauce
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Whisk flour and water. Stir soy and sugar together.
  2. Cook a thin crepe in an oiled pan.
  3. Pour a beaten egg on it with scallion and cook until the egg sets.
  4. Brush with the soy-sugar mix and roll.
  5. Repeat.
  6. Serve immediately.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A fried cruller inside is the fuller shop version.
  • Jianbing is the northern cousin, often with a cracker and cilantro.

China · East Asia

Laobing

A thick, layered northern griddle bread, chewy and flaky.

30 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 2 breads

History

Laobing is a northern Chinese layered griddle bread, thicker than a scallion pancake, coiled with oil and cooked until the outside is spotted and the inside is chewy. Beijing breakfasts include it. It can wrap eggs or be eaten plain. It is a bread, and it is in this journal as the thick griddled bing beside the thin duck pancake.

Cultural background

The coil is simple. The thickness is greater than cong you bing. It is torn, not stacked with syrup.

Why it tastes this way

Oil and a coil make layers, and a thicker round stays chewy rather than shattering. Northern wheat eating is why the breakfast is a bing and not a rice cake. A moderate griddle cooks the center. Salt is the seasoning. Scallion is optional. The plain bread tastes of fried wheat.

How it is made

A dough is coiled with oil, flattened thick, and cooked on a griddle.

Authentic touch. Roll it thicker than a scallion pancake, about a third of an inch after the coil, and cook it covered on medium-low so the center is not raw dough.

How it is used

Torn, warm, plain or with a fried egg.

Field notes

  • Laobing is a thick layered griddle bread of the north.
  • Cook it slowly enough that the center finishes.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups flour
  • 3/4 cup warm water
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons oil

Steps

  1. Make a dough and rest it 15 minutes.
  2. Roll it out, oil and salt it, coil it, and flatten to about 1/3 inch.
  3. Cook covered on a medium-low oiled griddle, turning once, until both sides are spotted and the center is cooked.
  4. Cool 2 minutes and tear.
  5. Serve warm.
  6. A fried egg on the side makes a breakfast.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Scallions in the coil move it toward cong you bing.
  • Duck pancakes are the thin wrappers.
