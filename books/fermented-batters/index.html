Adai

A thick, savory pancake of mixed dals and rice, spiced with chile and curry leaf.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 adai

History

Adai is a Tamil pancake of rice and several lentils, coarser and spicier than dosa, often cooked for dinner as much as breakfast. A common accompaniment is jaggery and butter, or avial, a vegetable stew. The batter is ground and may rest a few hours. It is not a thin crisp dosa.

Cultural background

The mix of dals is the point: toor, chana, and moong with a little urad, or a similar house blend. Curry leaves and dried chile go into the grind.

Why it tastes this way

Several lentils make it taste beany, hearty, and more savory than a rice-dominant dosa. Chile and curry leaf are ground in, so the heat is in the cake, not only in a side chutney. A coarser grind keeps it textured. Jaggery on the side is a Tamil contrast, sweet against a spicy pancake, and it surprises people who expect only chutney.

How it is made

Soaked rice and mixed dals are ground coarse with chile and curry leaves and cooked as thick pancakes.

Authentic touch. Grind it coarse, like wet sand, not a smooth dosa cream. Cook it thicker, with oil, until the center is no longer raw. A thin adai dries out and misses the point.

How it is used

With jaggery, or with a vegetable stew, and a spoon of yogurt if you eat dairy.

Field notes

  • Adai is thicker and spicier than dosa, and it uses several dals.
  • Jaggery on the side is a real way to eat it.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup rice
  • 2 tablespoons toor dal
  • 2 tablespoons chana dal
  • 2 tablespoons moong dal
  • 1 tablespoon urad dal
  • 2 dried red chiles
  • 8 curry leaves
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Soak the rice and dals together 4 hours. Drain.
  2. Grind coarse with chiles, curry leaves, salt, and a little water.
  3. Rest 2 hours if you can. It is good the same day.
  4. Spread thick rounds on a hot oiled tawa and cook, turning, until both sides are browned and the center is cooked.
  5. Serve with jaggery or a stew.
  6. Oil the edges so they crisp.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A smoother grind makes it more like dosa and less like adai.
  • Fewer chiles make it milder. The classic is noticeably spiced.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • jaggery
  • avial
  • yogurt
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free

Benne Dosa

A crisp fermented dosa finished with a notable amount of butter.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 dosas

History

Benne dosa is the butter dosa of Davangere in Karnataka, a tiffin-room specialty. Benne means butter. The dosa batter is the fermented one, and the butter on the tawa is generous. A potato filling is common. The butter is the defining point, so this recipe is not vegan and not dairy-free.

Cultural background

It is a restaurant breakfast more than a temple recipe. People go to Davangere, and to restaurants that copy it, for the butter.

Why it tastes this way

Butter on a hot tawa browns and flavors the rice batter in a way oil does not, which is the entire reason to name the dosa after butter. The ferment still supplies tang. The contrast of crisp edge and rich fat is the plate. It is a city specialty built on an older batter, and the dairy is honest.

How it is made

Fermented dosa batter is cooked on a buttered tawa until crisp.

Authentic touch. Use butter, not only oil, and let it foam on the tawa before the batter goes down. Too little butter and you have made a plain dosa.

How it is used

With chutney and, if you like, a potato filling.

Field notes

  • Benne means butter. The fat is the specialty.
  • Davangere is the city most tied to the name.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups fermented dosa batter
  • 4 tablespoons butter
  • A pinch of salt if the batter needs it

Steps

  1. Heat a tawa and add a spoon of butter.
  2. Spread a ladle of batter thin.
  3. Cook until crisp, adding a little more butter at the edge.
  4. Fold and serve.
  5. Repeat, refreshing the butter.
  6. Chutney on the side.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Oil instead of butter is a different, vegan dosa. Do not call it benne.
  • A potato filling makes it a meal.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Street food
  • coconut chutney
  • potato masala
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Chakuli Pitha

A soft, sour rice-and-lentil crepe from Odisha, a dosa cousin.

40 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 crepes

History

Chakuli pitha is an Odia fermented crepe of rice and black gram, clearly in the dosa family and named as a pitha. It is breakfast food, served with vegetable curries or a sweet side. The ferment is shorter or longer depending on the weather. It is not a copy of a Tamil dosa. It is the Odisha cake.

Cultural background

The texture is often a little thicker and softer than a crisp restaurant dosa. Coconut or a potato curry can sit beside it.

Why it tastes this way

Rice and urad, fermented, taste sour for the same reason dosa does. Odisha’s pitha tradition gives the cake its name and its place in a regional breakfast, often next to other rice dishes. The climate’s warmth is what makes the ferment reliable in season. A softer cook keeps it a pitha rather than a wafer.

How it is made

Soaked rice and urad dal are ground, fermented, and cooked as soft crepes.

Authentic touch. Spread it a little thicker than paper dosa and cook it on medium. If you crisp it to a cracker, you have made a different dosa.

How it is used

With a vegetable curry or coconut chutney.

Field notes

  • Chakuli is Odisha’s fermented rice-dal crepe.
  • It belongs with pitha as well as with dosa.

Ingredients

  • 3/4 cup rice
  • 1/4 cup urad dal
  • Water
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Soak rice and dal 6 hours. Grind smooth.
  2. Ferment 8 to 12 hours until slightly risen.
  3. Add salt and thin if needed.
  4. Cook as medium-thin crepes on a hot oiled tawa until set and lightly browned.
  5. Serve warm.
  6. They should still bend.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Chitau pitha is the holey Odia rice cake.
  • A very crisp cook is closer to dosa. Both are legitimate. This recipe aims at the softer side.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • vegetable curry
  • coconut chutney
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Dibba Rotti

A thick, pan-fried rice and dal cake, crisp outside and soft inside.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · one 8-inch cake

History

Dibba rotti is an Andhra dish: a thick cake of rice and lentil batter, often fermented idli or dosa batter, cooked in a deep pan with oil so the outside fries and the inside stays soft. It is cut into wedges. It is a tiffin food, richer than a steamed idli and thicker than a dosa.

Cultural background

Mustard, cumin, and onion are often mixed into the batter. The pan is covered for part of the cook. This is closer to a thick pancake than idli is, which is why it is here and steamed idli is not.

Why it tastes this way

Oil in a deep pan fries the crust while the thick ferment steams inside, so you taste sour batter and a fried edge at once. Onion and spices make it a snack rather than a plain bread. Andhra tiffin uses the same fermented batter in several shapes. Dibba rotti is the fried thick one.

How it is made

Seasoned fermented batter is cooked as one thick cake in an oiled covered pan, then cut.

Authentic touch. Use a small skillet so the cake is deep enough to stay soft inside. Cover it. Uncover at the end to crisp the top. Do not try to make it as thin as dosa.

How it is used

In wedges, with chutney.

Field notes

  • Dibba rotti is thick and pan-cooked, not steamed like idli.
  • The same batter can make dosa. The pan and the thickness are the dish.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups fermented dosa or idli batter
  • 1 small onion, chopped
  • 1 green chile, minced
  • 1/2 teaspoon cumin
  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Stir onion, chile, cumin, and salt into the batter.
  2. Heat oil in a small skillet, pour in the batter, and spread it about 3/4 inch thick.
  3. Cover and cook on medium-low until the bottom is crisp and the center is set.
  4. Flip or brown the top under a broiler.
  5. Rest 5 minutes and cut.
  6. Serve with chutney.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Leftover idli batter is the usual start.
  • A spicy gunpowder sprinkle is a condiment, not the cake.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Snack
  • coconut chutney
  • pickle
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free

Egg Dosa

A fermented dosa with an egg cracked on top and cooked until set.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 dosas

History

Egg dosa is a tiffin-room and home variation: a dosa topped with a beaten or cracked egg, chile, and onion. It is a modern, widely cooked breakfast, not a temple food. The batter is the fermented rice-dal batter. The egg is the addition. It is vegetarian in the Indian sense that includes eggs, and it is not vegan.

Cultural background

The egg sets on the hot dosa. Some cooks scramble it on the crepe. Some leave it sunny-side. This recipe spreads a beaten egg so it cooks through.

Why it tastes this way

Egg adds richness and protein to a tangy crepe and browns at the edge where it meets the hot tawa. Onion and chile keep it savory. It is a restaurant habit of the same shops that sell plain dosa, a way to make the crepe a fuller breakfast. The ferment is still the sour note underneath.

How it is made

A dosa is cooked, topped with beaten egg, onion, and chile, covered until the egg sets, and folded.

Authentic touch. Pour the egg on the dosa while it is still on the heat and cover the pan so the egg sets. A raw egg on a plated dosa is not the dish.

How it is used

With chutney.

Field notes

  • Egg dosa is a popular variation, not a separate ancient batter.
  • The egg should be cooked, not raw on the plate.

Ingredients

  • 4 ladles fermented dosa batter
  • 4 large eggs
  • 1 small onion, finely chopped
  • 1 green chile, minced
  • Salt
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Beat each egg with a pinch of salt, a little onion, and chile. Keep them separate.
  2. Cook a dosa on a hot oiled tawa.
  3. Pour one egg mixture over it, spread, and cover until the egg sets.
  4. Fold and serve.
  5. Repeat.
  6. Chutney on the side.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A cracked whole egg, like an appam egg, is a different look. Cook the white through.
  • Plain dosa is the egg-free page.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • coconut chutney
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Eritrean Injera

A sour teff flatbread, the sister of Ethiopian injera, served with stews.

30 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 rounds

History

Eritrean injera is the same highland family as Ethiopian injera: fermented teff batter cooked on a mitad into a spongy bread. Eritrea and Ethiopia share the bread and differ in some stews and in history. This page does not treat the bread as the property of one capital. The home method here is 100 percent teff, with the same warning that wheat blends are common and not gluten-free.

Cultural background

The bread lines a platter. Diners tear pieces and pick up stew. The sourness is part of the meal’s balance against spiced sauces.

Why it tastes this way

Teff and fermentation taste sour and earthy because that is what the grain does when it is soured in water. The highlands are why teff is the grain. A covered griddle is why the texture is a sponge. Eritrean and Ethiopian injera can differ in thickness and sourness by household. The written method is the shared one.

How it is made

Fermented teff batter is poured thin and cooked covered until the eyes form.

Authentic touch. Use the freshest ferment that already smells pleasantly sour. Over-fermented batter tastes harsh. Cook covered, one side only.

How it is used

With stews, as the bread of the meal.

Field notes

  • Eritrea and Ethiopia share injera. The stews are where many differences show up.
  • This recipe is teff only.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups teff flour
  • 3 cups water
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Mix teff and water and ferment 1 to 3 days until bubbly and sour.
  2. Salt the batter and thin it if needed.
  3. Cook thin rounds in a hot covered nonstick pan until the top sets. Do not flip.
  4. Cool on a cloth.
  5. Serve with stew.
  6. Discard any batter that molds.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Ethiopian injera is the neighboring page.
  • Wheat-mixed injera is a different bread.

Pairings and uses

  • Bread course
  • Supper
  • lentil stew
  • greens
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Injera

Sour, spongy teff flatbread, cooked on one side so the top is full of eyes.

30 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 rounds

History

Injera is the fermented teff flatbread of Ethiopia, and a close relative is eaten in Eritrea. Teff is a tiny grain domesticated in the highlands. The batter ferments for days, then is poured on a covered mitad, a clay griddle, and cooked on one side until it is spongy with holes. It is the plate and the utensil for stews. This recipe uses 100 percent teff so it is gluten-free. Many restaurant injeras mix in wheat or barley, which changes the taste and the gluten status.

Cultural background

A meal is injera with several stews, wat, shared from one platter. The sourness is desired. A sweet injera is a different, less typical bread.

Why it tastes this way

Teff grows in the highlands where other grains struggle, which is why the daily bread tastes of teff rather than of wheat. Fermentation makes the sourness and the bubbles. Cooking on one side, covered, steams the top into a sponge instead of a cracker. The eyes, the holes, hold the stew. Climate, grain, and fermentation are the whole flavor.

How it is made

Teff flour and water ferment, then the batter is cooked like a large pancake on one side in a covered pan.

Authentic touch. Ferment until the batter smells sour and looks bubbly, usually one to three days in a warm kitchen. Pour in a spiral on a hot nonstick pan, cover, and do not flip. If it browns black, the pan is too hot.

How it is used

Under and with stews, or rolled up beside them. It is not a syrup bread.

Field notes

  • One hundred percent teff injera is gluten-free. Many restaurant versions add other grains.
  • It is cooked on one side. The holes are the sign it worked.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups teff flour
  • 3 cups water, plus more as needed
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Stir teff and water into a thin batter. Cover loosely and ferment 1 to 3 days until sour and bubbly.
  2. Stir in the salt and thin with water if it is thicker than crepe batter.
  3. Heat a nonstick skillet, wipe it with a drop of oil, and pour a thin round.
  4. Cover and cook until the top is set and full of holes, about 2 minutes. Do not flip.
  5. Cool on a cloth.
  6. Stack and serve with stew.

Ferment in a clean container. If the batter smells rotten or grows mold, discard it. Sour and yeasty is the goal.

Keep cooked injera covered at room temperature for a day or refrigerated for a few days. Do not can the batter. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Eritrean injera is the sister bread.
  • A mixed-grain injera is common and is not gluten-free.

Pairings and uses

  • Bread course
  • Supper
  • lentil stew
  • greens
  • spiced chicken
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan

Kerala Appam

A bowl-shaped rice pancake, lacy at the rim and soft in the center.

40 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 appams

History

Appam, or palappam, is a Kerala rice pancake cooked in a small rounded pan, an appachatti. The edges are thin and lacy. The center is thick and soft. Fermented rice batter, often with coconut, is traditional. Yeast or toddy can start the ferment. This home version uses a pinch of yeast and coconut milk, and it notes that toddy is the older starter where it is used.

Cultural background

Appam is eaten with stew, egg roast, or sweetened coconut milk. The shape is the dish. A flat dosa pan will not make a bowl.

Why it tastes this way

A rounded pan sends the thin batter up the sides, where it crisps, and leaves a custardy center of rice and coconut. Coconut is Kerala’s fat and sweetness. Fermentation, whether from toddy or yeast, adds a light sourness and the lift. The result tastes of rice and coconut, not of wheat, which is why it matches a coconut-milk stew.

How it is made

Fermented rice and coconut batter is swirled in a rounded pan and cooked covered.

Authentic touch. Swirl the batter in a hot, oiled appam pan and let the excess sit in the middle. Cover it. Do not flip. A wok is a poor substitute that can work if you treat it gently.

How it is used

With vegetable stew or sweet coconut milk. An egg cooked in the center is a common variation and adds egg.

Field notes

  • The bowl shape comes from the pan and the swirl.
  • Toddy is traditional. Yeast is the home starter written here.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup rice, soaked 4 hours
  • 1/2 cup cooked rice
  • 1/2 cup coconut milk
  • 1/4 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1/2 teaspoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Grind soaked rice and cooked rice with a little water until smooth.
  2. Stir in coconut milk, yeast, sugar, and salt. Ferment 6 to 12 hours until bubbly.
  3. Heat an oiled appam pan.
  4. Pour a ladle, swirl to coat the sides, and let the rest pool in the center.
  5. Cover and cook until the center is set and the rim is lacy. Do not flip.
  6. Serve with stew.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Sri Lankan hoppers are the island cousin, often with an egg.
  • Kallappam is a toddy-fermented cousin.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Bread course
  • vegetable stew
  • coconut milk
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Kisra

A thin, sour sorghum crepe, used to scoop stew.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 sheets

History

Kisra is the fermented sorghum bread of Sudan, cooked paper-thin on a hot plate. It is a daily bread for stews, in the same broad family as injera and laxoox and not the same recipe. Sorghum is the grain. The ferment is sour. South Sudanese cooking also uses kisra. This page uses Sudan as the home and lists South Sudan as a place it is also cooked.

Cultural background

The sheets are thin enough to fold. They are not fluffy injera. A wide nonstick pan is the home version of the traditional hot plate.

Why it tastes this way

Sorghum tolerates heat and drought better than wheat, which is why the daily bread of much of Sudan tastes of sorghum. Fermentation softens the grain and adds sourness that balances rich stews. Cooking it thin and fast dries it into a flexible sheet rather than a sponge. The difference from injera is thickness and grain.

How it is made

Fermented sorghum batter is spread thin and cooked briefly on one side.

Authentic touch. The batter should be as thin as crepe batter. Spread it fast on a hot pan. If it tears, thin it with a spoon of water and work cleaner. Do not expect injera’s tall sponge.

How it is used

Folded, with stew.

Field notes

  • Kisra is thinner than injera and is typically sorghum.
  • It is a sour bread for stews, not a sweet pancake.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup sorghum flour
  • 1 1/2 cups water
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Mix sorghum and water. Ferment 1 to 2 days until sour and bubbly.
  2. Add salt and thin to crepe consistency.
  3. Spread very thin on a hot nonstick pan.
  4. Cook until the sheet lifts. Do not brown it hard.
  5. Fold and stack under a cloth.
  6. Serve with stew.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Injera is thicker and usually teff.
  • A wheat kisra exists in some cities and is not gluten-free.

Pairings and uses

  • Bread course
  • Supper
  • stew
  • lentils
  • Vegetarian

Laxoox

A spongy, lightly sour flatbread, softer and often smaller than injera.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 cakes

History

Laxoox, also called canjeero, is the fermented Somali pancake, a relative of injera and of Yemeni lahoh. It is breakfast food, eaten with honey, ghee, or a stew. Sorghum, teff, or a mix of flours appear in different houses. This version uses teff or sorghum flour, both gluten-free, and says to pick one and name it.

Cultural background

It is cooked in a pan on one side until holes form. Diaspora kitchens keep the ferment going with a spoon of yesterday’s batter. That spoon is a starter, not a packaged yeast.

Why it tastes this way

Fermentation sours the grain and lifts the holes, the same science as injera, with a Somali breakfast habit of honey or ghee on a smaller cake. Sorghum is a dryland grain, which fits the climate better than wheat. The sourness should be gentle. A harsh ferment means it went too long.

How it is made

A fermented sorghum or teff batter is cooked as small spongy pancakes on one side.

Authentic touch. Keep a spoon of batter in the refrigerator to start the next batch. Cook on medium, covered, without flipping, until the top is dry.

How it is used

With honey or ghee, or with a stew. Honey is not vegan. The bread is.

Field notes

  • Laxoox and canjeero are names for the Somali fermented pancake.
  • A saved spoon of batter starts the next batch.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup teff flour or sorghum flour
  • 1 1/2 cups water
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon of a previous batter, if you have it

Steps

  1. Mix flour, water, and starter if you have it. Ferment 1 to 2 days until bubbly.
  2. Stir in the salt.
  3. Cook small rounds in a covered nonstick pan until holes form and the top sets. Do not flip.
  4. Serve warm.
  5. Save a spoon of batter, refrigerated, for next time.
  6. Discard moldy batter.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Yemeni lahoh is the related pancake across the water.
  • Wheat flour versions exist and are not gluten-free.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Bread course
  • honey
  • ghee
  • stew

Masa

Sour rice cakes, cooked in small molds, crisp at the edge.

40 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 12 cakes

History

Masa, also called waina, is a Hausa fermented rice cake from northern Nigeria and the wider Sahel, including Niger. Rice or sometimes millet is soaked, ground, fermented, and cooked in small round molds or a pan. It is a street and home breakfast, eaten plain, with honey, or with a savory sauce. This version uses rice and a skillet if you do not have the clay molds.

Cultural background

The ferment is the sourness. Yeast can hurry a home batch, and a natural ferment is the older method. The recipe uses a pinch of yeast so the timing is reliable, and it says so.

Why it tastes this way

Rice, soaked and soured, tastes gently tart and a little sweet when the edges fry. The small molds, or a small skillet cake, give a crisp rim and a soft center. Masa is a Sahel food, where rice and millet are the grains, not wheat. Honey or pepper sauce are the two directions, sweet or hot, and both are served.

How it is made

Soaked rice is blended, fermented with a little yeast, and fried as small thick cakes.

Authentic touch. Grind or blend the soaked rice until smooth. Lumpy batter makes a raw center. Oil the pan well. The cakes stick when the surface is dry.

How it is used

Plain, with honey, or with a pepper sauce on the side.

Field notes

  • Masa and waina are names you will both meet for these rice cakes.
  • The yeast here is a home timing aid. A wild ferment of the same batter is traditional.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup raw rice, soaked 8 hours and drained
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 1/4 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1/2 teaspoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Blend the soaked rice with water until smooth.
  2. Stir in yeast, sugar, and salt. Rest 1 to 2 hours until bubbly.
  3. Oil a skillet or muffin pan over medium.
  4. Fry small mounds until set and browned on both sides.
  5. The center should be cooked, not gritty.
  6. Serve warm.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

Paniyaram

Small round fermented-rice cakes from a special pan, crisp outside.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 14 cakes

History

Paniyaram, also kuzhi paniyaram or gunta ponganalu, are small cakes cooked in a hemispherical pan from dosa or idli batter. Sweet and savory versions both exist. Savory ones carry mustard seed, curry leaf, and ginger. They are a way to use fermented batter, and the pan is the tool.

Cultural background

The cakes are turned in the wells so both sides brown. They are a snack or a breakfast with chutney.

Why it tastes this way

The same fermented rice-dal batter that makes dosa tastes different when it is fried in a little pool of oil in a well: crisper, rounder, and more like a dumpling. Mustard and curry leaf make the savory version smell like a tiffin snack. The pan is South India’s cousin of other well pans in this journal, arrived at for the same reason: a small mold fries the sides.

How it is made

Dosa batter is seasoned and cooked in a paniyaram pan until round and browned.

Authentic touch. Get the pan properly hot and oil each well, or the cakes stick and tear when you turn them. Fill the wells about two-thirds full.

How it is used

With coconut chutney.

Field notes

  • The well pan defines paniyaram.
  • Savory and sweet versions both exist. This one is savory.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups fermented dosa batter
  • 1 teaspoon mustard seeds
  • 8 curry leaves, chopped
  • 1 teaspoon grated ginger
  • 1 green chile, minced
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Stir mustard, curry leaf, ginger, and chile into the batter.
  2. Oil a hot paniyaram pan.
  3. Fill the wells two-thirds full and cook until the bottoms are brown.
  4. Turn and cook until set.
  5. Serve with chutney.
  6. They should be crisp outside and soft inside.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A sweet jaggery batter is a different paniyaram.
  • Without the special pan, a muffin tin in the oven is a different cake. Say so if you do it.

Paper Dosa

An extra-thin, extra-crisp, extra-large dosa.

10 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 large dosas

History

Paper dosa is a restaurant style of plain dosa spread very thin and large until it is crisp as a cracker. The batter is the same fermented rice and urad batter. The technique is the difference: more oil or ghee, a hotter tawa, and a wider spread. It is a showpiece of tiffin rooms, not a separate ancient recipe.

Cultural background

It is served upright or broken, with chutney and sambar. The center may be a little softer than the lace edge. Ghee makes it richer and adds dairy. Oil keeps this version vegan.

Why it tastes this way

Thin batter and high heat drive off moisture until the rice starch shatters. That is why it tastes toasty and not spongy. The ferment is still there, fainter because so much surface has browned. The size is theater and also a way to keep the crepe crisp until it reaches the table.

How it is made

Fermented dosa batter is spread extra thin and cooked until brittle.

Authentic touch. Spread in widening circles on a very hot tawa, thinner than you think you can. Let it crisp before you try to lift it. A flexible paper dosa is just a thin plain dosa.

How it is used

With chutney and sambar, broken into shards if that is easier.

Field notes

  • Paper dosa is a technique applied to plain dosa batter.
  • It should snap. If it bends, cook it longer or thinner.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups fermented dosa batter, thinned with a spoon of water if needed
  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • A pinch of salt if needed

Steps

  1. Heat a large tawa until very hot and oil it lightly.
  2. Ladle batter in the center and spread it in a thin, wide spiral.
  3. Drizzle oil around the edge.
  4. Cook without flipping until the dosa is crisp and lifts in a sheet.
  5. If it bends, cook it a minute longer.
  6. Serve at once with chutney and sambar.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Ghee roast dosa is the same idea finished with ghee and often a red chutney.
  • Set dosa is the soft opposite.

Plain Dosa

A thin, tangy rice-and-lentil crepe, crisp at the edge.

40 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 5 · about 10 dosas

History

Dosa is a South Indian fermented crepe of rice and urad dal, the black gram. It is a breakfast and tiffin food in Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Kerala, and far beyond those states now. The batter ferments overnight. A plain dosa is the cake itself, served with chutney and sambar. Masala dosa, the potato-filled one, has its own page.

Cultural background

Homes and tiffin shops both cook it on a hot tawa. The ferment is what makes it tangy and light. A batter that does not ferment in a cold kitchen can be helped by a warm spot, not by skipping the wait.

Why it tastes this way

Rice keeps the crepe crisp. Urad dal gives protein and the fermentation that tastes sour and a little sweet. The overnight rest is lactic fermentation in a warm climate, which is why dosa batter in a hot kitchen moves faster than batter in a cold one. The tawa’s heat is what laces the edge. Chutney and sambar are the sour, hot, and salty partners. The crepe itself is mild and tangy.

How it is made

Soaked rice and urad dal are ground, fermented, and cooked as thin crepes on a hot griddle.

Authentic touch. Spread the batter thin in a spiral from the center of a hot, lightly oiled tawa. Wait until the edge lifts. A pale, limp dosa needed more heat or more time.

How it is used

With coconut chutney and sambar. Ghee on the surface is a common finish and adds dairy.

Field notes

  • Plain dosa is the crepe. The potato filling makes it a masala dosa.
  • Fermentation is the flavor. An unfermented rice crepe is a different bread.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup rice
  • 1/4 cup urad dal
  • 1/2 teaspoon fenugreek seeds
  • Water, for soaking and grinding
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Oil for the tawa

Steps

  1. Soak rice, dal, and fenugreek together or separately for 6 hours.
  2. Grind to a smooth batter, a little thicker than cream. Ferment in a warm place 8 to 16 hours, until risen and sour.
  3. Stir in the salt. Thin with water if needed.
  4. Spread thin on a hot, lightly oiled tawa.
  5. Drizzle a few drops of oil around the edge. Cook until the underside is golden and the dosa lifts. Do not flip if it is already crisp.
  6. Serve with chutney and sambar.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Masala dosa adds a potato filling.
  • A spoon of soaked fenugreek in the batter is a common ferment aid.

Ragi Dosa

An earthy finger-millet dosa, darker than a rice dosa.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 dosas

History

Ragi, finger millet, is a traditional grain in parts of Karnataka and Tamil Nadu. Ragi dosa is a crepe that uses ragi flour with rice or with a rice-dal batter. It is both an older grain in a newer breakfast format and a home recipe people cook for the millet. This version mixes ragi flour into a fermented rice-dal batter.

Cultural background

The color is brown-gray. The taste is earthier than plain dosa. Chutney is still the partner.

Why it tastes this way

Finger millet tastes mineral and slightly bitter, a dryland grain that stores well. Mixed with fermented rice and dal, it keeps that earthiness and gains the sourness of the batter. The cake is a way millet enters the dosa breakfast without pretending ragi was always cooked as a crisp crepe in every century. The flavor is the grain.

How it is made

Ragi flour is stirred into fermented dosa batter and cooked as crepes.

Authentic touch. Ragi browns fast. Keep the heat at medium and cook the dosa through, because the millet can taste raw if it is only browned on the outside.

How it is used

With coconut chutney.

Field notes

  • Ragi is finger millet, a real South Indian grain.
  • The earthy taste is the millet, not a burnt pan, if you cook it moderately.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups fermented dosa batter
  • 1/2 cup ragi flour
  • 1/4 cup water
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Whisk ragi, water, and salt into the dosa batter. Rest 10 minutes.
  2. Cook thin dosas on a medium oiled tawa until the underside is browned and the center is cooked.
  3. Flip if needed.
  4. Serve with chutney.
  5. They will be darker than rice dosas.
  6. Do not use high heat.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • An instant ragi dosa with yogurt is a different, quicker batter and adds dairy.
  • Plain dosa is the rice-dal cake without millet.

Set Dosa

Soft, thick, spongy dosas served in a stack of two or three.

40 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · about 12 small dosas

History

Set dosa is a Karnataka tiffin: small, thick, spongy dosas served as a set, usually with a vegetable curry and chutney. The batter is a dosa batter made a little thicker, sometimes with a spoon of flattened rice for softness. It is not meant to be a crisp paper dosa.

Cultural background

The set is the serving. One lonely crisp dosa is a different order. The center stays pale and tender.

Why it tastes this way

A thicker, wetter ferment steams rather than fries into a cracker, so the crumb is spongy and sour. Flattened rice, poha, if you add it, holds moisture. Karnataka tiffin rooms built a breakfast around that softness plus a coconut chutney and a potato palya. The sourness is still the ferment. The texture is the regional choice.

How it is made

A thick fermented rice-dal batter is cooked covered, as small soft dosas.

Authentic touch. Pour small thick rounds and cover the pan. Do not press them thin. They are done when the top is set and the underside is only lightly browned.

How it is used

In a stack, with potato curry and coconut chutney.

Field notes

  • Set dosa is served in a set and is supposed to be soft.
  • Covering the pan is what keeps them tender.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups fermented dosa batter, left thicker than crepe batter
  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • A pinch of salt if the batter is unsalted

Steps

  1. Ferment the batter as for plain dosa. Do not thin it as much.
  2. Pour small thick rounds on a medium-hot tawa.
  3. Cover and cook until the top sets. Flip briefly if the top is still wet.
  4. Serve two or three to a plate.
  5. They should be spongy.
  6. Chutney and a potato curry are the meal.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A handful of soaked poha ground into the batter makes them softer.
  • Paper dosa is the opposite texture.

Sinasir

A fermented millet pancake, sour and soft, from the Hausa kitchen.

1 hr 10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 10 cakes

History

Sinasir is a Hausa fermented millet pancake, related to masa and often named separately. Millet is soaked, ground, and soured, then cooked as cakes. It is a northern Nigerian breakfast grain food. Recipes vary by town. This is a home version with millet flour and a short ferment.

Cultural background

It is eaten with sugar, honey, or a soup. The millet tastes earthier than rice masa.

Why it tastes this way

Millet is a Sahel staple that stores well in a hot, dry climate, which is why the cake tastes of millet rather than of wheat. Fermentation adds sourness and makes the flour easier to digest and more aromatic. A short ferment with a pinch of yeast is the reliable home path. A longer wild ferment is more sour.

How it is made

Millet flour is mixed with water and a little yeast, rested, and cooked as small pancakes.

Authentic touch. Rest the batter until it smells faintly sour and looks a little risen. Cook the cakes covered for a minute so the millet center sets.

How it is used

Warm, with honey or alongside a soup.

Field notes

  • Sinasir is the millet pancake. Masa is often the rice one. Households do blur the names.
  • Millet keeps the cake gluten-free.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup millet flour
  • 1 1/4 cups water
  • 1/4 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Stir millet, water, and yeast. Rest 1 hour, until slightly bubbly.
  2. Add salt.
  3. Fry small cakes in a lightly oiled pan, covered briefly, until set.
  4. Flip and brown.
  5. Serve warm.
  6. The center should not taste raw.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Masa is the rice cake in the same family.
  • Without yeast, ferment the batter 24 hours if your kitchen is warm, and watch for mold.

Sri Lankan Hoppers

A bowl-shaped rice and coconut pancake with a lacy edge.

40 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 hoppers

History

Hoppers, aappa, are Sri Lanka’s bowl-shaped fermented rice pancakes, close kin to Kerala appam. Coconut milk is usual. An egg hopper, with an egg broken into the center, is a common breakfast and adds egg. This recipe is the plain hopper, vegan, with the egg noted as a variation. They are cooked in a small curved pan.

Cultural background

Hoppers are eaten with sambol and curries. The center is soft. The rim is crisp. Yeast or a ferment starts the batter.

Why it tastes this way

Coconut milk and a curved pan are the flavor and the shape. The rim crisps because it is thin. The center stays creamy because the batter pools. Rice fermentation gives a slight sourness that matches a spicy sambol. The island and Kerala share the technique across the water. Egg in the middle is optional richness, not required for the bread to be a hopper.

How it is made

Fermented rice and coconut batter is swirled in a hopper pan and cooked covered.

Authentic touch. Swirl and cover, as with appam. Wipe the pan with oil between hoppers. If the rim burns, the pan was empty of batter at the top or the heat was too high.

How it is used

With sambol or curry. An egg in the center is the variation.

Field notes

  • Plain hoppers and egg hoppers are both common. This batter is the plain one.
  • The curved pan makes the bowl. A flat tawa will not.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup rice, soaked 4 hours
  • 1/2 cup cooked rice
  • 3/4 cup coconut milk
  • 1/4 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Grind soaked rice and cooked rice smooth. Stir in coconut milk, yeast, sugar, and salt.
  2. Ferment 8 to 12 hours until bubbly.
  3. Oil a hot hopper pan or appachatti.
  4. Swirl a ladle of batter and cover until the center is set and the rim is crisp.
  5. Do not flip.
  6. Serve with sambol or curry.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Crack an egg into the center for an egg hopper and cover until the white sets.
  • Kerala appam is the mainland cousin.

Uttapam

A thick, savory dosa pancake with onion and chile cooked into the top.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 3 · about 6 uttapams

History

Uttapam is a thick pancake made from dosa batter, topped with onion, tomato, chile, and coriander, and cooked softer than a crisp dosa. It is a South Indian breakfast and a way to use batter that has fermented a little longer than you meant. The toppings are pressed into the wet surface.

Cultural background

It is eaten with chutney. It is not folded around potato. The vegetables are the face of the cake.

Why it tastes this way

A thicker pour steams into a spongy, sour cake. Onion and green chile on top taste fresh and sharp against the fermented batter. The sourness of a well-fermented batter is an advantage here, which is why leftover dosa batter becomes uttapam. Oil around the edge crisps the rim while the center stays soft.

How it is made

Thick dosa batter is cooked covered with onion, chile, and tomato pressed into the top.

Authentic touch. Pour thick, sprinkle the toppings at once, and press them in gently. Cover the pan. Flip only if you must. Many cooks brown only the bottom.

How it is used

With coconut chutney.

Field notes

  • Uttapam is the thick savory pancake of dosa batter.
  • Leftover fermented batter is a traditional reason to cook it.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups fermented dosa batter
  • 1 small onion, chopped
  • 1 tomato, finely chopped and drained
  • 1 green chile, minced
  • 2 tablespoons chopped cilantro
  • Oil
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Salt the vegetables lightly.
  2. Pour a thick round of batter on a medium-hot oiled tawa.
  3. Scatter onion, tomato, chile, and cilantro and press them in.
  4. Cover and cook until the top is set and the bottom is browned.
  5. Flip carefully if the top is still wet.
  6. Serve with chutney.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A plain thick uttapam without vegetables is still uttapam and less interesting.
  • Tomato makes it wetter. Pat the pieces dry.

Yemeni Lahoh

A spongy fermented pancake, kin to Somali laxoox, eaten with ghee or honey.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 cakes

History

Lahoh is the Yemeni fermented pancake, a close relative of Somali laxoox across the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden. Sorghum or a mix of flours is traditional. It is cooked on one side until holes form. Honey, ghee, or a stew are the partners. This version uses sorghum flour.

Cultural background

It is breakfast bread. A saved spoon of batter starts the next batch. The sourness should be gentle.

Why it tastes this way

Sorghum suits a dry climate and ferments into a mildly sour, spongy bread. The covered pan makes the holes. Trade and migration across the water explain the kinship with laxoox without making them one recipe. Ghee is the rich finish. Honey is the sweet one. The bread itself is the grain and the ferment.

How it is made

A fermented sorghum batter is cooked as small spongy pancakes on one side.

Authentic touch. Ferment until bubbly and pleasant, not harsh. Cook covered, one side only, on medium heat.

How it is used

With honey or ghee. The bread is vegan. Those toppings may not be.

Field notes

  • Lahoh and laxoox are relatives across the water.
  • One-sided cooking is the method.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup sorghum flour
  • 1 1/4 cups water
  • 1/4 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Stir flour, water, and yeast. Ferment 8 to 24 hours until bubbly.
  2. Add salt.
  3. Cook small rounds in a covered nonstick pan until holes form. Do not flip.
  4. Serve warm.
  5. Save a spoon of batter for next time.
  6. Discard moldy batter.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Somali laxoox is the sister pancake.
  • A wheat blend is common in some kitchens and is not gluten-free.
