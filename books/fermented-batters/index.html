Alaska sourdough flapjacks

Mildly sour wheat cakes, a little richer from evaporated milk, with a browned edge.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 flapjacks

History

Sourdough flapjacks are the breakfast cooks made from a living starter in Alaska, where gold-rush camps kept a starter going because fresh yeast was hard to replace. The word sourdough became a nickname for an old hand in that country. The cakes are wheat pancakes leavened by the starter and finished with a little soda. Skagway, a Klondike gateway, is the map pin.

Cultural background

In this journal, flapjack means the American pancake, not the British oat bar. Evaporated milk was a practical canned dairy in camps and is used here. The starter has to be active before you start, so feeding it is not counted in the prep time.

Why it tastes this way

A fed starter brings a light sourness and some of the rise. Baking soda reacts with that acid and gives the cakes a quicker lift on the griddle. Evaporated milk tastes slightly cooked and rich. If the starter is sleepy, the cakes taste flat and do not brown as well.

How it is made

Sourdough batter finished with baking soda and cooked on a griddle.

Authentic touch. Use a bubbly wheat starter you have already fed. Stir the soda in just before cooking so the batter does not collapse on the counter.

How it is used

Hot, with butter and syrup.

Field notes

  • Gold-rush cooks in Alaska and the Yukon kept sourdough starters because fresh yeast was scarce.
  • Sourdough also became a nickname for someone who had lasted in that country.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup active sourdough starter, fed ahead of time
  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 large egg
  • 1/2 cup evaporated milk
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil, plus more for the griddle
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda

Steps

  1. Stir the starter, flour, sugar, salt, egg, and evaporated milk until smooth. The starter should already be bubbly from a feeding done before this recipe.
  2. Rest the bowl 15 minutes.
  3. Stir in the oil and baking soda.
  4. Oil a griddle over medium heat. Pour 1/4 cup cakes.
  5. Flip when the surface is full of holes and the underside is brown. Cook until the center is set.
  6. Serve hot with butter and syrup.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A water-based batter without evaporated milk is leaner and less dairy-rich, but then the recipe changes.
  • Fresh milk can replace evaporated milk if that is what you have. Use 1/2 cup.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Butter
  • Maple syrup or berry jam
  • Coffee
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Amboli

A soft, spongy, lightly sour rice pancake from the Konkan coast.

30 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 3 · 6 ambolis

History

Amboli is a fermented rice pancake of the Konkan, especially Malvani kitchens in Maharashtra and the coast down toward Goa. Rice is soaked and ground, then left to sour. Some cooks add a little urad. This recipe uses a spoon of flattened rice for softness and keeps the batter wheat-free. It is served with coconut curries and chutneys, a monsoon and breakfast food of the coast rather than a crisp hotel dosa.

Cultural background

Ratnagiri and Malvan cook amboli as a sponge that soaks up a spicy side. The pancake on this page is not itself a hot dish. The heat lives in the fish or vegetable curry beside it. A fish curry would change the allergens, so the pairing listed here is a coconut chutney.

Why it tastes this way

A long rice ferment in humid coastal air turns sweet soaked rice into something mildly sour and bready. The cake is thick, so the sourness stays soft instead of toasting away. Coconut chutney adds fat and sweetness the batter does not have. The coast's humidity is not a myth here: it is why an overnight bowl sours reliably without a starter.

How it is made

Grind soaked rice, ferment it overnight, and cook thick covered rounds on a moderate griddle.

Authentic touch. Cook it covered and do not chase a deep brown crust. Amboli is done when the top is set and the crumb looks spongy, like a pancake that never tried to be a wafer.

How it is used

Serve in a stack with coconut chutney. On the Konkan it also meets spicy curries. Those are separate dishes.

Field notes

  • Amboli is a Konkan fermented rice cake, soft on purpose.
  • This batter has no wheat, so the pancake is gluten-free.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup raw rice
  • 2 tablespoons flattened rice (poha)
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Water for soaking and grinding
  • Neutral oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Rinse the rice and soak it with the flattened rice for 4 hours.
  2. Drain and grind to a smooth batter a little thicker than crepe batter. Stir in the salt.
  3. Ferment covered overnight, until bubbly and mildly sour. Do not count those hours as active prep. Discard the bowl if it spoils.
  4. Oil a pan over medium-low. Pour a thick round and cover it.
  5. Cook until the top is set and spongy and the underside is pale gold. Flip only if the center needs it. Repeat.

Discard batter that smells rotten or shows mold.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A tablespoon of soaked urad ground with the rice
  • A pinch of sugar if the ferment went very sour

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • Coconut chutney
  • A dry potato bhaji
  • Sweetened coconut milk
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Appam

A bowl-shaped rice hopper with a lacy crisp rim, a spongy sweet center, and coconut in the batter.

35 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 appams

History

Appam is Kerala's fermented rice hopper, cooked in a curved appam pan so the center stays thick and the sides climb into a lace. Coconut milk is a usual part of the batter. Where toddy is still used, it is the ferment. This home recipe uses a pinch of yeast as the reliable stand-in and says so. Sri Lankan hoppers are a close cousin with their own page. Nei appam, the sweet ghee cake, is not this dish.

Cultural background

Kerala breakfasts pair appam with stew: vegetable, egg, or meat. The hopper itself is rice and coconut. A yeast ferment will not taste identical to a toddy ferment, and the page does not pretend it will. The bowl shape is non-negotiable.

Why it tastes this way

Rice and coconut milk make the center sweet and tender. Fermentation, whether from toddy or from yeast, adds a light sourness that keeps the coconut from tasting flat. The thin batter that climbs the hot wall of the pan dries into lace and picks up a toasted edge. Salt is the only savory note in the cake. The stew around it does the rest.

How it is made

Ferment a rice and coconut-milk batter and swirl it in a covered curved pan so it finishes bowl-shaped.

Authentic touch. Swirl the pan as soon as you pour, so a puddle remains in the center and only a film coats the sides. Then cover the pan. An open flat griddle cannot make an appam.

How it is used

Serve the bowls at once, center side up, with a mild vegetable stew. Meat stew is a common pairing and would add a meat allergen.

Field notes

  • The bowl shape comes from a curved appam pan and a swirl, not from a mold pressed into flat batter.
  • This savory hopper is a different cake from sweet nei appam.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup raw rice
  • 1/2 cup cooked rice
  • 1/2 cup coconut milk, plus more if needed
  • 1/4 teaspoon dry yeast
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Water as needed
  • Neutral oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Soak the raw rice for 3 hours. Drain and grind it with the cooked rice and a little water to a smooth batter.
  2. Stir in coconut milk, yeast, sugar, and salt. The batter should be thinner than dosa batter and thicker than milk.
  3. Cover and ferment until bubbly and lightly sour, often 6 to 10 hours in a warm kitchen. Active prep excludes that wait.
  4. Heat a curved appam pan, grease it, and pour a ladle of batter. Swirl so the sides take a thin coat and the center stays deep.
  5. Cover and cook on medium-low until the rim is lacy and the center is set and spongy. Ease it out and repeat.

Discard batter that smells rotten or grows mold. Cook the center until it is set, not glossy and raw.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Toddy in place of yeast where you can get it and trust it
  • A spoon of sugar for a sweeter palappam

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • Mild vegetable stew
  • Sweetened coconut milk
  • A ripe banana
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Djiboutian lahoh

Small, holey breakfast cakes, mildly sour, with a soft wheat crumb.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · About 8 breakfast cakes

History

Lahoh in Djibouti is a breakfast bread in a port city where Somali, Afar, and Yemeni cooking already meet. The batter is fermented and cooked in a small pan until the top is spongy. It is related to Somali canjeero and to Yemeni lahoh, and it is not either of those pages. This version is the Djibouti morning cake: a wheat batter, a little sorghum for grain flavor, eaten early with honey or eggs, not as a liner under a platter of stews.

Cultural background

Breakfast in the city can be lahoh, tea, and something sweet or a boiled egg. The bread is made at home and also bought from women who cook it to order on a round pan. The ferment usually starts the evening before.

Why it tastes this way

The overnight batter is gently sour. Sorghum beside the wheat adds a drier, grainier note than a sweet white loaf. The holes catch honey or a little butter. Because the cakes are small and thick, they taste softer and less like a sheet of injera. Egg on the side tastes of the egg, not of the batter.

How it is made

Mix wheat flour, a little sorghum flour, yeast, sugar, and water. Ferment it overnight, then salt it. Cook small rounds on one side in a covered pan. Serve them in the morning with honey or with eggs and tea. The sorghum does not cancel the wheat.

Authentic touch. Cook it as a breakfast cake in a small pan, one side, until the surface is full of holes. Do not copy the Somali soup-and-ghee service onto this page, and do not turn it into the Yemeni foul breakfast.

How it is used

Djiboutian lahoh is breakfast. Honey, a smear of butter, or a boiled egg are the usual company, with tea. It is not the bread under a large shared stew. Butter and egg add dairy and egg at the table.

Field notes

  • In Djibouti, lahoh is a breakfast cake, cooked small and eaten early.
  • It is related to Somali canjeero and Yemeni lahoh and written here as its own bread.
  • Sorghum in the batter adds grain flavor. The wheat still means this is not gluten-free.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups wheat flour (about 190 g)
  • 1/2 cup sorghum flour
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 2 cups warm water
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt

Steps

  1. Whisk both flours with the sugar, yeast, and warm water into a batter like cream. Do not add the salt yet.
  2. Cover the bowl overnight. In the morning it should smell faintly sour and look bubbly. This is the Djibouti breakfast batter, not a stew bread.
  3. Stir in the salt. Thin with water if it no longer pours.
  4. Heat a small pan, wipe it with a drop of oil, and pour a round that covers the bottom.
  5. Cover and cook on one side until the holes set and the top is matte. Keep the cakes warm for breakfast.

This batter contains wheat, even with sorghum in the bowl. Honey or egg at the table adds those allergens only if you use them.

Refrigerate leftover lahoh and reheat it covered. The batter should be cooked once it has soured, not left for a week. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • More sorghum makes a grainier cake and still contains wheat in this recipe.
  • A saved spoon of batter can start the next morning.
  • Black sesame on the oiled pan is a finish some cooks like. Sesame would add that allergen.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Honey
  • A boiled egg
  • Cardamom tea
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Eritrean taita

Sour teff flatbread with a spongy top, meant to carry tsebhi.

20 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 6 · About 8 large sheets

History

In Eritrea the fermented teff bread is called taita. It belongs to the same teff family as Ethiopian injera and uses the same grain, but the meal around it is organized as tsebhi, the Tigrinya name for the stews. Zigni is a berbere stew, alicha is the milder one, hamli is greens, and shiro is a paste of ground chickpeas or broad beans. This page is that Asmara bread, not a second copy of the Ethiopian platter.

Cultural background

Taita is torn at the table and used to scoop tsebhi from a shared tray. The bread is the plate. Bakeries in Asmara sometimes blend wheat into the batter for a paler, less fragile sheet. This home recipe does not. It stays with teff.

Why it tastes this way

The sourness comes from a teff batter that has had time to ferment, and the earthy taste is the grain itself. Cooking on one side leaves a soft, holey top that soaks up a rich tsebhi. A wheat blend tastes milder and tighter. This version keeps the sharper teff flavor because nothing else is in the flour.

How it is made

Mix teff flour and water, cover the bowl, and let it sour until bubbly. Salt it at the end. Cook thin rounds on a mogogo, the Tigrinya clay griddle, or on a wide nonstick pan. Cover each round and cook one side until the surface is set and full of holes. Stack the sheets in a cloth. The stews are cooked apart from the bread.

Authentic touch. Serve the bread with at least two tsebhi, one hot with berbere and one milder, so the same piece of taita tastes different from bite to bite. Do not flip the bread.

How it is used

Taita is the base of an Eritrean meal: a tray of tsebhi zigni, hamli, and shiro, with the bread torn as the utensil. It is an accompaniment, not a rolled snack. The stews can be meat or fasting dishes. The bread recipe itself has neither.

Field notes

  • Taita is the Tigrinya name for this teff bread. The stews beside it are tsebhi.
  • The clay griddle is a mogogo. The bread cooks on one side.
  • South of the city, the same teff batter is still the everyday starch, with the stew changing by season.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups teff flour (about 280 g)
  • 3 1/4 cups water
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
  • A few drops of neutral oil if the griddle sticks

Steps

  1. Stir the teff flour and water into a lump-free batter a little thinner than pancake batter. Cover it. Do not add wheat flour.
  2. Leave it at cool room temperature until it is sour and foamy, usually two or three days. Stir once each day.
  3. Add the salt. Thin with a splash of water if the batter no longer pours in a ribbon.
  4. Heat a mogogo or a wide nonstick skillet. Spread a ladle of batter from the outside inward into a round that reaches the edges.
  5. Cover and cook until the top changes from glossy to set and the holes stay open. Do not flip. Lay each sheet on a cloth and cover it while the next one cooks.

Gluten-free only if the teff was not milled with wheat. Berbere stews served alongside can be very hot even though the bread is not.

Keep taita wrapped so it does not dry out. Refrigerate leftovers and warm them in a covered pan. Do not leave a meat stew on the bread at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of an older sour batter makes the next batch more reliable.
  • Some Asmara bakeries add wheat. That bread is not gluten-free and is not written here.
  • Brown teff gives a darker sheet than ivory teff. Both are taita.

Pairings and uses

  • Accompaniment to a meal
  • Savory meal
  • Tsebhi zigni, a berbere stew
  • Hamli, cooked greens
  • Shiro, a chickpea or broad-bean stew
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Injera

Sour, spongy teff bread with an earthy crumb and open holes on top.

25 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 6 · About 8 large rounds

History

Teff, Eragrostis tef, was domesticated in the Horn of Africa, and injera is the fermented flatbread made from it. A teff batter is left to sour, then cooked on one side on a mitad, the clay griddle. The bread covers a platter and is torn to pinch stews. This recipe is 100 percent teff. Wheat blends used by some bakeries are a different loaf and are not in this method.

Cultural background

Injera is both the starch and the utensil at a shared Ethiopian meal. Guests tear pieces and use them to pick up wat. The sourness is expected. A fresh injera should bend and hold a stew without cracking into dust.

Why it tastes this way

Two to three days of fermentation feed lactic acid bacteria and wild yeast, so the bread tastes sour rather than sweet. Teff itself is earthy and slightly nutty. On the hot mitad the bubbles set into ayen, the eyes. The top stays soft because the bread is never flipped, and steam under a cloth finishes it so it can soak up sauce.

How it is made

Whisk teff flour and water into a batter like heavy cream and ferment it, covered, for 2 to 3 days until sour and bubbly. Salt it only after that rest. Pour it in a spiral on a hot mitad or a wide nonstick skillet, cover, and cook on one side until the ayen are open and the top is matte. Do not flip. Stack the rounds in a cloth so they steam and soften.

Authentic touch. Cook on one side only and leave the ayen open. Do not flip. If the eyes close under a second side of heat, the surface toughens and stops drinking the stew.

How it is used

Injera lines a platter under meats and vegetable stews, or under a fasting spread of lentils and greens. It is the accompaniment to the meal, not a side dish in a separate basket. Leftover bread is torn into firfir the next day.

Field notes

  • The holes on the surface are called ayen, the Amharic word for eyes.
  • A mitad is the round clay griddle used to cook injera on one side.
  • One hundred percent teff injera is gluten-free only if the flour was not milled with wheat.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups teff flour, ivory or brown (about 280 g)
  • 3 cups water, plus more for thinning
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
  • A few drops of neutral oil, only if the pan sticks

Steps

  1. Whisk the teff flour and water into a smooth batter about as thick as heavy cream. There is no wheat in this recipe. Cover the bowl.
  2. Leave the batter at room temperature for 2 to 3 days, until it smells sour and looks bubbly. Stir it once a day. This is the ferment. Do not add baking powder.
  3. Stir in the salt only after the ferment. Add a spoon of water if the batter is thicker than pourable cream.
  4. Heat a mitad or a wide nonstick skillet over medium heat. Wipe it with a few drops of oil only if the surface sticks. Pour the batter in a spiral and tilt the pan into a large thin round.
  5. Cover and cook on one side until the top is set and the ayen, the eyes, are open. Do not flip. Move the bread to a clean cloth and let the steam soften it while you cook the rest.

This loaf is gluten-free only when the teff flour has not been milled or stored with wheat. A shared griddle can transfer wheat flour.

Wrap cooked injera in a clean cloth for the day, then refrigerate and reheat in a covered pan. Keep sour batter refrigerated if you are not cooking it the same day. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Ivory teff makes a paler injera with the same ferment.
  • A spoon of saved batter from a previous batch can start the next one.
  • Adding wheat flour is a bakery shortcut. It is not this recipe, and it removes the gluten-free diet tag.

Pairings and uses

  • Accompaniment to a meal
  • Savory meal
  • Feast or fast day
  • Red lentil stew
  • Collard greens
  • Spiced chicken stew, for cooks who eat meat
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Jowar dosa

A pale sorghum dosa, mildly sweet and peppery, with a dry crisp edge.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 jowar dosas

History

Jowar dosa is a sorghum griddle cake from the dry-grain belts of Karnataka and Maharashtra, where jowar is a daily flour. This recipe ferments jowar flour with a spoon of rice flour and no wheat, so it can be tagged gluten-free. Sorghum rotis are the unleavened cousin. The dosa is the watered, soured, griddled one. The page does not pin the habit to a century.

Cultural background

Hubballi sits in a sorghum-growing region, and the flour is ordinary pantry stock there. A jowar dosa is a breakfast use of that flour, not a novelty millet. Cooks who skip the ferment and add yogurt are making the instant kind. This bowl is the fermented one.

Why it tastes this way

Sorghum tastes mildly sweet and a little grassy, less bitter than finger millet. Fermentation brings a gentle sour note that keeps the sweetness from tasting raw. Rice flour helps the fragile sorghum starch hold together on the pan. Cumin and green chile are surface seasoning, so the grain still leads.

How it is made

Ferment a sorghum and rice-flour batter, then cook it in small thin rounds with cumin and chile.

Authentic touch. Spread gently. Jowar batter tears if you press the ladle the way you would on a glossy urad batter. A nonstick or very well-seasoned pan is the practical tool.

How it is used

Eat it hot with coconut chutney or a dry peanut chutney. Peanut chutney would add a peanut allergen, so the written cake stays nut-free.

Field notes

  • Jowar is sorghum, not millet and not wheat.
  • This recipe stays gluten-free by using sorghum and rice flour only.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup jowar flour (sorghum), with no wheat added
  • 2 tablespoons rice flour, with no wheat added
  • 1 1/2 cups water
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon cumin seeds
  • 1 green chile, minced
  • Neutral oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Whisk the flours, salt, and water into a lump-free batter.
  2. Ferment covered 8 to 12 hours until slightly sour. Count only the mixing as active prep. Discard batter that spoils.
  3. Stir in cumin and green chile. Loosen with water if needed.
  4. Oil a medium-hot pan. Spread a small round with a light hand.
  5. Cook until the underside is spotted and the edge is dry. Flip for a short minute. Repeat, oiling the pan each time.

Discard fermented batter that smells rotten or shows mold.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Chopped spinach stirred into the batter
  • Black pepper instead of green chile

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • Coconut chutney
  • A sprinkle of chopped onion
  • Sambar
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Masala dosa

A fermented rice-and-urad sheet wrapped around a turmeric potato filling, with curry leaf and green chile.

45 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 4 · 6 stuffed dosas

History

Masala dosa uses the same fermented rice and urad batter as a plain dosa. The potato masala is a filling, not a second grain and not a different ferment. Restaurant kitchens in Karnataka, including Bengaluru's hotel dosas, made the potato-stuffed fold a city breakfast. The filling itself is a home potato palya that already belonged on the plate beside a plain dosa.

Cultural background

The potato should be dry enough to sit inside the roll without soaking the crisp side. Onion cooked in oil with mustard seed, curry leaf, and turmeric is the usual base. A wet curry would belong in a bowl, not in the fold.

Why it tastes this way

The batter still tastes of fermented rice and urad. The masala adds an earthy, lightly bitter turmeric note, the sweetness of onion, and the pop of mustard seed. Because the potato is spiced and then wrapped, each bite mixes a sour crisp sheet with a soft, salted center. The green chile is there for aroma more than burn.

How it is made

Ferment a rice and urad batter, cook a dry spiced potato, spread a thin dosa, lay the potato in a line, and roll.

Authentic touch. Spread the potato in a strip on the set dosa, not in a tall mound. Too much filling cracks the sheet before it can be rolled.

How it is used

Cut the roll only if you must. Most tables eat it with coconut chutney and sambar, starting at the crisp tail.

Field notes

  • The batter is still rice and urad. Potato never replaces either grain.
  • A masala dosa that sits too long steams the crust from the inside out.

Ingredients

  • 1 batch fermented rice and urad dosa batter, about 3 cups
  • 3 medium potatoes, boiled, peeled, and crumbled
  • 1 small onion, sliced
  • 1 green chile, slit
  • 1/2 teaspoon mustard seeds
  • 8 curry leaves
  • 1/4 teaspoon turmeric
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil, plus more for the griddle
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt, or to taste

Steps

  1. Use a bubbly fermented rice and urad batter, ground and rested as for a plain dosa. Active prep here is the filling and the cooking, not a second grind.
  2. Heat 2 tablespoons of oil. Add mustard seeds. When they pop, add curry leaves, onion, and green chile. Cook until the onion softens.
  3. Add turmeric and the crumbled potato. Season with salt. Mash lightly and cook until the mixture looks dry. Cool a little.
  4. Heat a griddle, grease it, and spread a ladle of batter in a thin round. Oil the edge and cook until the underside is golden.
  5. Lay a strip of potato across the center. Fold the dosa over the filling. Serve at once with chutney.

Use batter that smells pleasantly sour. Discard it if it is moldy or putrid.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of ghee on the potato, which adds dairy
  • A little cooked green pea in the masala

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • Street food
  • Coconut chutney
  • Sambar
  • Fresh coconut and cilantro

Homes with a smaller pan still make a paper-style dosa by using less batter and a hotter surface. The restaurant version is larger because the griddle is larger. Either way, the test is thinness and a dry crackle, not a new recipe of flours.

Why it tastes this way

More of the batter touches iron, so toast and a light ferment sourness dominate. There is almost no soft center to cushion the taste. Oil at the edge fries the lace. Because the grain is still rice and urad, the aroma matches a plain dosa that has been cooked further and thinner.

How it is made

Spread fermented rice and urad batter very thin on a broad hot griddle and cook it until the sheet is crisp enough to crack.

Plain dosa

Crisp at the edge, faintly sour, with a toasted rice smell and a soft underside.

40 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 dosas

History

Dosa is old in South India. The exact origin century is not settled, so this page does not name one. What the kitchens agree on is the batter: raw rice and skinned black gram, soaked, ground, salted, and fermented, then spread on a hot griddle. Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, and Kerala all keep that ferment and change the sides.

Cultural background

A home batter is judged by smell and bubbles. A warm night may finish it by morning. A cool kitchen needs more time near the stove. The sourness should stay gentle. Harsh smell or visible mold means the bowl is discarded.

Why it tastes this way

Rice supplies the starch that dries against iron and turns crisp. Urad dal brings protein and a soft stickiness so the sheet can spread without tearing. Fermentation adds a mild tang and the gas that opens lacy holes at the rim. A few fenugreek seeds, traditional in many batters, leave a faint bitter aroma under the toast.

How it is made

Soak rice and urad separately, grind them to different textures, ferment until the batter is lively, and cook thin rounds on a lightly oiled griddle.

Authentic touch. Spread from the center while the batter still flows. A pan that is too hot tears the sheet. A pan that is too cool glues it down and never crisps.

How it is used

Eat it at once with coconut chutney and sambar. A covered dosa steams itself and loses the crisp edge.

Field notes

  • Skinned black gram is the urad dal in this batter, sometimes sold as white urad.
  • The same fermented batter can be steamed as idli. The griddle is what makes the dosa.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup idli rice or other short-grain raw rice
  • 1/3 cup skinned urad dal (black gram)
  • 1/4 teaspoon fenugreek seeds
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
  • Water for soaking and grinding
  • Neutral oil for the griddle

Steps

  1. Rinse the rice. Rinse the urad with the fenugreek. Soak them in separate bowls for 4 to 6 hours.
  2. Drain and grind the urad with cold water to a light, fluffy paste. Grind the rice with water to a slightly grainy batter. Stir them together with the salt. The mix should thickly coat a spoon.
  3. Cover the bowl and ferment until it is bubbly and mildly sour, often 8 to 14 hours. In a cool room give it longer. This wait is not part of the active prep time. Discard the batter if it smells putrid or grows mold.
  4. Stir the batter down and loosen it with a spoon or two of water if it is too stiff to spread. Heat a griddle until a drop of water skitters. Grease it lightly.
  5. Pour a ladle in the center and spiral outward. Drizzle a little oil around the edge. Cook until the underside is spotted gold and the top looks dry. Fold and serve immediately.

Ferment in a clean bowl. Discard batter that smells rotten, turns an odd color, or shows mold.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Ghee on the rim instead of oil
  • A spoon of cooked rice in the rice grind for a softer crumb

Ragi porridge and ragi mudde are the better-known millet dishes. The dosa is the griddle form, useful when cooks want the same grain in a crisp sheet. The color is naturally dark. That darkness is the millet, not burnt batter.

San Francisco sourdough pancakes

History

San Francisco's sourdough reputation belongs first to its bread bakeries, which were part of the city's nineteenth-century food supply. Pancakes made from a bubbly starter are a home and diner use of that same ferment. This page does not name a bakery brand or a single inventor of the pancake. The map pin is the city itself.

Cultural background

A San Francisco starter is a wheat starter kept in a cool coastal kitchen. Any active wheat starter will make the cakes. The long life of the starter happens before this recipe. Active prep is mixing and cooking.

Why it tastes this way

The starter's acid is the sour taste, and it also reacts with baking soda for lift. Milk and egg soften a flavor that would be sharper in lean bread. Butter browns the surface. A starter that has not been fed tastes sluggish and the cakes spread without rising.

How it is made

Thinner sourdough batter cooked like pancakes on a buttered griddle.

Authentic touch. Start with a starter that is already bubbly and has been fed ahead of time. Stir in the soda at the last minute.

How it is used

Hot, with butter and jam or syrup.

Field notes

  • The city's sourdough fame is a bread story. These pancakes are a breakfast use of that starter.
  • The starter has to be fed before you begin. That feeding is not the active prep time.

Ingredients

  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 1/4 cups whole milk
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the griddle

Sudanese kisra

Thin, tangy sorghum sheets with a flexible, faintly bitter grain taste.

History

Kisra is the fermented sorghum flatbread of Sudan, cooked in sheets thin enough to fold. The grain is sorghum, dura in the local market, mixed with water and soured before it ever meets the pan. The same bread is part of the daily table in South Sudan as well. It is an accompaniment to mullah, the stews, including okra. This recipe is 100 percent sorghum, so it is gluten-free if the flour is uncontaminated.

Cultural background

Kisra is torn or folded and used to pinch stew. A meal can be several sheets beside a bowl of bamia, okra, or another mullah. The ferment is ordinary home practice, not a special-occasion loaf. Cooks who have an older batter use a spoon of it to start the next one.

Why it tastes this way

Sorghum has a grainy, slightly tannic taste that wheat does not. Fermentation adds a clean sourness and loosens the batter so it can be spread paper thin. The hot plate dries the surface just enough to peel the sheet off while the inside stays flexible. No sweetener is part of the bread.

How it is made

Stir sorghum flour into water and ferment it until sour, salting only at the end. On a hot flat griddle, spread a ladle of batter in a quick thin sweep and cook it on one side until it peels free. Fold the sheets and keep them covered so they stay pliable for scooping stew.

Authentic touch. Spread the batter thinner than you think you should. Kisra is a sheet, not a plump pancake. Keep the flour sorghum only if you want the gluten-free version written here.

How it is used

Kisra is the utensil for Sudanese stews. Okra mullah and other vegetable or meat stews sit beside a stack of folded sheets. The bread on this page has no meat in it. South Sudanese tables use the same kind of sheet.

Field notes

  • Kisra is fermented sorghum, cooked in thin sheets rather than fluffy rounds.
  • The bread is also a staple in South Sudan, which is why that country is listed here.
  • One hundred percent sorghum kisra is gluten-free if the flour was not cut with wheat.

  2. Leave it at room temperature for one to two days, until it smells sour and small bubbles hold on the surface. Stir once if it separates.
  3. Stir in the salt and thin the batter until it spreads under a quick push of the ladle.
  4. Heat a flat griddle or a large skillet until a drop of water dances. Wipe on a trace of oil only if needed. Pour a ladle of batter and sweep it into a thin sheet.
  5. Cook on one side until the sheet releases and the top looks dry. Fold it onto a plate and cover it. Repeat with the remaining batter.

Gluten-free only when the sorghum flour has no wheat in it. Taste the stew you serve alongside if you are watching chile.

Fold cooled kisra in a clean cloth, then refrigerate. Reheat it covered so it bends instead of cracking. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of old batter starts the ferment faster.
  • Some cooks add a little wheat. That version is not gluten-free and is not this recipe.
  • A slightly thicker sheet is easier for a beginner and less like market kisra.

  • Savory meal
  • Okra stew, bamia
  • A dried-okra mullah
  • A bowl of stewed vegetables

India · Mild · 6 uttapams

Flying Pancakes of the World

India · South Asia

Uttapam

A thick, soft fermented cake topped with onion, tomato, and green chile.

35 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 3 · 6 uttapams

History

Uttapam is the thick member of the South Indian fermented rice and urad family. Instead of a thin spread, the cook pours a deeper round and presses onion, tomato, or chile into the wet top. Tamil homes and vegetarian hotels both claim it as everyday breakfast food. It uses the dosa ferment, then chooses toppings and thickness rather than a new flour.

Cultural background

The toppings go on while the batter is still soft so they sink and hold. A crisp paper dosa cannot carry tomato pieces. Uttapam can. Green chile keeps the heat mild if you leave the seeds out.

Why it tastes this way

A deeper batter stays moist, so the ferment tastes round and a little sweet. Onion and tomato steam on the covered pan and add juice. Chile and curry leaf keep it savory. The underside still toasts against the pan, which is why uttapam is a griddle cake and not a steamed idli with vegetables.

How it is made

Pour thick rounds of fermented rice and urad batter, press onion, tomato, and chile into the top, cover, and flip once.

Authentic touch. Press the toppings in with a spatula as soon as you pour. If you wait until the top dries, they fall off when you flip.

How it is used

Serve open so the vegetables show, with coconut chutney. Two small uttapams make a breakfast.

Field notes

  • Uttapam is thick on purpose. Spreading it thin turns it back into a dosa.
  • Onion, tomato, and chile are toppings on the ferment, not a change of grain.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups fermented rice and urad dosa batter
  • 1 small onion, finely chopped
  • 1 small tomato, seeded and chopped
  • 1 green chile, minced
  • 2 tablespoons chopped cilantro
  • Neutral oil for the pan
  • Salt if the batter needs it

Steps

  1. Start with bubbly fermented rice and urad batter. If it is stiff, stir in a spoon of water. It should hold a thick round.
  2. Mix onion, tomato, chile, and cilantro in a bowl. Salt them lightly if the batter is already salted.
  3. Oil a medium-hot pan. Pour a ladle of batter and let it spread only into a cake about a centimeter thick.
  4. Scatter a handful of the vegetable mix on top and press gently. Drizzle oil around the edge. Cover for about 2 minutes.
  5. Flip once and cook until the vegetable side takes a little color. Serve the vegetable side up.

Use fermented batter that smells clean and sour, not rotten.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Grated carrot in place of tomato
  • A few curry leaves pressed into the top

  • Sambar
  • Corriander leaves

Yemen · No chili heat · About 10 spongy cakes

Flying Pancakes of the World

Yemen · Middle East

Yemeni lahoh

Spongy sorghum cakes, sour and earthy, with holes that hold beans or egg.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · About 10 spongy cakes

History

Yemeni lahoh is a spongy fermented pancake, often on the breakfast table beside foul, which is stewed fava beans, or a fried egg and a spoon of samn, clarified butter. Sorghum is a traditional flour for it. This page uses sorghum alone and stays in Sana'a. Djiboutian lahoh and Somali canjeero are related names with their own recipes. They are not this plate of foul and eggs.

Cultural background

Lahoh is morning food. The batter is mixed ahead, and the pan is busy while tea is poured. Foul is a separate pot. The bread can also be eaten with honey or with a slick of samn. The sour crumb is supposed to meet something rich.

Why it tastes this way

Sorghum tastes earthier and drier than wheat, and the ferment adds sourness that cuts through beans and butter. The holes are small cups for foul or egg yolk. Because this batter has no wheat and no sugar, it does not eat like a sweet Djibouti breakfast cake. Samn on top tastes nutty because it is clarified butter, not because the grain is sweet.

How it is made

Mix sorghum flour and water and ferment it until sour. Salt it, then cook rounds on one side in a covered pan until the holes set. Serve with foul, fried eggs, or samn. Those accompaniments are cooked separately. The bread itself contains no wheat.

Authentic touch. Serve it as a Yemeni breakfast with foul or eggs. Cook one side until the surface is spongy. A wheat-and-sugar batter is a different lahoh. This one is sorghum.

How it is used

Yemeni lahoh is breakfast and a savory meal when it is next to foul or eggs. Honey is the sweeter path. Samn and eggs bring dairy and egg to the plate even though the batter has neither.

Field notes

  • Yemeni lahoh is often eaten with foul or eggs.
  • This recipe is sorghum only, so it can be gluten-free if the flour is pure.
  • The Djibouti and Somali pancakes with similar names are separate pages.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups sorghum flour (about 240 g)
  • 2 1/2 cups water, plus more as needed
  • 1/2 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt

Steps

  1. Whisk the sorghum flour, water, and yeast into a batter like thin cream. Leave the salt out. There is no wheat in this recipe.
  2. Cover it overnight, until it smells sour and shows bubbles. Give it longer if the room is cold.
  3. Stir in the salt. Add water if the batter thickened past a pour.
  4. Cook rounds in a covered nonstick pan on one side until the top is full of holes and no longer wet. Do not flip.
  5. Serve with foul or eggs. Those are the Yemeni breakfast partners, cooked in their own pans.

Gluten-free only if the sorghum flour is free of wheat. Foul and eggs are separate dishes with their own ingredients.

Refrigerate leftover lahoh and reheat it covered. Cooked foul should be refrigerated promptly and not left on the bread. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some cooks add wheat flour to help the batter hold. That version is not gluten-free.
  • A longer ferment tastes sharper and works well with bland beans.
  • Honey instead of foul makes the same bread a sweet breakfast.

  • Savory meal
  • Foul, stewed fava beans
  • Fried eggs
  • Samn, clarified butter
