Adai

A thick, savory pancake of mixed dals and rice, spiced with chile and curry leaf.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 adai

History

Adai is a Tamil pancake of rice and several lentils, coarser and spicier than dosa, often cooked for dinner as much as breakfast. A common accompaniment is jaggery and butter, or avial, a vegetable stew. The batter is ground and may rest a few hours. It is not a thin crisp dosa.

Cultural background

The mix of dals is the point: toor, chana, and moong with a little urad, or a similar house blend. Curry leaves and dried chile go into the grind.

Why it tastes this way

Several lentils make it taste beany, hearty, and more savory than a rice-dominant dosa. Chile and curry leaf are ground in, so the heat is in the cake, not only in a side chutney. A coarser grind keeps it textured. Jaggery on the side is a Tamil contrast, sweet against a spicy pancake, and it surprises people who expect only chutney.

How it is made

Soaked rice and mixed dals are ground coarse with chile and curry leaves and cooked as thick pancakes.

Authentic touch. Grind it coarse, like wet sand, not a smooth dosa cream. Cook it thicker, with oil, until the center is no longer raw. A thin adai dries out and misses the point.

How it is used

With jaggery, or with a vegetable stew, and a spoon of yogurt if you eat dairy.

Field notes

  • Adai is thicker and spicier than dosa, and it uses several dals.
  • Jaggery on the side is a real way to eat it.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup rice
  • 2 tablespoons toor dal
  • 2 tablespoons chana dal
  • 2 tablespoons moong dal
  • 1 tablespoon urad dal
  • 2 dried red chiles
  • 8 curry leaves
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Soak the rice and dals together 4 hours. Drain.
  2. Grind coarse with chiles, curry leaves, salt, and a little water.
  3. Rest 2 hours if you can. It is good the same day.
  4. Spread thick rounds on a hot oiled tawa and cook, turning, until both sides are browned and the center is cooked.
  5. Serve with jaggery or a stew.
  6. Oil the edges so they crisp.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A smoother grind makes it more like dosa and less like adai.
  • Fewer chiles make it milder. The classic is noticeably spiced.

Äggakaka

A thick, fried egg cake from Skåne, served with bacon and lingonberry.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · one cake

History

Äggakaka is a Scanian egg cake, associated with Skåne in southern Sweden. It is thicker and more egg than a usual pancake, fried in a pan, and served with pork and lingonberries. The contrast of salty meat, rich egg, and tart berries is the regional plate.

Cultural background

It is a meal, not a stack with syrup. The cake can be cut into wedges. Some cooks make it almost like a thick omelette.

Why it tastes this way

Eggs are the body of the cake, so it tastes custardy and browned rather than floury. Skåne is farming country, and the plate uses what a farm had: eggs, pork, and preserved lingonberries. The berries cut the fat. Flour is only there to keep the egg from scrambling into crumbs.

How it is made

Beaten eggs, a little flour, and milk are fried as one thick cake, then cut.

Authentic touch. Cook it slower than a crepe so the center sets without the bottom turning black. Cover the pan. A wet center is undercooked egg.

How it is used

With fried pork or bacon and lingonberry jam.

Field notes

  • Äggakaka is a Skåne dish. It is not the same as a thin Swedish pancake.
  • Lingonberry and pork are the traditional partners.

Ingredients

  • 6 large eggs
  • 1/2 cup milk
  • 1/3 cup flour
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • 8 ounces bacon or salt pork
  • Lingonberry jam, to serve

Steps

  1. Fry the bacon and set it aside. Leave a little fat in the pan.
  2. Whisk eggs, milk, flour, and salt.
  3. Pour into the pan and cook covered over medium-low until the center is just set.
  4. Brown the top under a broiler or flip carefully.
  5. Cut into wedges.
  6. Serve with the bacon and lingonberry jam.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A meatless version leaves off the pork and is then a different plate. The classic includes it.
  • More flour makes it more like an ordinary pancake and less like äggakaka.

Pairings and uses

  • Supper
  • Savory meal
  • bacon
  • lingonberry jam
  • Nut-free

Bánh Xèo

A crisp turmeric rice crepe filled with shrimp, bean sprouts, and herbs.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 crepes

History

Bánh xèo is the sizzling rice-flour crepe of Vietnam, named for the sound of batter on a hot pan. Turmeric colors it. Shrimp and pork are usual fillings, with bean sprouts and a pile of herbs, eaten wrapped in lettuce with nước chấm. This version uses shrimp and leaves out pork. Cambodian bánh xèo is a cousin. The crepe is rice flour and coconut milk.

Cultural background

It should be thin and shatter at the edge. The herbs are not a garnish. They are half the bite. The filling is savory and the wrapper is crisp.

Why it tastes this way

Rice flour and coconut milk fry into a lacy, rich crepe that wheat would make tough and pale. Turmeric is color and a mild earthiness. Shrimp is the sweet saline filling of a coastal crepe. Herbs and lettuce make it fresh. The high heat is why it crackles, the xèo of the name.

How it is made

A thin rice and coconut batter is cooked crisp, filled with shrimp and sprouts, and folded.

Authentic touch. Use a hot pan and a thin batter. Add the filling as soon as the batter sets, cover briefly so the shrimp cook, and fold. Do not stack the crepes or they steam soft.

How it is used

With lettuce, mint, and a dipping sauce of lime, fish sauce, and chile. Fish sauce adds fish. A soy-lime dip is the fish-free option and should be called that.

Field notes

  • The name refers to the sizzle.
  • This written filling is shrimp, not pork.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup rice flour
  • 1 cup coconut milk
  • 1/2 teaspoon turmeric
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 8 ounces shrimp
  • 2 cups bean sprouts
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Whisk rice flour, coconut milk, turmeric, salt, and enough water to make a thin batter.
  2. Oil a hot skillet, pour a thin crepe, and lay on shrimp and sprouts.
  3. Cover until the shrimp are opaque and the edge is crisp.
  4. Fold and serve.
  5. Do not stack.
  6. Herbs and lettuce belong on the plate.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pork alongside the shrimp is common.
  • A vegetarian filling of mushrooms is a different crepe.

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Street food
  • Supper
  • lettuce
  • mint
  • lime

Besan Cheela

A savory chickpea-flour pancake with onion, chile, and spices.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 3 · about 6 pancakes

History

Besan cheela, or chilla, is a North Indian savory pancake of chickpea flour, water, onion, and spices, cooked on a tawa. It is a fast breakfast, related to Gujarati pudla. It does not need a ferment. Besan is the flour left when chana dal is ground, and it is a pantry staple.

Cultural background

It is eaten with chutney, pickle, or yogurt. The pancake is vegan as written. Yogurt on the side adds dairy.

Why it tastes this way

Chickpea flour tastes nutty and binds with water alone, so the cake needs no egg or wheat. Ajwain or cumin, chile, and onion make it savory rather than flat. It is a breakfast of the chickpea-growing north, quick because there is no ferment. The gram flavor is the difference from a rice dosa.

How it is made

Besan, water, onion, and spices are mixed into a batter and cooked as pancakes.

Authentic touch. Rest the batter 10 minutes so the besan hydrates, or the center tastes raw. Cook it on medium. Besan burns at the edge before the middle sets if the pan is screaming.

How it is used

With green chutney or pickle.

Field notes

  • Besan cheela is an instant savory pancake. It is not fermented.
  • Chickpea flour keeps it gluten-free if the besan is pure.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup besan (chickpea flour)
  • 3/4 cup water
  • 1 small onion, finely chopped
  • 1 green chile, minced
  • 1/2 teaspoon cumin
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons chopped cilantro
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Whisk besan, water, onion, chile, cumin, salt, and cilantro. Rest 10 minutes.
  2. Cook small pancakes on a medium oiled tawa.
  3. Flip when the underside is browned and the top looks set.
  4. Cook through.
  5. Serve with chutney.
  6. The center should not taste of raw flour.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Moong dal cheela is the lentil cousin.
  • Pudla is the Gujarati besan pancake.

  • green chutney
  • pickle

Bindaetteok

A crisp mung-bean pancake with cabbage and kimchi.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 pancakes

History

Bindaetteok, also nokdujeon, are mung-bean pancakes. Soaked mung beans are ground, mixed with kimchi or vegetables, and fried. They are a popular snack and drinking food, and an older grain cake than the wheat pajeon. This version keeps them free of wheat and meat.

Cultural background

The edge should be lacy. The bean flavor is earthy. Kimchi adds acid. A dipping sauce is optional because the cake is already seasoned.

Why it tastes this way

Mung beans taste earthy and fry crisp when the batter is coarse, not a smooth milk. Kimchi juice seasons the batter without a long spice list. The cake is a bean pancake in a wheat-pancake culture, which is why it has its own name. Oil and a hot pan make the lace. A blender that purees it too fine makes a dense cake.

How it is made

Soaked mung beans are ground coarse, mixed with kimchi and cabbage, and fried as thick pancakes.

Authentic touch. Grind the beans until they are gritty, not baby-food smooth. Squeeze a little of the water off if the batter is a puddle. Fry in enough oil that the edge frills.

How it is used

Hot, in wedges. A vinegar dip is optional.

Field notes

  • Bindaetteok are mung-bean pancakes, also called nokdujeon.
  • A coarse grind fries lacier than a smooth puree.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup dried mung beans, soaked 8 hours and drained
  • 1/2 cup chopped kimchi
  • 1/2 cup chopped napa cabbage
  • 1 clove garlic
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Grind the beans with garlic and a splash of water until coarse and spreadable.
  2. Stir in kimchi, cabbage, and salt.
  3. Fry four thick cakes in a filmed skillet until both sides are crisp and the center is cooked.
  4. Drain.
  5. Serve hot.
  6. Add water only if the batter will not spread.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pork in the batter is a common version and adds meat.
  • A smooth grind is a different, heavier texture.

Bolani

A thin stuffed flatbread filled with potato or lentils, pan-cooked.

30 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 bolani

History

Bolani are Afghan stuffed flatbreads, pan-cooked, filled with potato, lentils, or leeks. They are a celebratory and everyday food. This recipe is the potato filling. A chutney on the side is common. The dough is unleavened and thin.

Cultural background

They are cut into wedges. The filling should be dry and well salted. Cilantro in the potato is typical.

Why it tastes this way

A thin dough and a savory potato filling make a meal you can cook on a pan, which is why bolani show up at gatherings and at home. Cilantro and green chile brighten the potato. The griddle toasts the bread. It is a stuffed bread in the same broad family as gözleme, with Afghan seasoning, not a copy of the Turkish dish.

How it is made

Unleavened dough is filled with seasoned potato, folded, and cooked on a griddle.

Authentic touch. Mash the potato dry. A wet filling bursts. Roll the dough thin, seal the edges, and cook on medium with a little oil until the bread is spotted and the filling is hot.

How it is used

Hot, in wedges, with a yogurt or chutney sauce if you eat dairy. The bread is vegan.

Field notes

  • Potato bolani are a standard filling. Lentils are the other.
  • Keep the filling dry so the bread stays crisp.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups flour
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 3/4 cup water
  • 2 cups mashed potato
  • 2 tablespoons chopped cilantro
  • 1 green chile, minced
  • Oil

Steps

  1. Mix a soft dough and rest 15 minutes.
  2. Season the potato with salt, cilantro, and chile. It should be dry.
  3. Fill and seal 4 thin rounds.
  4. Cook on a medium oiled griddle until spotted and hot inside.
  5. Cut into wedges.
  6. Serve warm.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A lentil filling is another classic and stays vegan.
  • Leek bolani are a third filling.

  • Stuffed or filled
  • Supper

Buckwheat Blini

Darker, earthier yeasted blini with a buckwheat bite.

1 hr 40 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · about 16 blini

History

Buckwheat blini, grechnevye blini, are the older-tasting Russian pancake, made with buckwheat flour and yeast. Buckwheat was a staple grain. These blini are especially associated with Maslenitsa and with savory toppings. A portion of wheat flour in many home recipes makes them less fragile. This version uses both and says so.

Cultural background

They are smaller and darker than white blini. Sour cream, mushrooms, or fish are the partners. The batter should taste faintly sour.

Why it tastes this way

Buckwheat grows in a short season and tastes mineral, which is why these blini do not need sugar. Yeast sourness plus the grain tastes deeper than a white pancake. Butter and sour cream are the Maslenitsa fats. A little wheat keeps the cake from crumbling on the spatula, a practical compromise cooks actually use.

How it is made

Buckwheat and wheat flours are mixed with yeast, milk, and egg, risen, and cooked as small cakes.

Authentic touch. Do not expect them to taste like a diner pancake. If they taste bitter, the buckwheat was scorched on too hot a pan. Keep the heat moderate.

How it is used

Sour cream and butter, or mushrooms. Caviar is optional and is fish.

Field notes

  • Buckwheat blini are a Maslenitsa food and an everyday savory pancake.
  • This recipe includes some wheat so the cakes hold.

Ingredients

  • 3/4 cup buckwheat flour
  • 3/4 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 1 1/2 cups warm milk
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons melted butter

Steps

  1. Mix flours, yeast, sugar, and milk. Rest 45 minutes.
  2. Add salt, egg, and butter. Rest 30 minutes.
  3. Cook small cakes on a buttered griddle over medium.
  4. Flip when set.
  5. Serve with sour cream.
  6. They should be tender, not crisp crackers.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • All-buckwheat batter is more traditional and harder to flip.
  • White blini are the milder page.

Pairings and uses

  • Holiday table
  • Savory meal
  • sour cream
  • butter
  • mushrooms
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Cachapas

A sweet-corn pancake, soft and irregular, often folded with cheese.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 6 cachapas

History

Cachapas are Venezuelan pancakes of fresh ground sweet corn, cooked on a griddle and often folded around cheese, queso de mano or similar. They are street and home food. The batter is the corn itself, sometimes loosened with a little milk or water. This recipe keeps the batter dairy-free and serves cheese as an optional fold, so the cake can be vegan and the cheese is a choice.

Cultural background

The corn must be sweet and juicy. Canned corn, drained and blended, is a workable off-season substitute and tastes less alive. Say which you used.

Why it tastes this way

Fresh corn is sweet because the kernels are immature and full of sugar, which is why a cachapa tastes like corn and dessert at once even when the filling is salty cheese. The griddle browns the natural sugar. No wheat is required. The cheese, when used, is salty and squeaky, the contrast. Street griddles make them irregular. That rustic edge is normal.

How it is made

Fresh corn is blended with a little salt and cooked as thick pancakes.

Authentic touch. Blend the corn until it is a coarse batter, not a juice. Cook on medium and cover briefly so the center sets. It will not look like a smooth wheat pancake.

How it is used

Folded, with cheese if you eat dairy, or plain.

Field notes

  • Cachapas are fresh-corn pancakes. Cheese is optional here.
  • Canned corn works and tastes less alive than fresh.

Ingredients

  • 4 ears sweet corn, kernels cut off
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon cornmeal, if needed
  • Oil
  • Cheese, optional

Steps

  1. Blend the corn and salt to a coarse batter. Add cornmeal if it is watery.
  2. Cook thick rounds on a medium oiled griddle, covered briefly.
  3. Flip when the underside is spotted.
  4. Fold with cheese if you eat dairy.
  5. Serve hot.
  6. They will look rustic.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of cornmeal helps a watery blend hold.
  • Costa Rican chorreadas are the close cousin.

Crespelle

Thin Italian pancakes, usually rolled around a savory filling and baked.

30 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 rolls

History

Crespelle are the Italian crêpe, used more often as a savory wrapper than as a lemon-sugar street snack. Baked crespelle with spinach and ricotta, or with a meat ragù, appear in several regions. Cannelloni are sometimes made from crespelle instead of from pasta tubes. The pancake itself is a thin egg batter.

Cultural background

This recipe is the plain crespelle and a simple spinach and ricotta filling, which is a meatless classic. A meat ragù is a different dish.

Why it tastes this way

An Italian table that already made pasta used the same flour and eggs to make a thin cake when a baked dish was wanted. Ricotta and spinach taste mild and green, and a little nutmeg is the traditional perfume. Baking under tomato or butter keeps the crêpe from drying. The cake is a vessel. The filling is the flavor.

How it is made

Thin pancakes are filled with seasoned spinach and ricotta, rolled, and baked with a little butter and cheese.

Authentic touch. Cook the crespelle only until they release. If they are brittle, they crack around the filling. A damp towel over the stack keeps them flexible while you fill.

How it is used

Hot, as a first course or supper.

Field notes

  • Crespelle are the Italian crêpe, most often baked with a filling.
  • Cannelloni can be pasta or crespelle. This page is the pancake.

Ingredients

  • 3/4 cup flour
  • 2 eggs
  • 1 cup milk
  • Pinch of salt
  • 1 tablespoon butter
  • 1 cup ricotta
  • 1 cup cooked chopped spinach, squeezed dry
  • 1/4 teaspoon nutmeg
  • 1/4 cup grated Parmesan
  • Salt and pepper

Steps

  1. Make thin crespelle from the flour, eggs, milk, salt, and butter.
  2. Mix ricotta, spinach, nutmeg, half the Parmesan, salt, and pepper.
  3. Fill and roll the crespelle. Lay them in a buttered dish.
  4. Dot with butter and the rest of the cheese.
  5. Bake at 375°F about 15 minutes.
  6. Serve hot.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A meat filling is common and changes the allergens.
  • Unfilled, with sugar, they are closer to a French crêpe and less typical.

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Stuffed or filled
  • Supper
  • tomato sauce
  • parmesan
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Derbyshire Oatcakes

A soft oat pancake, a neighbor to the Staffordshire cake.

1 hr 10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 6 oatcakes

History

Derbyshire oatcakes are the neighboring county’s soft oat pancake, sold and cooked at home in a tradition that overlaps Staffordshire’s. They are large, yeasted, and savory. Treating them as a separate named cake respects the county line cooks actually use, without inventing a dramatic difference that is not there. The batter is oat-forward and flexible.

Cultural background

They are breakfast wrappers for cheese or bacon, much like Staffordshire oatcakes. Some Derbyshire versions are a little thicker.

Why it tastes this way

The same industrial Midlands pantry, oats plus a little wheat and yeast, produces a savory pancake because oats were cheaper than a white-flour diet and yeast made a bread you could fold. Small differences in thickness and sweetness are house differences. The flavor is oat and salt.

How it is made

An oat and wheat yeast batter is cooked as soft pancakes on a griddle.

Authentic touch. If you want them a little thicker than Staffordshire cakes, use a bit less water. Do not add sugar. Sweet oatcakes are a different British food.

How it is used

Cheese or eggs. Roll or fold them.

Field notes

  • Derbyshire and Staffordshire oatcakes are close relatives with local names.
  • Neither one is a crisp Scottish oatcake.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/4 cups oat flour
  • 1/4 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 1/4 cups warm water
  • 1 egg
  • Oil for the griddle

Steps

  1. Mix all the batter ingredients and rest 1 hour.
  2. Cook thin rounds on a medium oiled griddle.
  3. Flip when the top sets.
  4. Stack under a towel.
  5. Fill while warm.
  6. They keep a day in the fridge and reheat in a dry pan.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • The Staffordshire page is the twin, with the shop tradition of Stoke called out there.
  • A pinch of sugar appears in some home recipes. This one stays savory.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • cheese
  • eggs

Netherlands · Western Europe

Dutch Pannenkoeken

A large, thin-but-sturdy pancake, often eaten with bacon or apple.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 large pancakes

History

Pannenkoeken are the Dutch pancake: larger and a bit thicker than a French crêpe, cooked one to a plate, and eaten with sweet or savory toppings. Pannenkoeken restaurants serve them as a meal. Bacon cooked into the batter, or apple, or a syrup pour, are all ordinary. They are not the American Dutch baby, whose name comes from Deutsch, and they are not poffertjes.

Cultural background

A family pan cooks one large cake. You eat it with a knife and fork. Stroop, a syrup, or powdered sugar are sweet finishes. Cheese and bacon are the savory ones.

Why it tastes this way

Pesarattu is an Andhra crepe made from whole green gram, moong, often with ginger and green chile. It is frequently served with upma, a semolina porridge, as pesarattu-upma. The crepe itself is the page. Upma is a partner and contains wheat. The pesarattu batter does not.

Cultural background

It is a breakfast crepe, thinner than a cheela and more specific than a generic moong pancake. Ginger is not optional if you want it to taste like pesarattu.

Why it tastes this way

Green gram tastes earthy and slightly sweet, different from urad dal in a classic dosa. Ginger and green chile cut that earthiness. There is often no long ferment, or only a short one, so the flavor stays beany rather than sour. Andhra breakfasts pair it with upma for a soft, salty contrast. The crepe is the protein. The upma is the wheat side.

How it is made

Soaked green gram is ground with ginger and chile and cooked as thin crepes.

Authentic touch. Grind the soaked moong very smooth with ginger. Cook it thinner than a cheela and oil the edges so it crisps. Do not expect dosa sourness.

How it is used

With ginger chutney, or with upma if you want the classic Andhra plate. Upma is a separate wheat dish.

Field notes

  • Pesarattu is green gram, an Andhra breakfast crepe.
  • The crepe is gluten-free. Upma beside it is not.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup whole green gram (moong), soaked 4 hours and drained
  • 1 inch ginger
  • 1 green chile
  • 1/2 teaspoon cumin
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Water, to grind
  • Oil for the pan

Steps

  1. Grind dal, ginger, chile, cumin, salt, and enough water to make a spreadable batter.
  2. Cook thin crepes on a hot oiled tawa until the edges lift and the underside browns.
  3. Flip briefly.
  4. Serve warm.
  5. The batter should be smooth.
  6. Ginger should be visible as a flavor, not a hint.

Qutab

History

Qutab are Azerbaijani stuffed flatbreads, folded into half-moons and cooked on a dry griddle called a saj. Greens qutab, with herbs, and meat qutab are the two main kinds. This recipe is the herb one, so it stays meatless. Sumac and yogurt are common at the table. The dough is unleavened and thin.

Cultural background

They are a staple and a street food. The herb mix often includes cilantro, dill, and green onion. Pomegranate or sumac on the finished bread is a typical sour note.

Why it tastes this way

A thin dough cooks in a minute and tastes of the herbs inside, not of a thick batter. Greens in quantity are the dish, a way to eat herbs as a meal. The dry saj browns the dough in spots without frying it. Sumac’s lemony acidity is the finish, a Caucasus flavor that replaces a heavy sauce.

How it is made

Unleavened dough is filled with seasoned herbs, folded, and cooked on a dry griddle.

Authentic touch. Roll the dough thin enough to see the herbs faintly. A thick qutab is doughy. Cook it dry or with the faintest oil, and press the edges so the filling does not leak.

How it is used

Warm, with sumac and yogurt if you eat dairy. The bread itself has no dairy.

Field notes

  • Herb qutab and meat qutab are both traditional. This is the herb one.
  • The saj is a griddle. A heavy skillet is the home stand-in.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 cup water

Raggmunk

Grated raw potato in a batter, fried, with pork and lingonberry.

History

Raggmunk is a Swedish potato pancake made with raw grated potato in a batter, as opposed to rårakor, which are grated potato fried without much batter. Both are served with salted pork and lingonberries. The combination is a classic Swedish everyday meal.

Cultural background

The cake is crisp at the edge. The berries and the pork do the sweet-and-salt work. This page includes the pork as the traditional plate and marks the meat allergen.

Why it tastes this way

Raw potato fries up sweeter and lacier than mashed potato. The batter of flour, egg, and milk holds the shreds into a cake you can flip. Lingonberry cuts the fry. Salt pork is the preserved meat of a winter kitchen. Together they are a balanced plate from a cold climate that stored potatoes, berries, and cured pork.

How it is made

Raw potato is stirred into a thin batter and fried as cakes, served with pork and lingonberry.

Authentic touch. Squeeze some water from the potato, not all of the starch. A wet batter steams. Fry in a generous film of fat until the cake is actually crisp.

How it is used

With fried salt pork or bacon and lingonberry jam.

Field notes

  • Raggmunk has batter. Rårakor are mostly potato.
  • Lingonberry and pork are the documented partners.

Ingredients

  • 4 medium potatoes, peeled and grated
  • 1/2 cup flour
  • 1 large egg
  • 1/2 cup milk
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • Oil or butter for frying
  • 8 ounces bacon or salt pork
  • Lingonberry jam

Steps

  1. Squeeze the grated potato lightly and mix with flour, egg, milk, and salt.
  2. Fry the pork until browned and set it aside.
  3. Fry spoonfuls of batter in the fat until crisp on both sides.
  4. Drain.
  5. Serve with the pork and jam.
  6. Keep the heat moderate so the potato cooks through.

Grated, squeezed potato is fried in thin cakes in a hot pan.

Salt, lingonberry jam, and sour cream or a fried egg if you eat dairy and eggs.

Rösti

A broad potato cake, crisp on the outside and soft within.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · one 8-inch cake

History

Rösti is a Swiss potato cake, originally a farmer’s breakfast in German-speaking cantons, now a national side dish. Grated potato is fried in one cake and flipped. The “röstigraben” is a joking name for the cultural line between German- and French-speaking Switzerland, which says something about how identified the cake is with the German-speaking side. Butter is common. This version uses oil so it can be dairy-free, and butter is noted as the traditional fat.

Cultural background

It is a side for meat or a meal with a fried egg. Parboiled potato is easier to bind. Raw potato makes a crisper, more fragile cake.

Why it tastes this way

Potato starch, fried, is the whole flavor: sweet, browned, and salty. Alpine farms stored potatoes, and a skillet cake was a way to make them a meal. One large cake, rather than little latkes, is the Swiss shape, so the inside steams while the crust shatters. The lack of onion in the plain version keeps it milder than a latke.

How it is made

Cooked potatoes are grated, seasoned, and fried as one cake in a skillet, then flipped.

Authentic touch. Parboil the potatoes and chill them, then grate. Press the cake and leave it alone until it releases. A nervous spatula breaks it. Flip with a plate if you need to.

How it is used

As a side, or with a fried egg and cheese. The cake itself is only potato, salt, and fat.

Field notes

  • Plain rösti is potato and fat. Fillings are extras.
  • A single cake, flipped once, is the form.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 pounds waxy potatoes, boiled until just tender and chilled
  • 3/4 teaspoon salt
  • Black pepper
  • 3 tablespoons oil or butter

Steps

  1. Grate the cold potatoes and toss with salt and pepper.
  2. Heat the fat in a skillet. Add the potato and press it into a cake.

  4. Flip with a plate and cook the second side.
  5. The center should be hot and the crust crisp.
  6. Slide onto a plate and cut into wedges.

Russian Blini

Yeasted wheat pancakes, lightly sour, full of small holes.

1 hr 40 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · about 16 blini

History

Blini are yeasted Russian pancakes. During Maslenitsa, the week before the Orthodox Great Lent, they are the festival food, tied in folk explanation to the sun and to using up butter and eggs before the fast. Maslenitsa is a real holiday on the Orthodox calendar. The sun symbolism is folk interpretation, so it is named here as that, not as doctrine. Blini are also eaten the rest of the year with butter, sour cream, herring, or caviar.

Cultural background

A risen batter makes them different from a baking-powder pancake. Toppings are rich because the week before Lent was for finishing dairy. Caviar is a famous topping and is not required.

Why it tastes this way

Yeast and a warm rest give a slight tang and a holey crumb that holds melted butter. Maslenitsa explains the butter: the fast ahead limited dairy, so the week used it. Wheat blini are milder than buckwheat ones. Sour cream and salt fish are the savory logic of a rich, mild cake. The holiday is religious and seasonal, which is why the same batter tastes like a feast in that week and like breakfast later.

How it is made

A yeasted batter of flour, milk, egg, and butter is risen twice and cooked as small pancakes.

Authentic touch. Let the batter rise until puffy. Cook the blini smaller than a dinner plate so they stay tender. Brush the cooked side with butter if you want the classic sheen.

How it is used

Butter, sour cream, smoked fish, or jam. Herring and caviar are traditional and add fish.

Field notes

  • Maslenitsa is the Orthodox pre-Lenten week, and blini are its pancake.
  • The sun story is folk symbolism, not a verified ancient rite this page can date.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups warm milk
  • 1 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 1 1/2 cups all-purpose flour
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 large egg
  • 2 tablespoons melted butter, plus more for the pan

Steps

  1. Stir milk, yeast, sugar, and flour. Rest 45 minutes until bubbly.
  2. Beat in salt, egg, and melted butter. Rest 30 minutes more.
  3. Cook small pancakes on a buttered medium griddle.
  4. Flip when the surface is full of holes.
  5. Brush with butter.
  6. Serve warm with sour cream.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Buckwheat blini are the earthier Maslenitsa cousin.
  • A spoon of batter saved as a starter makes the next batch sourer.

History

Scallion pancakes, cong you bing, are a Chinese pan bread: a dough rolled with oil and scallions into a coil, then fried until flaky. They are eaten across China and in Taiwan as a snack and a side. How it is made

A soft dough is coiled with oil and scallions and pan-fried.

How it is used

Cut into wedges, with a soy-vinegar dip.

Socca

Chickpea flour, olive oil, salt, and a blistered surface.

40 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · one 10-inch pancake

History

Socca is the chickpea pancake of Nice, baked very hot, often in a wide copper pan, and sold by the piece as street food. It is a cousin of Ligurian farinata, across the border in Italy. The shared batter is chickpea flour, water, olive oil, and salt. In Nice it is peppered and eaten hot, sometimes with a finger-burn warning from the vendor.

Cultural background

It is a snack, not a dessert, and not a wheat crêpe. A home oven at its highest heat, or a screaming skillet, is the workable version of the wood oven.

Why it tastes this way

Chickpea flour tastes nutty and stays eggless, which is why socca is gluten-free and vegan. Olive oil crisps the top. A fierce oven browns it in patches the way a griddle would, only faster. The Mediterranean port link with Genoa explains the cousin farinata. The two cities cooked the same batter and did not pretend it was French pastry.

How it is made

Chickpea flour, water, olive oil, and salt are rested, poured into a hot pan, and baked until blistered.

Authentic touch. Rest the batter so the flour hydrates, and bake it thin in a preheated oiled pan. Thick socca stays pasty. Eat it immediately. It toughens as it cools.

How it is used

Hot, with black pepper, as a snack. It needs no syrup.

Field notes

  • Socca and farinata are the same family across the French-Italian border.
  • Chickpea flour needs a rest or it tastes raw and floury.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup chickpea flour
  • 1 cup water
  • 2 tablespoons olive oil, plus more for the pan
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Black pepper

Steps

  1. Whisk chickpea flour, water, oil, and salt. Rest 30 minutes.
  2. Heat the oven to 450°F or as high as it goes. Oil a metal pan and heat it.
  3. Pour in a thin layer of batter.
  4. Bake until the surface is blistered and the edges are brown, about 10 to 15 minutes.
  5. Pepper it.
  6. Cut and eat at once.

Spekdik

A thick bacon pancake, salty and substantial.

History

Spekdik is a bacon pancake from the northeast of the Netherlands, especially Drenthe and Groningen. Pieces of spek, pork belly or bacon, are cooked in the batter so the fat flavors the cake. It is a regional farm dish, heartier than a city crêpe, and it is still served at local events and at home.

Cultural background

It is a meal, not a dessert. Syrup on a corner of the plate is a Dutch habit even with bacon, and some people do that. Others keep it savory.

Why it tastes this way

Pork fat from the bacon bastes the batter as it cooks, which is why the crumb tastes salty and rich without a lot of butter. The northeast’s farming table had pork and flour and a pan big enough for one thick cake. How it is made

Bacon is set in a pan, a thick batter is poured over, and the cake is cooked slowly, then flipped.

Authentic touch. Start the bacon in the pan so some fat renders, then pour the batter over it. If the bacon goes in raw on top of a thick batter, it stays pale.

How it is used

Cut into wedges. Mustard or syrup, depending on the house.

Field notes

  • Spekdik belongs to Drenthe and Groningen, not to every Dutch pancake menu.
  • It is a bacon dish. The plain pannenkoek page is the meatless cake.

How it is made

How it is used

Warm, with cheese, or with bacon if you eat meat. Roll them while they are flexible.

Thalipeeth is a Maharashtrian savory flatbread made from bhajani, a roasted multigrain flour that often includes wheat, sorghum, chickpea, and other grains. The multigrain mix is the Maharashtrian pantry, a way to use several local grains in one bread. Yogurt on the side is cool and tangy. Wheat in the mix is why it holds together and why it is not a dosa.

How it is made

A soft dough of mixed flours, onion, and spice is patted into rounds and cooked on a tawa with oil.

Authentic touch. Pat the dough onto a wet cloth or parchment if it sticks to your hands, then invert it onto the tawa. Poke a hole in the middle and add a few drops of oil there.

How it is used

With yogurt or pickle. Yogurt adds dairy. The bread is vegan.

Field notes

  • Thalipeeth is a multigrain Maharashtrian bread, not a crepe.

15 min prep · 10 min cook · Serves 4 · about 12 fritters

History

Tortillitas de camarones are a Cádiz tapa: tiny shrimp in a thin chickpea-flour batter, fried into lacy cakes. They are a specialty of the Bay of Cádiz, where small shrimp and chickpea flour meet. They are fritters, and they are the shrimp pancake of southern Spain.

Cultural background

They should be thin and crisp, eaten immediately with a squeeze of lemon. The shrimp are small. If you use larger shrimp, chop them.

Why it tastes this way

Chickpea flour fries into a nutty lace, and the shrimp taste sweet and saline because they are the local catch, not a garnish. A very thin batter is why you can see the shrimp. Olive oil is the frying fat of Andalusia. Lemon is the acid. The tapa is a port food.

How it is made

Chickpea flour, water, parsley, and chopped shrimp are fried as thin lacy cakes.

Authentic touch. The batter should be as thin as cream. Fry spoonfuls in hot oil and do not let them brown black. Drain well. Eat at once.

How it is used

Immediately, with lemon.

Field notes

  • These are a Cádiz specialty.
  • Thin batter and hot oil make the lace.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup chickpea flour
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 4 ounces small shrimp, chopped
  • 2 tablespoons chopped parsley
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Olive oil for frying
  • Lemon

Steps

  1. Whisk chickpea flour, water, parsley, and salt into a thin batter. Stir in the shrimp.
  2. Fry spoonfuls in hot oil until lacy and golden.
  3. Drain.
  4. Serve at once with lemon.
  5. Stir the batter so the flour does not settle.
  6. The shrimp should be cooked through.

Fry over steady heat. Shrimp should be cooked through.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Without shrimp they are a different, vegan chickpea fritter and should not keep the name.
  • Onion in the batter is used by some cooks.

Uttapam

A thick, savory dosa pancake with onion and chile cooked into the top.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 3 · about 6 uttapams

History

Uttapam is a thick pancake made from dosa batter, topped with onion, tomato, chile, and coriander, and cooked softer than a crisp dosa. It is a South Indian breakfast and a way to use batter that has fermented a little longer than you meant. The toppings are pressed into the wet surface.

Cultural background

It is eaten with chutney. It is not folded around potato. The vegetables are the face of the cake.

Why it tastes this way

A thicker pour steams into a spongy, sour cake. Onion and green chile on top taste fresh and sharp against the fermented batter. The sourness of a well-fermented batter is an advantage here, which is why leftover dosa batter becomes uttapam. Oil around the edge crisps the rim while the center stays soft.

How it is made

Thick dosa batter is cooked covered with onion, chile, and tomato pressed into the top.

Authentic touch. Pour thick, sprinkle the toppings at once, and press them in gently. Cover the pan. Flip only if you must. Many cooks brown only the bottom.

How it is used

With coconut chutney.

Field notes

  • Uttapam is the thick savory pancake of dosa batter.
  • Leftover fermented batter is a traditional reason to cook it.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups fermented dosa batter
  • 1 small onion, chopped
  • 1 tomato, finely chopped and drained
  • 1 green chile, minced
  • 2 tablespoons chopped cilantro
  • Oil
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Salt the vegetables lightly.
  2. Pour a thick round of batter on a medium-hot oiled tawa.
  3. Scatter onion, tomato, chile, and cilantro and press them in.
  4. Cover and cook until the top is set and the bottom is browned.
  5. Flip carefully if the top is still wet.
  6. Serve with chutney.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A plain thick uttapam without vegetables is still uttapam and less interesting.
  • Tomato makes it wetter. Pat the pieces dry.
