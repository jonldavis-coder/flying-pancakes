Sweet Crêpes

A thin wheat pancake with a tender, lightly sweet crumb.

40 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 10 crêpes

History

Crêpes are thin pancakes cooked across France. In Brittany they are a regional pride, and February 2, Chandeleur, is the candle-day associated with eating them. That date is Candlemas, not Shrove Tuesday, though both belong to the late-winter Christian calendar. Sweet crêpes are usually wheat. Savory buckwheat galettes are the other Breton cake and have their own page.

Cultural background

Street stands and home kitchens both cook them. Sugar, jam, chocolate, or a squeeze of lemon are ordinary finishes. Crêpes Suzette is a restaurant dish, not the everyday cake.

Why it tastes this way

A rested batter of flour, egg, milk, and butter spreads paper-thin and tastes of browned butter rather than of baking powder. Wheat, not buckwheat, is what makes a sweet crêpe pale and mild enough for sugar. Chandeleur explains a spike in home cooking. The rest of the year, the cake is simply a fast dessert.

How it is made

A thin wheat batter is swirled in a hot, lightly buttered pan and cooked briefly on both sides.

Authentic touch. Rest the batter at least 30 minutes, or the gluten snaps back and the crêpe tears. The first one seasons the pan and is rarely the prettiest.

How it is used

Sugar, jam, lemon, or a chocolate spread. Fold or roll.

Field notes

  • Chandeleur, February 2, is the French crêpe day most often named. It is not the same day as Shrove Tuesday.
  • Sweet crêpes are wheat. Galettes are buckwheat.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 2 large eggs
  • 1 1/4 cups milk
  • 2 tablespoons melted butter, plus more for the pan
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • Pinch of salt

Steps

  1. Blend or whisk into a smooth batter. Rest 30 minutes.
  2. Heat a crêpe pan and film it with butter.
  3. Swirl a thin layer and cook until the edge browns.
  4. Flip for a few seconds.
  5. Fill or sugar, and fold.
  6. Keep finished crêpes warm under a plate.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Buckwheat galettes are the savory Breton cousin.
  • A splash of beer or cider in the batter is a Breton home habit and changes the flavor.

Pairings and uses

Galette Bretonne

Earthly buckwheat, lacy and flexible, with a toasted edge.

40 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 galettes

History

The Breton galette is a buckwheat crêpe. Buckwheat reached Brittany and became a staple on poor soils where wheat was less dependable. Galettes are the savory cake. Wheat crêpes are the sweet one. A galetterie serves them as a meal, often with cider.

Cultural background

The plain galette is the bread. The complète, with ham, cheese, and egg, is a separate named serving on its own page. This recipe is the buckwheat cake itself, which is naturally free of wheat, egg, and dairy if you cook it in oil.

Why it tastes this way

Buckwheat has a mineral, slightly bitter taste that stands up to cheese and egg and does not suit sugar the way wheat does. Brittany’s damp climate and acid soils favored the crop. A watery batter cooked very thin gets lacy holes. Water, not milk, is the traditional liquid in many Breton recipes, which keeps the flavor on the grain.

How it is made

Buckwheat flour and water, with salt, are rested and cooked as thin savory crêpes.

Authentic touch. Use a hot, barely greased pan, ideally a flat billig if you have one. A heavy skillet works. Do not add sugar. If the galette tears, the batter needs a spoon more water and a hotter pan.

How it is used

Butter, cheese, egg, or simply folded beside a salad. Cider is the Breton drink with them.

Field notes

  • A galette in Brittany usually means buckwheat. In other parts of France the word can mean a different cake.
  • Water is the classic liquid. Milk makes a milder, less traditional batter.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup buckwheat flour
  • 1 1/4 cups water
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon oil, for the pan

Steps

  1. Whisk flour, water, and salt until smooth. Rest 30 minutes or overnight in the fridge.
  2. Heat a skillet until a drop of water dances. Oil it lightly.
  3. Swirl a thin layer.
  4. Cook until the surface looks dry and the edge lifts.
  5. Flip briefly.
  6. Stack and cover so they stay flexible.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A little wheat flour is sometimes added to make flipping easier. That version is not gluten-free.
  • Galette complète is this cake with ham, cheese, and an egg.

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Street food
  • Supper
  • butter
  • cheese
  • cider
  • egg
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Galette Complète

Buckwheat folded around ham, cheese, and a soft egg.

40 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 filled galettes

History

A galette complète is the Breton restaurant plate: a buckwheat galette filled with ham, cheese, and an egg. The name means the complete one, the galette as a full meal. It is newer as a standard menu item than the plain buckwheat cake, and it is the version most visitors meet.

Cultural background

The egg is often cracked onto the galette while the second side finishes, so it sets in the cake. Gruyère is a common cheese. The ham is a thin slice.

Why it tastes this way

Buckwheat’s bitterness is why the filling is salty and rich rather than sweet. Ham and cheese were pantry foods that turned a farm cake into a meal you could sell. The egg sets from the heat of the pan and tastes soft, not scrambled, if you cover the pan for a minute. Cider cuts the salt.

How it is made

A buckwheat galette is topped with ham, cheese, and a cracked egg, covered until the white sets, then folded.

Authentic touch. Crack the egg onto the cooked side and cover the pan. If you stir the egg, you have scrambled eggs on a pancake, which is pleasant and not a complète.

How it is used

Folded in a square, with cider or a green salad. The yolk may still be soft. Say so if you are serving it.

Field notes

  • Complète means ham, cheese, and egg together. Leaving one out makes a different order.
  • The buckwheat cake is the older staple. This filled plate is the meal built on it.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup buckwheat flour
  • 1 1/4 cups water
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 4 thin slices ham
  • 1 cup grated Gruyère
  • 4 large eggs
  • Oil or butter for the pan

Steps

  1. Make the batter as for a plain galette and rest it.
  2. Cook a galette on one side, flip, and lay on ham and cheese.
  3. Crack an egg in the center and cover until the white sets.
  4. Fold the edges in to make a square.
  5. Slide it onto a plate.
  6. Repeat. The yolk can stay soft, so cook the white through.

The white should be set. A fully firm yolk is the safer choice if you are unsure about soft eggs.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A cheese-and-egg galette without ham is a simpler plate and is not a complète.
  • The plain galette page is the cake with no filling.

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Supper
  • Stuffed or filled
  • cider
  • green salad
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Crêpes Suzette

Wheat crêpes in orange butter, with a citrus sauce.

40 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · about 8 crêpes

History

Crêpes Suzette are thin crêpes in an orange butter sauce, a restaurant dessert rather than a Breton farm cake. The origin stories, including who Suzette was, are disputed and are left out here. What is solid is the dish: crêpes, butter, sugar, and orange, flambéed in some dining rooms. This home version skips the tableside flame and reduces the orange sauce instead, which is safer and still the flavor.

Cultural background

It is a dessert course, made from the sweet crêpe batter. Grand Marnier or another orange liqueur is traditional in the sauce. The alcohol cooks off if you simmer it. You can leave it out.

Why it tastes this way

Orange zest’s oil is the perfume, and butter carries it. Sugar and a reduction make a sauce that clings to a thin crêpe instead of soaking it into porridge. The dish tastes like a restaurant because it is one: a plain crêpe dressed after the fact. Skipping the flame does not remove the orange. It removes a fire hazard.

How it is made

Sweet crêpes are folded into a butter and orange sauce and warmed through.

Authentic touch. Zest the orange before you juice it. Reduce the sauce until it coats a spoon, then add the crêpes and turn them. If the sauce is watery, the dessert tastes like juice.

How it is used

Immediately, as dessert, with the sauce spooned over.

Field notes

  • The dedication stories are disputed, so this page does not repeat them as fact.
  • This home recipe does not ask you to set alcohol on fire.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 2 large eggs
  • 1 1/4 cups milk
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • Pinch of salt
  • 1 tablespoon melted butter
  • 4 tablespoons butter, for the sauce
  • 3 tablespoons sugar, for the sauce
  • Zest and juice of 1 orange

Steps

  1. Make the crêpe batter, rest 30 minutes, and cook thin crêpes.
  2. In a wide pan, melt 4 tablespoons butter with 3 tablespoons sugar, the zest, and the juice.
  3. Simmer until the sauce looks syrupy.
  4. Add the folded crêpes and turn them in the sauce for a minute.
  5. Serve at once.
  6. Do not flame the sauce for this version.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A tablespoon of orange liqueur in the sauce is traditional. Simmer it.
  • Lemon is a sharper, less Suzette finish.

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • orange
  • vanilla ice cream
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Ficelle Picarde

A crêpe rolled around ham and mushrooms in a creamy sauce, then baked.

40 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 rolls

History

Ficelle picarde is a specialty of Picardy in northern France: a crêpe rolled around a filling of ham, mushrooms, and a creamy sauce, then baked with more sauce and cheese. The name means a Picard string, from the rolled shape. It is a regional restaurant and home dish of the twentieth century, built on the thin pancake rather than on buckwheat.

Cultural background

It is a first course or a light supper. The crêpe is wheat. The filling is savory and rich.

Why it tastes this way

Northern France’s cooking leans on dairy, ham, and mushrooms. A thin crêpe is a way to hold a creamy filling without a pie crust. Baking browns the cheese and thickens the sauce. It tastes of thyme, mushroom, and ham, not of sugar. The region’s difference from Brittany is wheat and cream instead of buckwheat and cider.

How it is made

Wheat crêpes are filled with ham and a mushroom cream, rolled, and baked with a little more sauce and cheese.

Authentic touch. Cook the mushroom liquid off before you add cream, or the sauce runs out of the crêpe in the oven.

How it is used

Hot, as a first course or supper, with a salad.

Field notes

  • Ficelle picarde is a rolled and baked crêpe, not a galette complète.
  • Picardy is in the north. The cake is wheat.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup flour
  • 2 eggs
  • 1 1/4 cups milk
  • Pinch of salt
  • 1 tablespoon butter
  • 8 ounces mushrooms, sliced
  • 4 slices ham
  • 1/2 cup cream
  • 1/2 cup grated Gruyère
  • Salt and pepper
  • 1 teaspoon thyme

Steps

  1. Cook thin crêpes from the flour, eggs, milk, salt, and butter. Rest the batter first.
  2. Fry the mushrooms in a little butter until their liquid evaporates. Add thyme, salt, pepper, and half the cream.
  3. Lay ham and mushrooms on each crêpe, roll, and place in a buttered dish.
  4. Spoon on the rest of the cream and the cheese.
  5. Bake at 400°F about 15 minutes, until bubbling.
  6. Serve hot.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A vegetarian filling of mushrooms only is a home change and is then not the classic ficelle.
  • Crème fraîche can replace the cream.

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Supper
  • Stuffed or filled
  • green salad
  • Nut-free

Normandy Apple Crêpes

Wheat crêpes with sautéed apple and a little butter.

40 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 crêpes

History

Normandy is apple and dairy country, famous for apples, cider, and butter. Crêpes filled with sautéed apples are a regional way to use that fruit. They are home cooking more than a single codified restaurant dish. Calvados, the apple brandy, is a local option in the pan and is not required.

Cultural background

The apples are cooked until soft and a little browned, then rolled in a plain sweet crêpe. A spoon of cream or a thread of cider finishes the plate.

Why it tastes this way

Norman apples are often tart cooking apples, which is why the filling needs butter and a little sugar and still tastes sharp. Butter from the region’s dairies is the fat. The crêpe stays plain so the fruit is the flavor. Cider in the pan, if you use it, reduces to an apple sauce rather than a drink.

How it is made

Apples are sautéed in butter with sugar, then rolled in wheat crêpes.

Authentic touch. Cook the apples until they are tender and the edges color. Raw apple in a hot crêpe stays crunchy and watery.

How it is used

Warm, with a little of the pan juices. Cream is optional.

Field notes

  • Normandy’s apple and butter culture is the reason for the filling.
  • This is a home regional plate, not a claim of one official recipe.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup flour
  • 2 eggs
  • 1 1/4 cups milk
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • Pinch of salt
  • 1 tablespoon melted butter
  • 2 apples, sliced
  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • 1 tablespoon sugar, for the apples

Steps

  1. Make and rest a sweet crêpe batter. Cook thin crêpes.
  2. Sauté the apples in butter and sugar until tender and lightly browned.
  3. Spoon apples onto the crêpes and fold.
  4. Warm them briefly in the pan.
  5. Serve with the pan juices.
  6. A spoon of cream is optional.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A tablespoon of cider in the apple pan is the local deglaze. Simmer it dry.
  • Calvados is traditional and alcoholic. Skip it and the dessert is still Norman apple.

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • apples
  • cider
  • cream
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Pain Perdu

Stale bread soaked in milk and egg, fried in butter.

15 min prep · 10 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 slices

History

Pain perdu, lost bread, is the French kitchen’s way of frying stale bread that has been soaked in milk and egg. The name says the bread would have been wasted. It is the ancestor of American French toast and a relative of New Orleans pain perdu, which is richer and often orange-scented. The French home version is simpler: stale bread, custard, butter, and sugar.

Cultural background

It is breakfast or a last-minute dessert. Brioche makes it cake-like. A lean country loaf makes it more bread-like. Both are fair if the bread is dry.

Why it tastes this way

Stale bread drinks custard because it has lost moisture. Fresh bread turns to paste. Butter browns the milk sugars on the surface. The thrift is the cultural fact: a bakery culture had leftover loaves, and the pancake journal includes this because it is the fried, egg-soaked cousin of the crêpe, not because it is poured batter.

How it is made

Stale bread is dipped in sweetened milk and egg and fried in butter.

Authentic touch. Dry the slices overnight if they are fresh. Soak them until heavy, then let them drip. Fry in butter over medium so the center custard sets.

How it is used

Sugar, honey, or fruit.

Field notes

  • The name means lost bread, bread that would have been thrown out.
  • Dry bread is the requirement. Fresh bread will not do.

Ingredients

  • 4 slices stale bread
  • 1 cup milk
  • 1 large egg
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon vanilla
  • Pinch of salt
  • 2 tablespoons butter

Steps

  1. Whisk milk, egg, sugar, vanilla, and salt.
  2. Soak the bread until the centers soften.
  3. Fry in butter over medium until deep golden on both sides.
  4. The center should be set.
  5. Sugar them lightly.
  6. Serve at once.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • The New Orleans page is the Creole version with orange zest.
  • Brioche needs less soaking than a tight crumb.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Dessert
  • sugar
  • honey
  • berries
  • Vegetarian
