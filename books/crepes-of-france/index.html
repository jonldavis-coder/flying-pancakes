Breton crepes

Thin wheat crepes from Brittany, butter and sugar, the cake of Chandeleur.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 10 crepes

History

The French crepe day is Chandeleur, Candlemas, on 2 February. It is not Mardi Gras, and it is not Shrove Tuesday. Breton wheat crepes are the sweet crepe of Brittany, usually eaten with butter and sugar or a spoon of jam. The savory buckwheat cake is a galette and has its own page. A custom at Chandeleur is to flip a crepe with one hand while holding a coin in the other, as a wish for the year. The wish is not a cooking step.

Cultural background

Crepes are street food and home food in Brittany, cooked on a flat billig. Cider is the regional drink beside them. The wheat crepe is the one for sugar. Buckwheat is the one for ham and cheese.

Why it tastes this way

A thin batter browns in spots and stays flexible. Butter on the crepe, then sugar, makes a sauce as the sugar melts. The egg keeps it tender. Resting the batter hydrates the flour so the sheet does not tear. The flavor is toasted milk and butter, not spice.

How it is made

Flour, egg, milk, water, salt, and melted butter are whisked into a thin batter, rested, and cooked in a very thin layer.

Authentic touch. Say Chandeleur, 2 February, when you mean the French crepe day. Do not call this a Mardi Gras recipe. Spread the batter with a wooden T or swirl the pan. The first crepe still counts as the test.

How it is used

Folded with butter and sugar, or with jam. At a creperie they are the sweet side of the menu. The savory side is the galette.

Field notes

  • Chandeleur, Candlemas, is 2 February. It is the French crepe day, not Mardi Gras.
  • In Brittany the wheat crepe is usually sweet. The buckwheat galette is usually savory.
  • Holding a coin while you flip the crepe is a Chandeleur custom, not part of the batter.

Ingredients

  • 125 g plain wheat flour
  • 2 large eggs
  • 250 ml whole milk
  • 100 ml water
  • 1 pinch fine salt
  • 30 g unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the pan and for serving
  • 3 tbsp sugar, for serving

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, eggs, milk, water, salt, and melted butter into a smooth thin batter. Rest 20 minutes.
  2. Heat a crepe pan and brush it with butter. Pour a thin layer and swirl.
  3. Cook until the edge lifts and the underside is spotted, then turn and cook the second side for a few seconds.
  4. Brush with butter, sprinkle with sugar, and fold. Repeat with the rest of the batter.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A squeeze of lemon
  • Normandy apple crepes, which are the filled apple version

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Feast or fast day
  • Snack
  • Butter and sugar
  • Jam
  • Dry cider
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Buckwheat galette

A savory Breton buckwheat crepe, lacy and a little bitter, cooked on a billig.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 galettes

History

In Brittany a galette is the buckwheat crepe, and it is usually savory. Buckwheat became a staple grain of inland Brittany. This page does not assign a century to that shift. The batter here is buckwheat flour, water, egg, and salt, cooked with butter. Wheat crepes are the other page. A galette complète, with ham, cheese, and egg, is also its own page. Use buckwheat milled apart from wheat if you need the cake to be gluten-free.

Cultural background

Galettes are everyday food, sold at markets and creperies with cider. The word galette means other flat cakes elsewhere in France. On this page it means the Breton buckwheat crepe.

Why it tastes this way

Buckwheat tastes earthy and slightly bitter, which is why sugar is the wrong default. Water, not milk, keeps that flavor clear. Butter on the billig browns the lace. An egg makes the sheet less fragile. Salt is not optional. The holes and crisp edge come from a hot surface and a thin batter.

How it is made

Buckwheat flour is whisked with salt, egg, and water, rested, and cooked in a thin lacy round on a buttered griddle.

Authentic touch. A galettière spreads the batter with a rozell, a wooden T, and lifts it with a spanell, a small spatula. A hot pan and a quick spiral are the home version of that pair of tools.

How it is used

Plain with a rub of butter, or as the base for cheese, egg, or ham. It is a savory meal, not a dessert crepe.

Field notes

  • In Brittany, galette means buckwheat and usually savory.
  • The wheat crepe is a different page, and so is the ham-and-egg complète.
  • This batter has no wheat. The gluten-free claim depends on buckwheat that was not milled with wheat.

Ingredients

  • 200 g buckwheat flour
  • 1 large egg
  • 450 ml cold water
  • 1/2 tsp fine salt
  • 30 g unsalted butter, for the pan

Steps

  1. Whisk the buckwheat flour, egg, water, and salt into a thin batter. Rest 30 minutes. It will look darker than a wheat batter.
  2. Heat a wide pan until hot and grease it with butter.
  3. Pour a thin layer, swirl, and cook until the edge crisps and the top looks dry. Turn if you want both sides colored. Many cooks leave the top soft.
  4. Rub with a little butter and serve, or add a savory filling before folding.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Galette complète, with ham, cheese, and an egg
  • A wheat-flour blend, which some shops use and which removes the gluten-free diet

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Salted butter
  • Dry cider
  • Grated firm cheese
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Galette complète

A buckwheat galette with ham, cheese, and an egg cooked in the middle.

20 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 filled galettes

History

A galette complète is the Breton creperie standard: a buckwheat galette topped with ham, a firm cheese, and an egg that cooks on the hot cake. Complète means the usual complete filling, not a claim that other fillings are incomplete meals. The batter matches the buckwheat galette page. The ham makes this a meat dish. Stories about a single creperie inventing the trio are not needed to cook it, and this page does not pick one.

Cultural background

It is lunch and dinner in Brittany, folded in a square so the yolk stays visible, and served with cider. The egg should still be soft unless you ask for it set.

Why it tastes this way

Buckwheat is bitter and toasted. Ham is salty. Cheese melts into fat. The egg yolk sauces the dry lace of the galette. Butter on the griddle ties them together. A hard, dry yolk misses the point of the dish.

How it is made

A buckwheat galette is cooked, then ham and cheese go on, an egg is cracked on top, and the cake is covered until the white sets and folded square.

Authentic touch. Crack the egg onto the galette while the top is still hot, cover the pan for a minute, and fold the four sides in after the white sets. Use a firm cheese such as Gruyère. Do not build this on a sweet wheat crepe.

How it is used

As a meal, with a green salad and cider. It is not a dessert.

Field notes

  • Complète means ham, cheese, and egg on buckwheat.
  • The egg is cooked on the galette, not fried in a separate pan.
  • Fold it square so the yolk stays in the middle.

Ingredients

  • 200 g buckwheat flour
  • 1 large egg, for the batter, plus 4 eggs for the filling
  • 450 ml cold water
  • 1/2 tsp fine salt
  • 40 g unsalted butter
  • 4 thin slices cooked ham
  • 120 g grated firm cheese, such as Gruyère

Steps

  1. Whisk the buckwheat flour, the batter egg, water, and salt. Rest 30 minutes.
  2. Cook a thin galette in butter on a hot pan until the top is set.
  3. Lay on a slice of ham and a quarter of the cheese. Crack an egg in the center. Cover the pan and cook until the white sets and the yolk is still soft.
  4. Fold the four sides over the filling, slide onto a plate, and repeat.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Cheese and egg without ham, which drops the meat allergen and can be vegetarian
  • A wheat-flour galette, which is a different batter and not gluten-free

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • Dry cider
  • A green salad
  • A second galette if the table is sharing
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Crêpes Suzette

Thin crepes in orange butter, with a warm orange sauce.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 crepes in sauce

History

Crêpes Suzette are dessert crepes folded in orange butter and an orange sauce, often finished with a warmed orange liqueur. Stories that credit one chef, one restaurant, or one royal lunch are disputed. This page keeps the dish and does not pick a winner among those stories. The crepes are ordinary wheat crepes. The orange butter is what makes them Suzette. They are a restaurant dessert, not the Chandeleur custom, though the same thin crepe batter can be used on 2 February.

Cultural background

The dish belongs to a dining room more than to a weekday kitchen, but the sauce is not difficult. The theatrics of a flame are optional. The orange and butter are not.

Why it tastes this way

Orange zest oils the butter. Sugar and juice make a syrup that coats a thin, slightly bitter crepe. A little liqueur sharpens the peel. If you burn the butter, the sauce turns harsh. The crepe should stay soft enough to fold, with a few browned spots.

How it is made

Thin crepes are cooked first. Butter, sugar, orange zest, and juice make a sauce in a wide pan, the crepes are turned in it, and a spoon of orange liqueur can be warmed and lit.

Authentic touch. Rub the sugar with the zest before it meets the butter, so the oil from the peel is in the sauce. If you light the liqueur, do it in the pan, never by pouring from the bottle.

How it is used

At once, as a dessert, folded in quarters. They are not a breakfast stack.

Field notes

  • The origin stories for the name Suzette are disputed.
  • Orange butter is the dish. The flame is optional.
  • These are a dessert service, not the Chandeleur custom itself.

Ingredients

  • 125 g plain wheat flour
  • 2 large eggs
  • 300 ml whole milk
  • 1 pinch fine salt
  • 80 g unsalted butter, divided
  • 60 g sugar
  • Zest of 2 oranges and 150 ml orange juice
  • 2 tbsp orange liqueur, optional

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, eggs, milk, and salt into a thin batter. Rest 15 minutes. Cook eight thin crepes in a little of the butter and set them aside.
  2. Rub the sugar with the orange zest. Melt the remaining butter in a wide pan, add the sugar, and stir in the orange juice. Simmer until the sauce looks syrupy.
  3. Fold the crepes into quarters and turn them in the sauce until coated.
  4. If you use liqueur, warm it in a separate spoon or small pan, add it, and light it in the pan if you want. Shake until the flame dies. Serve at once.

Do not pour alcohol from the bottle into a flame. Keep hair and sleeves back if you light the sauce.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • The sauce without a flame, which is the same dish
  • Lemon in place of orange, which is a different cake and closer to an English Shrove finish

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Orange liqueur in the sauce
  • Coffee after, not on the plate
  • Nothing else sweet beside them
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Ficelle picarde

A Picardy crepe rolled around ham and mushrooms in cream, then baked with cheese.

35 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 rolled crepes

History

Ficelle picarde is a baked stuffed crepe from Picardy, in northern France. A thin wheat crepe is rolled around a slice of ham and a duxelles of mushrooms bound with cream, then covered with more cream and cheese and baked. Ficelle means string, which fits the thin rolled shape. Stories that name a single inventor are disputed, so this page describes the dish without picking one. It is a first course in Picard restaurants, not a Breton galette.

Cultural background

Picardy cooking uses cream, mushrooms, and ham. The ficelle is that pantry rolled inside a crepe and browned. It is served hot, as a starter or a light meal with salad.

Why it tastes this way

The crepe goes crisp at the edges in the oven while the mushroom cream stays soft. Ham salts the mild mushrooms. Cheese browns and adds a savory crust. Nutmeg, if you use a scrape, should stay in the background. The flavor is cream and browned cheese more than sugar. There is no sugar in this recipe.

How it is made

Crepes are filled with ham and a mushroom-cream mixture, laid in a buttered dish, covered with cream and cheese, and baked until bubbling.

Authentic touch. Cook the mushroom moisture out before you add cream. A wet filling makes the crepe soggy in the dish. Roll them tight, seam down.

How it is used

Hot, as a first course or a light savory meal. It is not a sweet crepe.

Field notes

  • Ficelle means string. The crepe is rolled thin.
  • This is a Picardy baked dish, not a Breton galette.
  • Single-inventor stories are disputed, so the page does not name one.

Ingredients

  • 100 g plain wheat flour
  • 2 large eggs
  • 250 ml whole milk
  • 1 pinch fine salt
  • 40 g unsalted butter, divided
  • 250 g mushrooms, finely chopped
  • 200 ml heavy cream
  • 4 thin slices cooked ham
  • 60 g grated firm cheese

Steps

  1. Make a thin batter with the flour, eggs, milk, and salt. Rest 15 minutes and cook eight small crepes in a little butter.
  2. Fry the mushrooms in the remaining butter with a pinch of salt until the liquid has gone and they start to brown. Stir in half the cream and cook until thick.
  3. Lay a piece of ham and a spoon of mushrooms on each crepe. Roll tightly and place seam side down in a buttered baking dish.
  4. Pour over the rest of the cream, add the cheese, and bake at 200°C until bubbling and spotted brown, about 15 minutes.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Leeks instead of part of the mushrooms
  • A meat-free filling of mushrooms and cheese, which can be vegetarian if the crepe has no ham

Pairings and uses

  • Savory meal
  • A green salad
  • A dry white wine from the north, if you drink wine
  • Nothing sweet on the same plate
  • Nut-free

Normandy apple crepes

Wheat crepes filled with apples cooked in butter, a Normandy dessert.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 filled crepes

History

Normandy's apples and butter are the reason this crepe exists. Thin wheat crepes are filled with apples cooked in butter and sugar, sometimes with a splash of the region's apple brandy. This page uses apple brandy as an optional spoonful and does not require a named brand. The crepes are the Breton-style wheat batter. The filling is the Norman part. They are a dessert, not the savory galette and not the Chandeleur ritual itself.

Cultural background

Apple desserts run through Norman cooking: tarts, baked apples, and this filled crepe. Cream is a fair accompaniment. The apples should be a variety that keeps its shape when fried.

Why it tastes this way

Butter browns the apple edges. Sugar and a little lemon keep the fruit from tasting flat. The crepe is mild and slightly toasted, so the apple stays in front. Apple brandy, if you add it, should cook off its raw edge and leave the perfume of the peel. Cinnamon is optional and easy to overdo.

How it is made

Crepes are cooked, apples are fried in butter with sugar until tender, and each crepe is rolled around a spoon of apple.

Authentic touch. Cook the apples until they are tender and spotted gold before they go into the crepe. Raw apple in a hot crepe does not become a filling. A firm eating apple works if you do not have a local Norman variety.

How it is used

Warm, as a dessert, with the apple juices spooned over. A spoon of cream is optional.

Field notes

  • The Norman part is the apple and the butter, not a different crepe grain.
  • Buckwheat galettes are the savory Breton page, not this dessert.
  • Apple brandy is optional. The cooked apples are not.

Ingredients

  • 125 g plain wheat flour
  • 2 large eggs
  • 300 ml whole milk
  • 1 pinch fine salt
  • 50 g unsalted butter, divided
  • 3 firm apples, peeled, cored, and sliced
  • 40 g sugar
  • 1 tbsp lemon juice
  • 1 tbsp apple brandy, optional

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, eggs, milk, and salt. Rest 15 minutes. Cook thin crepes in a little of the butter.
  2. Melt the remaining butter in a frying pan. Add the apples, sugar, and lemon juice. Cook over medium heat until the apples are tender and golden at the edges.
  3. Add the brandy if you use it and let it cook for a minute. There is no need to light it.
  4. Spoon apples onto each crepe, roll, and serve warm with the pan juices.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A pinch of cinnamon
  • Skip the brandy and keep the butter and apples, which is the essential dish

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • The pan juices from the apples
  • Cold cream
  • A small glass of apple brandy, for drinkers
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Galette-saucisse

A pork sausage rolled in a buckwheat galette, the Rennes street snack.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 wraps

History

Galette-saucisse is a buckwheat galette rolled around a hot pork sausage. It is street food in Rennes and across Ille-et-Vilaine, sold at markets and at football matches. It is not the ham-and-egg complète, and it is not a sweet crepe. This recipe grinds the sausage at home from pork, salt, and pepper so the filling has no wheat filler. A shop sausage can contain cereal. If you use one, read the label before you call the plate gluten-free.

Cultural background

It is food you eat in your hand, often with a line of mustard. Mustard is optional here and, if you add it, it is a mustard allergen. The galette has to be flexible enough to wrap a hot sausage without cracking.

Why it tastes this way

Buckwheat is earthy and dry at the edge. Pork is salty and rich. Together they do not need a sauce. The contrast is the point: a crisp galette and a juicy sausage. Pepper in the meat is enough heat for this page, which stays at no chili.

How it is made

A buckwheat galette batter is cooked in thin rounds. Pork is seasoned, shaped into thin sausages, fried, and rolled inside the galettes.

Authentic touch. Wrap the sausage while both parts are hot, galette first, sausage inside, seam down. A cold sausage in a cold galette is a different and worse snack. Cook the galette in oil here so the cake itself stays dairy-free.

How it is used

In the hand, as street food or a match-day snack. It is a meat meal.

Field notes

  • This is the Rennes street wrap: sausage in buckwheat, not ham and egg.
  • It is sold at markets and at football matches.
  • A packaged sausage may contain wheat. This recipe seasons plain pork so the wrap stays gluten-free.

Ingredients

  • 200 g buckwheat flour
  • 1 large egg
  • 450 ml cold water
  • 1/2 tsp fine salt, plus 1 tsp for the pork
  • 400 g ground pork
  • 1/2 tsp black pepper
  • 2 tbsp neutral oil, for cooking

Steps

  1. Whisk the buckwheat flour, egg, water, and 1/2 tsp salt. Rest 20 minutes.
  2. Mix the pork with 1 tsp salt and the pepper. Shape four thin sausages. Fry them in a spoon of oil until cooked through and browned. Set aside.
  3. Cook four thin galettes in a lightly oiled pan.
  4. Roll each hot sausage in a hot galette. Serve at once, with mustard if you want it.

Cook the pork until it is no longer pink in the center.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A shop pork sausage with no cereal filler
  • Cheese added, which is tasty and no longer dairy-free

Pairings and uses

  • Street food
  • Savory meal
  • Mustard, which adds a mustard allergen
  • Dry cider
  • Nothing else required
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Socca

A crisp-edged chickpea flatbread from Nice, eaten hot with black pepper.

40 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 1 large socca, about 6 pieces

History

Socca is the chickpea flatbread of Nice, cooked in a wide pan and eaten hot, cut into pieces and peppered. Farinata, the Ligurian cousin, has its own page. The batters are related and the cakes are not identical: socca is usually thinner and more of a street portion. This recipe is chickpea flour, water, olive oil, and salt. There is no wheat, egg, or dairy, so it is gluten-free and vegan if your chickpea flour was not milled with wheat.

Cultural background

In Nice, socca is street food, sold hot and often eaten standing up. It also belongs at the start of a meal. It is not a sweet crepe and not a sandwich bread.

Why it tastes this way

Chickpea flour turns sweet and nutty when it roasts, without any nuts in the recipe. Olive oil keeps the center soft and the edge crisp. Salt and a heavy grind of black pepper are the seasoning. An undercooked center tastes of raw flour. High heat is what makes the browned spots.

How it is made

Chickpea flour is whisked with water, oil, and salt, rested, poured thin in an oiled pan, and baked very hot until spotted and set.

Authentic touch. Pepper it after it comes off the heat, not only in the batter. Eat it hot. Cold socca loses the contrast between crisp edge and custardy middle.

How it is used

Hot, in torn pieces, as a street snack or a first plate. It is vegan as written.

Field notes

  • Socca is Nice. Farinata is Liguria. They are cousins with separate pages.
  • The batter is chickpea flour, water, olive oil, and salt.
  • It is eaten hot, with pepper, often as street food.

Ingredients

  • 150 g chickpea flour
  • 300 ml cold water
  • 3 tbsp olive oil, plus more for the pan
  • 1/2 tsp fine salt
  • Black pepper, for serving

Steps

  1. Whisk the chickpea flour, water, olive oil, and salt until smooth. Rest 30 minutes. Skim any foam.
  2. Heat the oven to 230°C, or as hot as it will go. Oil a wide ovenproof pan and heat it.
  3. Pour in the batter in a thin layer. Bake until the surface is set and browned in spots, about 12 to 15 minutes.
  4. Pepper generously, cut or tear, and eat while hot.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Farinata, the thicker Ligurian oven cake on its own page
  • A rosemary leaf on top, more common on farinata than on Nice socca

Pairings and uses

  • Street food
  • Snack
  • Black pepper
  • A green salad after
  • Olives
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free
