Åland Pancake

A baked rice and cardamom pancake from the Åland Islands.

30 min prep · 45 min cook · Serves 8 · one 8-inch dish

History

Åland pancake is a baked rice porridge pancake from the Åland Islands, which are Swedish-speaking and autonomous within Finland. Cardamom and rice porridge are the base. It is a local dessert and café food, served with jam, often prune jam, and whipped cream. It is distinct from a thin lettu and from Gotland’s saffron pancake, though both are baked rice cakes of the Baltic.

Cultural background

The porridge is cooked, cooled a little, enriched, and baked until the top browns. Prune jam is the usual partner. Another dark jam is a fair substitute if you name it honestly.

Why it tastes this way

Rice cooked in milk tastes sweet and substantial, and cardamom perfumes it the way it perfumes Nordic buns. Åland’s position between Sweden and Finland shows in the language and in this cake, which sits beside both countries’ oven pancakes and keeps its own name. Prune jam is tart and dark, a preserved fruit that stands up to cream.

How it is made

Rice porridge is mixed with egg, sugar, and cardamom and baked in a buttered dish.

Authentic touch. Cook the rice fully before baking, or the finished cake has hard grains. The bake is for setting and browning, not for cooking raw rice.

How it is used

With prune jam or another dark jam, and whipped cream.

Field notes

  • Åland pancake is a named island dish, not a generic baked custard.
  • Prune jam is the traditional fruit partner.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup short-grain rice
  • 2 cups milk
  • 2 large eggs
  • 1/4 cup sugar
  • 1 teaspoon cardamom
  • 1 tablespoon flour
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • Butter for the dish

Steps

  1. Simmer rice and milk until the rice is soft and the porridge is thick. Cool 10 minutes.
  2. Stir in eggs, sugar, cardamom, flour, and salt.
  3. Pour into a buttered dish.
  4. Bake at 375°F about 40 minutes until set and brown.
  5. Rest 10 minutes.
  6. Serve with prune jam and cream.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Gotland’s pancake uses saffron. This one uses cardamom. Do not treat them as one recipe.
  • A gluten-free version can skip the spoon of flour. The rice is the body.

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Breakfast
  • prune jam
  • whipped cream
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Bahamian Johnny Cake

A slightly sweet, dense cornmeal bread, baked, with a tender crumb.

15 min prep · 35 min cook · Serves 8 · one 8-inch pan

History

Bahamian johnny cake is usually a baked bread of flour and cornmeal, slightly sweet, not the thin Rhode Island griddle cake that shares the name. The shared word traveled. The Bahamian loaf is a side for stew fish and peas and rice. It is included as the baked cousin, and the difference is stated so the names are not collapsed.

Cultural background

It is cut into squares. Coconut milk is used in some islands’ versions. This one uses evaporated milk’s richness with regular milk so the ingredient is clear, plus butter.

Why it tastes this way

Cornmeal and a little sugar make it taste sweeter and grainier than a plain biscuit. The bake, rather than a griddle, is why it is a bread you slice. The name links it to other Atlantic johnnycakes without pretending the methods match. Butter keeps it tender. It is a table bread for savory food.

How it is made

Flour, cornmeal, milk, sugar, and butter are baked in a pan as a dense bread.

Authentic touch. Do not overbake it. It should be set and pale gold, still moist. A dry johnny cake means it stayed in too long.

How it is used

Warm, with butter, beside a stew.

Field notes

  • Bahamian johnny cake is typically baked. The thin New England cake is a different method.
  • The shared name is worth explaining, not hiding.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup flour
  • 1 cup cornmeal
  • 1/4 cup sugar
  • 1 tablespoon baking powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup milk
  • 1/4 cup melted butter
  • 1 egg

Steps

  1. Heat the oven to 375°F and butter an 8-inch pan.
  2. Stir the dry ingredients. Add milk, butter, and egg.
  3. Spread in the pan.
  4. Bake about 30 minutes, until set and pale gold.
  5. Cool 10 minutes.
  6. Cut into squares.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Coconut milk instead of dairy milk is a coastal variation and removes dairy if the butter is also swapped for oil.
  • Rhode Island thin johnnycakes are the griddled namesake, not this loaf.

Pairings and uses

  • Bread course
  • Side dish
  • butter
  • stew
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Finnish Pannukakku

A cardamom-scented baked pancake, custardy in the center.

10 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 8 · one 9-by-13-inch pan

History

Pannukakku is Finland’s oven pancake, baked in a buttered pan and cut into squares. Cardamom is a frequent flavor, as it is in Finnish pulla. The Upper Peninsula of Michigan bakes a community version of the same dish, written separately because the American context is part of that story.

Cultural background

It is breakfast or a simple dessert with jam. One pan replaces a stack of flipped cakes.

Why it tastes this way

Cardamom’s perfume in a milky baked batter is the Finnish signature, a spice that became ordinary in Nordic baking through trade and stayed in Sunday buns and this pancake. The oven gives a browned rim and a soft middle. Jam adds the berry. The method is practical in a kitchen that is already heating the oven.

How it is made

Eggs, milk, flour, sugar, and cardamom are baked in a hot buttered pan.

Authentic touch. Melt the butter in the pan in the oven so the batter sizzles when it goes in. That sound is the bottom crust forming.

How it is used

With jam or berries.

Field notes

  • Cardamom is a traditional Finnish baking spice.
  • Pannukakku is baked. Lettu is cooked in a skillet.

Ingredients

  • 3 large eggs
  • 2 cups milk
  • 1 cup flour
  • 1/4 cup sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon cardamom
  • 3 tablespoons butter

Steps

  1. Heat the oven to 425°F and melt the butter in the pan in the oven.
  2. Whisk the rest into a smooth batter.
  3. Pour into the hot pan.
  4. Bake 25 to 30 minutes until puffed at the edges and set.
  5. It will fall when cut.
  6. Serve with jam.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • The Upper Peninsula page is the Finnish American pan.
  • Lettus are the thin flipped version.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Dessert
  • jam
  • berries
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Kentucky Spoonbread

A soft cornmeal custard, rich with butter and egg, spooned rather than sliced.

15 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 6 · one 8-inch dish

History

Spoonbread is a baked Southern cornmeal dish, soft enough to serve with a spoon. Kentucky and Virginia both claim it as a company table dish. It descends from cornmeal mushes and from soufflé-like breads that became popular in nineteenth-century American cooking. It is not a flipped pancake. It is the baked cousin of the corn griddle cake, included here because Appalachian and Southern tables treat it as part of the same corn-bread family.

Cultural background

It is a side for ham, beans, or a Sunday table, not a syrup stack. The crisp top and custard center are the contrast people wait for.

Why it tastes this way

Cornmeal scalded in milk tastes toasted and sweet, and eggs lift it in the oven the way they lift a Dutch baby. Wheat flour, when a recipe uses a spoon of it, is only a stabilizer. The flavor is corn, butter, and egg. Mountain kitchens that kept a cow and a sack of meal could make it without citrus, spice, or sugar.

How it is made

Cornmeal is cooked briefly with milk, enriched with butter and egg, and baked in a hot dish until set.

Authentic touch. Bake it in a preheated buttered dish so the edges fry a little. Serve it immediately. It sinks as it cools, which is normal, but it is best hot.

How it is used

Spoon it beside ham, greens, or beans. Butter on top is enough.

Field notes

  • Spoonbread is eaten with a spoon from the dish. It is not cut into squares like cornbread.
  • It belongs to the corn-bread family, which is why it sits in an Appalachian griddle book beside cakes that are actually flipped.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups whole milk
  • 3/4 cup plain cornmeal
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons butter, plus more for the dish
  • 2 tablespoons all-purpose flour
  • 3 large eggs, separated

Steps

  1. Heat the oven to 375°F. Butter a small baking dish and put it in the oven.
  2. Simmer milk, cornmeal, sugar, and salt, stirring, until thick, about 5 minutes.
  3. Stir in butter and flour. Cool 5 minutes.
  4. Beat in the yolks. Whip the whites to soft peaks and fold them in.
  5. Pour into the hot dish and bake about 35 minutes, until puffed and set.
  6. Serve at once with a spoon.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some recipes add a spoon of baking powder for a drier puff. This one stays custardy.
  • A spoon of white flour is traditional in some pots and is why this version is not marked gluten-free.

Pairings and uses

  • Side dish
  • Supper
  • Bread course
  • country ham
  • greens
  • butter
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

New England Popovers

Crisp shell, eggy steam, and a hollow center.

10 min prep · 35 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 popovers

History

Popovers are an American baked batter related to English Yorkshire pudding and to earlier popped breads. New England cookbooks have carried them since the nineteenth century as a breakfast bread and a partner for roast beef. They are not flipped. High egg and a very hot oven puff the batter into a hollow roll.

Cultural background

They come to the table in a basket, buttered, beside eggs at breakfast or a roast at dinner. The drama is the puff, which collapses if you open the oven early.

Why it tastes this way

Steam, not baking powder, lifts a popover. A hot oven turns the water in milk and egg into steam before the gluten and egg set, so the shell inflates. That is the same principle as Yorkshire pudding. New England’s version is usually a breakfast bread, served with butter and jam rather than roast drippings, because the region’s famous use of it moved from the meat course to the morning table.

How it is made

A thin batter of egg, milk, and flour is baked in a preheated hot pan until puffed and browned.

Authentic touch. Do not open the oven for the first 20 minutes. Fill the cups no more than half. A crowded cup cannot rise.

How it is used

Butter and jam at breakfast, or with a roast as you would Yorkshire pudding.

Field notes

  • Popover batter is close to Yorkshire pudding batter. The New England habit is often breakfast.
  • They are leavened by steam. Baking powder is not the engine.

Ingredients

  • 2 large eggs
  • 1 cup whole milk
  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon melted butter, plus more for the pan

Steps

  1. Heat the oven to 425°F. Butter a popover or muffin pan and put it in the oven for 5 minutes.
  2. Whisk eggs, milk, flour, salt, and butter into a smooth thin batter.
  3. Pour the batter into the hot cups, filling them halfway.
  4. Bake 20 minutes without opening the door.
  5. Lower the heat to 375°F and bake about 10 minutes more, until deep brown.
  6. Serve at once. They settle as they cool.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A little grated cheese makes a savory popover.
  • Whole wheat flour works but rises less.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Bread course
  • Side dish
  • butter
  • jam
  • roast beef
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Saffron Pancake of Gotland

A baked rice pancake perfumed with saffron, served with jam and cream.

30 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 8 · one 8-inch dish

History

Gotland’s saffron pancake is a baked dessert of rice porridge, cream, eggs, and saffron, associated with the island and often with the Christmas season. Saffron also scents Swedish Lucia buns, so the spice is a Swedish holiday flavor, not a random addition. The pancake is cut in squares. It is not a stack.

Cultural background

Rice porridge is cooked first, then mixed with the rest and baked. Dewberry or blackberry jam and cream are traditional partners. If you cannot get dewberry, say you are using blackberry or another dark jam.

Why it tastes this way

Saffron’s aroma is floral and bitter in a pinch too large, which is why a small amount perfumes a rich rice custard without turning it medicinal. Rice pudding as a base makes the cake thick and soothing. Gotland kept this as an island dessert. Holiday saffron links it to Lucia season, when the same spice is in the buns.

How it is made

Rice is cooked to a porridge, mixed with cream, egg, sugar, and saffron, and baked.

Authentic touch. Bloom the saffron in a spoon of warm milk so the color and smell spread. Stirring threads straight into a cold batter leaves yellow streaks and blank bites.

How it is used

Warm or cool, with dark jam and whipped cream.

Field notes

  • This is a Gotland baked pudding-pancake, not a crêpe.
  • Saffron is the defining spice, used in a small amount.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup short-grain rice
  • 2 cups milk
  • 1 pinch saffron threads
  • 2 large eggs
  • 1/4 cup sugar
  • 1/2 cup cream
  • 1 tablespoon flour
  • Butter for the dish

Steps

  1. Simmer the rice in the milk until soft and thick, about 20 minutes. Cool slightly.
  2. Bloom the saffron in a spoon of warm milk and stir it in.
  3. Beat in eggs, sugar, cream, and flour.
  4. Pour into a buttered dish.
  5. Bake at 375°F about 35 minutes until set and browned.
  6. Serve with jam and cream.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A pinch of cardamom beside the saffron is a home choice. Saffron should remain the main perfume.
  • Skip the saffron and you have a plain rice pancake, not the Gotland dish.

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Holiday table
  • blackberry jam
  • whipped cream
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Seattle Dutch Baby

A puffed oven pancake, crisp at the rim, eggy in the center.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · one 10-inch pancake

History

The Dutch baby is a German oven pancake. The “Dutch” in the name is generally explained as a muddle of Deutsch, not a recipe from the Netherlands. In Seattle, Manca’s Cafe is often credited with serving it in the early twentieth century and with the name that stuck in American restaurants. David Eyre’s pancake, a related skillet pancake published later, is a cousin. The Netherlands has its own pannenkoeken, which are a different food.

Cultural background

It is baked in a hot buttered skillet and brought to the table puffed, then collapses. Lemon and powdered sugar are the usual finish. Fruit is a fair variation.

Why it tastes this way

Steam from a hot pan and a wet, eggy batter inflates the rim before the center sets. That is why it looks like a popover the size of the skillet. Butter browns the bottom. Lemon is there because the cake is rich and needs acid. The Seattle story is immigration and restaurants, not a medieval Dutch guild.

How it is made

A blender batter of egg, milk, and flour is poured into a preheated buttered skillet and baked until risen.

Authentic touch. The skillet and the oven must be hot before the batter goes in. A cold pan makes a flat custard. Serve it as soon as it leaves the oven if you want anyone to see the puff.

How it is used

Lemon juice and powdered sugar, or fruit.

Field notes

  • Dutch here is widely understood as a twist on Deutsch. The cake is German in origin.
  • Manca’s Cafe in Seattle is the restaurant usually credited with the American name. Treat that credit as the common account.

Ingredients

  • 3 large eggs
  • 2/3 cup whole milk
  • 2/3 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 3 tablespoons butter
  • Lemon and powdered sugar, to serve

Steps

  1. Heat the oven to 425°F with a 10-inch ovenproof skillet inside.
  2. Blend eggs, milk, flour, sugar, and salt until smooth.
  3. Add the butter to the hot skillet and let it melt.
  4. Pour in the batter and bake 16 to 20 minutes, until puffed and browned.
  5. Do not open the door early.
  6. Dust with sugar and lemon and serve at once.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A savory baby skips the sugar and is finished with herbs and cheese.
  • David Eyre’s version is the same family, often in a smaller skillet.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Dessert
  • lemon
  • powdered sugar
  • berries
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Ugnspannkaka

A baked pancake slab, custardy and browned at the rim.

10 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 6 · one 9-by-13-inch pan

History

Ugnspannkaka means oven pancake. It is the Swedish batter baked in a pan instead of fried one cake at a time, close to Finnish pannukakku. It is weeknight food, cut into squares and served with jam or berries.

Cultural background

One dish feeds a household without anyone standing at the stove flipping. That practicality is the point.

Why it tastes this way

Milk and egg set in the oven into a soft center, and the edges brown where they meet the buttered pan. It tastes less fried than a skillet pancake and more like a custard bread. Jam on top supplies the acid. The method suits a cold evening: the oven heats the kitchen and the meal at once.

How it is made

A thin batter is poured into a hot buttered pan and baked until set.

Authentic touch. Bake it in a pan you have heated with butter, so the bottom fries a little. A cold glass dish makes a pale, steamed cake.

How it is used

Warm, with lingonberry or strawberry jam.

Field notes

  • The name means oven pancake.
  • It is the same batter family as skillet pannkakor, cooked in one pan.

Ingredients

  • 3 large eggs
  • 2 cups milk
  • 1 cup flour
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 3 tablespoons butter

Steps

  1. Heat the oven to 425°F. Melt the butter in a baking pan in the oven.
  2. Whisk eggs, milk, flour, sugar, and salt.
  3. Pour into the hot pan.
  4. Bake about 25 minutes until set and browned at the edges.
  5. Cut into squares.
  6. Serve with jam.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Finnish pannukakku is the close cousin, often with cardamom.
  • A handful of berries in the batter is a summer change.

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Breakfast
  • lingonberry jam
  • berries
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Upper Peninsula Pannukakku

A baked custard pancake, browned at the edge and creamy in the center.

10 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 8 · one 9-by-13-inch pan

History

Finnish immigrants settled in Michigan’s Upper Peninsula and in northern Minnesota to work mines and logging. They brought pannukakku, an oven pancake closer to a custard than to a flipped stack. In the Upper Peninsula it is still a community and home breakfast, sometimes baked in a large pan for a crowd. The Finnish original has its own page. This is the Upper Midwest pan.

Cultural background

It is cut into squares and served with butter, jam, or cardamom sugar. Cardamom is a Finnish baking spice that survived the move.

Why it tastes this way

An oven pancake puffs at the edge and stays custardy because it is mostly milk and egg, with just enough flour to set. That texture fits a cold climate breakfast: one pan, no standing at a griddle, and a dish that feeds a household. Cardamom came into Finnish baking through trade and stayed in American Finnish kitchens as the smell of holiday bread and of this pancake.

How it is made

A thin batter of milk, egg, flour, and sugar is poured into a hot buttered pan and baked.

Authentic touch. Bake it in a preheated buttered pan so the edges fry. If the pan is cold, the bottom steams and turns gray.

How it is used

Butter and jam, or sugar and a pinch of cardamom.

Field notes

  • Finnish settlement in the Upper Peninsula is why this oven pancake is a regional American breakfast.
  • Cardamom is a traditional Finnish baking spice, not a random addition.

Ingredients

  • 3 large eggs
  • 2 cups milk
  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1/4 cup sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground cardamom
  • 3 tablespoons butter

Steps

  1. Heat the oven to 425°F. Put the butter in a 9-by-13-inch pan and melt it in the oven.
  2. Whisk eggs, milk, flour, sugar, salt, and cardamom into a smooth batter.
  3. Pour the batter into the hot buttered pan.
  4. Bake about 25 to 30 minutes, until puffed and browned at the edges.
  5. It will settle when cut.
  6. Serve warm with jam.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • The Finnish pannukakku page is the same family baked at home in Finland.
  • A handful of berries in the pan is a summer change.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Holiday table
  • jam
  • butter
  • cardamom sugar
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Yorkshire Pudding

Crisp, eggy, and hollow, meant to catch gravy.

15 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 puddings

History

Yorkshire pudding is the baked batter of an English roast dinner, known in print by the eighteenth century. It was a way to make a roast feed more people: batter cooked under the meat, or in the drippings, so it puffed and took up the fat. Modern kitchens often bake it in a separate tin with oil or drippings. It is a cousin of the popover and of the Dutch baby, and it is not a sweet pancake.

Cultural background

Sunday roast beef with pudding, potatoes, and greens is the plate most people mean. The pudding can be one large tray or individual cups. Toad in the hole bakes sausages in the same batter and is a variation, not the classic pudding.

Why it tastes this way

Hot fat and a wet batter make steam, and the steam puffs the pudding before the egg and flour set. Beef drippings taste meaty. This version uses oil so the pudding itself is vegetarian, with a note that drippings are the roast-dinner fat. The flavor is mostly browned batter and salt, which is why gravy is served with it. The original economy was stretching a joint of meat.

How it is made

A thin batter of egg, milk, and flour is poured into preheated oil and baked until puffed.

Authentic touch. The tin and the fat must be smoking hot. Cold fat makes a flabby disk. Do not open the oven while it rises.

How it is used

With roast beef and gravy, or as a first course with gravy, which is an older way of serving it. Onion gravy is a fair vegetarian stand-in and is a separate recipe.

Field notes

  • Yorkshire pudding is a batter pudding, included here as the roast-dinner cousin of oven pancakes.
  • Hot fat is not optional. It is the reason the pudding rises.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 2 large eggs
  • 1 cup milk
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 3 tablespoons neutral oil or beef drippings

Steps

  1. Whisk flour, eggs, milk, and salt into a smooth batter. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Heat the oven to 425°F. Divide the oil among a muffin tin and heat it until very hot.
  3. Pour in the batter, filling the cups about halfway.
  4. Bake about 20 to 25 minutes until puffed and brown.
  5. Do not open the door early.
  6. Serve at once with gravy.

Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Individual cups are the modern Sunday shape. A single tin is the older tray.
  • Toad in the hole adds sausages and is then not vegetarian.

Pairings and uses

  • Side dish
  • Supper
  • gravy
  • roast beef
  • mustard
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free
