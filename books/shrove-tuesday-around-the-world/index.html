English Shrove pancakes

Thin egg pancakes, lemon and sugar, made to clear the kitchen before Lent.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 thin pancakes

History

These are the thin English pancakes of Shrove Tuesday, the day before Lent. The batter uses the eggs, milk, and fat that a strict Lenten fast would set aside. Shrove Tuesday is one day, not a week, and it is not the French crepe holiday. In England the usual finish is lemon juice and sugar. Butter, jam, or a savory filling exist, but lemon and sugar is the plate this page records.

Cultural background

Pancake races and church bells are local Shrove customs in some English towns. The cake itself is older than those events and does not depend on them. A home cook only needs a pan and a toss they can afford to miss.

Why it tastes this way

The batter is loose with egg and milk, so it sets in a thin sheet instead of a fluffy stack. The surface browns in butter. Lemon cuts the fat, and sugar stays gritty for a moment on the hot cake. There is no baking powder, so the flavor is egg, milk, and browned butter rather than a soda tang.

How it is made

Whisk a thin batter of flour, egg, and milk. Cook one ladle at a time in a hot buttered pan, turning once, until both sides are spotted brown.

Authentic touch. Rest the batter for half an hour if you can. The first pancake is the test piece. Toss it or turn it with a spatula, then eat it while the next one cooks.

How it is used

On Shrove Tuesday, folded or rolled with lemon and sugar. They are a feast-day pudding more than a tall breakfast stack.

Field notes

  • Shrove Tuesday is the single day before Lent, not a butter week.
  • The first pancake is traditionally the cook's test, and it is still eaten.
  • Lemon and sugar, not syrup, is the usual English finish.

Ingredients

  • 100 g plain wheat flour
  • 2 large eggs
  • 300 ml whole milk
  • 1 pinch fine salt
  • 30 g unsalted butter, for the pan and for serving
  • 2 lemons, cut into wedges
  • 3 tbsp sugar

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour and salt. Beat in the eggs, then the milk, until the batter is as thin as light cream. Rest it for 20 minutes if you have the time.
  2. Heat a frying pan over medium-high heat and grease it with a little butter.
  3. Pour in a thin layer of batter and swirl. Cook until the top looks dry and the underside is golden, about 1 minute. Turn and cook the second side for about 30 seconds.
  4. Fold or roll onto a warm plate. Add a squeeze of lemon and a spoon of sugar. Repeat, buttering the pan lightly between pancakes.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Orange juice instead of lemon
  • A savory ham and cheese fold, which is a later meal and not the Shrove plate

Pairings and uses

  • Feast or fast day
  • Dessert
  • Breakfast
  • Lemon wedges and sugar
  • A little jam, if lemon is not on the table
  • Tea
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Welsh crempog

Welsh buttermilk griddle cakes, stacked with butter between the layers.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 small cakes

History

Crempog, plural crempogau, is the Welsh griddle cake. Buttermilk and bicarbonate are a common home batter, and yeasted overnight batters are also traditional. This page uses buttermilk. The cakes are stacked with butter between them, which is the serving that matters. Some families make a stack for a birthday, and some make crempog when eggs and fat are used up before Lent. Neither occasion is the only one.

Cultural background

The cake is Welsh-language food as well as a recipe. The country on the map is the United Kingdom. The culture is Welsh. A heavy pan or bakestone is the tool.

Why it tastes this way

Buttermilk and soda give a light tang and a soft crumb. Butter between hot cakes melts and salts each layer. The outside is only just browned. They taste less of sugar than a dessert crepe, unless you add jam.

How it is made

Flour, egg, buttermilk, melted butter, and baking soda are mixed to a pourable batter and cooked as small rounds, then stacked with butter.

Authentic touch. Stack them with a smear of butter on each hot cake, not only on the top one. A yeasted batter, left overnight, is the other honest version.

How it is used

In a buttered stack, at breakfast, tea, a birthday, or Shrove. Jam is optional.

Field notes

  • The plural is crempogau. The stack is buttered between the cakes.
  • Buttermilk versions and yeast versions both exist.
  • Some Welsh families stack them for a birthday.

Ingredients

  • 200 g plain wheat flour
  • 1/2 tsp baking soda
  • 1 pinch fine salt
  • 2 large eggs
  • 300 ml buttermilk
  • 25 g unsalted butter, melted, plus more for stacking and the pan

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, baking soda, and salt. Beat in the eggs, buttermilk, and melted butter until smooth.
  2. Grease a bakestone or frying pan over medium heat.
  3. Cook small ladlefuls until the top dulls, then turn and color the other side.
  4. Stack the cakes with butter between them. Serve the stack warm.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A yeasted crempog batter left to rise overnight
  • A little oatmeal in place of part of the wheat flour

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Feast or fast day
  • Dessert
  • Salted butter
  • Jam
  • Tea
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Irish boxty

Pan boxty: raw grated potato and mashed potato, fried as cakes.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 pan cakes

History

Boxty is an Irish potato cake. There are three forms: pan boxty, loaf boxty, and dumpling boxty. This page is only the pan cake. The batter mixes raw grated potato with mashed potato and flour, then the cakes are fried. Potatoes reached Irish kitchens and became a staple. The cake is one of the ways a raw potato was cooked without waiting for a roast. Flour means this recipe is not gluten-free.

Cultural background

Pan boxty is breakfast and supper food. It is associated with the north and the midlands as much as with any single county. This page does not assign it to one village.

Why it tastes this way

Raw potato brings starch and a clean flavor. Mash brings softness. Flour binds the two. Frying browns the starch. If you rinse all the starch off the grated potato, the cake falls apart and tastes watery. Salt is the seasoning. A crisp edge and a soft center is the point.

How it is made

Equal parts raw grated potato and mashed potato are mixed with flour, salt, and a little milk, then fried as flat cakes in butter or oil.

Authentic touch. Grate the raw potato and keep the liquid that settles into starch. Pour off the water and put the starch back. That paste helps the cake hold.

How it is used

Hot, with butter, eggs, or bacon. The cake on this page is vegetarian. Bacon is a serving choice, not part of the batter.

Field notes

  • Pan, loaf, and dumpling boxty are three methods. This is the pan cake.
  • The mix is raw grated potato plus mashed potato, not mash alone.
  • The starch from the grated potato helps bind the cake.

Ingredients

  • 250 g raw potatoes, peeled
  • 250 g cold mashed potato
  • 100 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tsp fine salt
  • 60 ml milk
  • 30 g unsalted butter, for frying

Steps

  1. Grate the raw potatoes onto a clean cloth. Squeeze the liquid into a bowl. Let it sit so the starch settles, pour off the water, and keep the starch.
  2. Mix the squeezed potato, the starch, the mash, flour, salt, and enough milk to make a stiff batter.
  3. Heat the butter in a frying pan. Shape flat cakes and fry over medium heat until both sides are brown and the raw potato is cooked through, about 4 minutes a side.
  4. Rest them a minute and serve with butter or eggs.

The raw potato must cook through. Cut one cake open. The center should be soft and hot, not glassy.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Boxty loaf, baked as a bread, which is a different method
  • Boxty dumpling, boiled, which is also a different method

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • Butter
  • Fried eggs
  • Bacon, if you want a meat breakfast
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Scotch pancakes

Small thick drop scones from a Scottish girdle, butter and jam.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 16 small pancakes

History

Scotch pancakes, also called drop scones, are small thick cakes cooked on a girdle. They are not the thin English Shrove pancake and they are not a crepe. A modern batter uses baking powder or buttermilk and soda. Earlier dropped cakes relied more on eggs. This recipe uses baking powder and buttermilk because that is the home cake people mean by the name now. The country pin is the United Kingdom, and the culture is Scottish.

Cultural background

They are a tea-table and breakfast cake across Scotland. A heavy girdle, or a thick frying pan, is the tool. The cakes are eaten the day they are cooked.

Why it tastes this way

Buttermilk and a quick leavener make a soft, slightly tangy crumb. The girdle browns the outside before the inside dries. Sugar in the batter is light. Jam does the rest. Overmixing makes them tough, because the flour is wheat.

How it is made

Flour, egg, buttermilk, and baking powder are mixed briefly and dropped onto a lightly greased girdle.

Authentic touch. Drop the batter from a spoon. Do not spread it into a crepe. Turn when bubbles sit open on the top, and only once.

How it is used

Warm, with butter and jam, at breakfast or tea. They are not rolled.

Field notes

  • Drop scone is the other name. The cake is thick and small.
  • They are a different batter from English Shrove pancakes.
  • A girdle is a griddle. The spelling is the Scottish one.

Ingredients

  • 150 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tsp baking powder
  • 1 tbsp sugar
  • 1 pinch fine salt
  • 1 large egg
  • 180 ml buttermilk
  • 20 g unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the girdle

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, baking powder, sugar, and salt. Beat the egg with the buttermilk and melted butter.
  2. Stir the wet ingredients into the dry until just combined. A few lumps are fine.
  3. Grease a girdle or heavy pan over medium heat. Drop spoonfuls and cook until bubbles open on top.
  4. Turn once and cook until the centers spring back. Butter them and serve with jam.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of raisins in the batter
  • Thick cream instead of butter

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Snack
  • Butter
  • Raspberry jam or another jam
  • Tea
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Filloas

Thin Galician Carnival pancakes, scented with anise, rolled with sugar.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 10 filloas

History

Filloas are the thin pancakes of Galicia, eaten at Entroido, the Galician Carnival. They are a Carnival food, not the French Chandeleur crepe and not the English Shrove pancake, though all three sit in the late-winter calendar. Some Galician recipes use pork blood. This page writes the milk, egg, and anise filloa, which is the home sweet pancake, and names the blood version only as a variation it does not cook. Frixuelos, from Asturias, are the neighboring Carnival pancake and a separate page.

Cultural background

Entroido is a public festival with its own pastries and costumes. Filloas are the home griddle part of that season. Anise is the scent many sweet filloas carry. Sugar is the filling.

Why it tastes this way

They taste like a thin crepe with a perfumed anise note, browned in butter. Sugar on the hot cake melts in patches. The anise should be a scent, not a mouthful of seeds. Milk and egg make the sheet tender. A blood filloa would taste minerally and savory. That is a different cake.

How it is made

A thin batter of flour, egg, milk, sugar, and a pinch of anise is cooked as crepes and rolled with sugar.

Authentic touch. Make them for Carnival and call the season Entroido. Keep this recipe as the sweet milk filloa. Do not confuse them with Asturian frixuelos, which have their own page.

How it is used

Rolled with sugar, during Carnival, as a sweet plate.

Field notes

  • Filloas belong to Galician Entroido, Carnival.
  • This recipe is the sweet milk filloa. Blood filloas exist and are not written here.
  • Asturian frixuelos are a related Carnival pancake with a separate page.

Ingredients

  • 150 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tbsp sugar, plus more for serving
  • 1 pinch fine salt
  • 1/4 tsp ground anise
  • 2 large eggs
  • 350 ml whole milk
  • 30 g unsalted butter, for the pan

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, sugar, salt, anise, eggs, and milk into a thin batter. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Cook thin pancakes in a buttered pan until spotted on both sides.
  3. Sprinkle with sugar.
  4. Roll and serve during Carnival, or whenever you want the same cake.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Filloas de sangue, made with pork blood, a different and non-vegetarian recipe this page does not write
  • Frixuelos, the Asturian Carnival pancake

Pairings and uses

  • Feast or fast day
  • Dessert
  • Sugar
  • Honey, which adds a honey allergen
  • A lemon squeeze
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Frixuelos

Asturian Carnival pancakes, a little richer than Galician filloas, with lemon.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 pancakes

History

Frixuelos are the Carnival pancakes of Asturias, thin egg crepes related to Galician filloas and not the same recipe. This batter uses lemon zest and a little extra egg, and it is finished with sugar or honey. Carnival is the season. They are not Entroido filloas, not Spanish tortitas, and not a tortilla de patatas. Honey, if you choose it at the table, is a honey allergen. The written ingredients stop at sugar.

Cultural background

Asturian Carnival has its own fried pastries. Frixuelos are the griddled ones, made at home and eaten as they come off the pan. They are a seasonal dessert that people also cook later in the year.

Why it tastes this way

More egg makes the sheet richer and more yellow than the anise filloa. Lemon zest brightens the butter. Sugar on top stays crystalline for a moment. They brown in spots and stay flexible. The flavor is lemon and egg, not anise.

How it is made

A thin batter with lemon zest is cooked as crepes and folded with sugar.

Authentic touch. Keep them distinct from filloas: lemon here, anise on the Galician page. Cook them thin. Sugar is the default finish. Honey is a choice, not a requirement.

How it is used

During Asturian Carnival, folded with sugar, as a sweet plate.

Field notes

  • Frixuelos are the Asturian Carnival pancake.
  • They are related to Galician filloas and they are not the same batter.
  • This page uses lemon. The filloa page uses anise.

Ingredients

  • 125 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tbsp sugar, plus more for serving
  • 1 pinch fine salt
  • 3 large eggs
  • 300 ml whole milk
  • 1 tsp lemon zest
  • 30 g unsalted butter, for the pan

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, sugar, salt, eggs, milk, and lemon zest into a thin batter. Rest 10 minutes.
  2. Cook thin pancakes in butter until both sides are golden in spots.
  3. Sprinkle with sugar and fold.
  4. Serve warm.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A splash of apple brandy in the batter
  • Galician filloas, the anise Carnival pancake

Pairings and uses

  • Feast or fast day
  • Dessert
  • Sugar
  • Honey, which adds a honey allergen
  • A glass of milk
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Breton crepes

Thin wheat crepes from Brittany, butter and sugar, the cake of Chandeleur.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 10 crepes

History

The French crepe day is Chandeleur, Candlemas, on 2 February. It is not Mardi Gras, and it is not Shrove Tuesday. Breton wheat crepes are the sweet crepe of Brittany, usually eaten with butter and sugar or a spoon of jam. The savory buckwheat cake is a galette and has its own page. A custom at Chandeleur is to flip a crepe with one hand while holding a coin in the other, as a wish for the year. The wish is not a cooking step.

Cultural background

Crepes are street food and home food in Brittany, cooked on a flat billig. Cider is the regional drink beside them. The wheat crepe is the one for sugar. Buckwheat is the one for ham and cheese.

Why it tastes this way

A thin batter browns in spots and stays flexible. Butter on the crepe, then sugar, makes a sauce as the sugar melts. The egg keeps it tender. Resting the batter hydrates the flour so the sheet does not tear. The flavor is toasted milk and butter, not spice.

How it is made

Flour, egg, milk, water, salt, and melted butter are whisked into a thin batter, rested, and cooked in a very thin layer.

Authentic touch. Say Chandeleur, 2 February, when you mean the French crepe day. Do not call this a Mardi Gras recipe. Spread the batter with a wooden T or swirl the pan. The first crepe still counts as the test.

How it is used

Folded with butter and sugar, or with jam. At a creperie they are the sweet side of the menu. The savory side is the galette.

Field notes

  • Chandeleur, Candlemas, is 2 February. It is the French crepe day, not Mardi Gras.
  • In Brittany the wheat crepe is usually sweet. The buckwheat galette is usually savory.
  • Holding a coin while you flip the crepe is a Chandeleur custom, not part of the batter.

Ingredients

  • 125 g plain wheat flour
  • 2 large eggs
  • 250 ml whole milk
  • 100 ml water
  • 1 pinch fine salt
  • 30 g unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the pan and for serving
  • 3 tbsp sugar, for serving

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, eggs, milk, water, salt, and melted butter into a smooth thin batter. Rest 20 minutes.
  2. Heat a crepe pan and brush it with butter. Pour a thin layer and swirl.
  3. Cook until the edge lifts and the underside is spotted, then turn and cook the second side for a few seconds.
  4. Brush with butter, sprinkle with sugar, and fold. Repeat with the rest of the batter.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A squeeze of lemon
  • Normandy apple crepes, which are the filled apple version

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Feast or fast day
  • Snack
  • Butter and sugar
  • Jam
  • Dry cider
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Russian blini

Yeasted wheat blini for Maslenitsa, butter week, with sour cream and butter.

1 hr 40 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 4 · 12 blini

History

These are yeasted wheat blini from Russia. Maslenitsa is butter week, the week before Lent, not a single Tuesday. Blini are cooked through that week and also outside it. Buckwheat blini are traditional too and have their own page. This page is the wheat batter, the one most tables mean when they pile on butter and sour cream. A popular image treats the round blin as a sun at the end of winter. That is a popular reading. The kitchen facts are yeast, butter, and a week of cooking.

Cultural background

Maslenitsa food is rich in butter and dairy before the fast. Blini are breakfast, snack, and feast food in that week. Caviar and smoked fish are festive toppings. Sour cream and butter are the everyday ones, and they are what this recipe requires.

Why it tastes this way

Yeast gives a mild sourness and a softer crumb than an unleavened crepe. Butter in the pan and on the cake is the point of butter week. Sour cream adds tang and fat. The cakes are a little thicker than a French crepe and full of small holes. Sugar in the batter is only a pinch, so the topping does the sweetening if you want it.

How it is made

A yeast batter of wheat flour, milk, egg, and butter rises until bubbly, then is cooked in a buttered pan and stacked with more butter.

Authentic touch. Cook them through butter week if you are keeping Maslenitsa, and do not call that week a Tuesday. Brush each blin with butter as it comes off the pan. Keep them covered so they steam and stay flexible.

How it is used

Hot, with butter and sour cream. Jam, herring, or smoked fish are toppings. Fish would add a fish allergen and is not in this batter.

Field notes

  • Maslenitsa is butter week before Lent, not one Tuesday.
  • Wheat blini and buckwheat blini both exist. This page is the wheat yeast batter.
  • The sun story is a popular reading. The recipe is yeast and butter.

Ingredients

  • 250 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tsp instant yeast
  • 1 tsp sugar
  • 300 ml warm milk
  • 1 large egg
  • 1/2 tsp fine salt
  • 40 g unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the pan and for brushing
  • Sour cream, for serving

Steps

  1. Stir the flour, yeast, sugar, warm milk, egg, salt, and melted butter into a batter like cream. Cover until bubbly and risen, about 1 hour.
  2. Stir it down. Add a splash of milk if it is too thick to spread.
  3. Cook small rounds in a buttered pan until the top is set and full of holes, then turn and brown the other side.
  4. Brush each blin with butter and stack them. Serve with sour cream.

The batter should smell yeasty. Discard it if it smells spoiled.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of buckwheat in the batter, still not the buckwheat page
  • A thin unleavened blinchik, which is the filled crepe page

Pairings and uses

  • Feast or fast day
  • Breakfast
  • Savory meal
  • Melted butter
  • Sour cream
  • Jam, or herring for a fish plate
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Poland · Eastern Europe

Polish naleśniki

Thin Polish crepes with a sweet cheese filling, rolled and warmed.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 filled crepes

History

Naleśniki are Poland's thin wheat crepes, eaten sweet or savory. This page writes the sweet farmer-cheese filling, a home dessert and a weekend breakfast. They are not placki ziemniaczane, the potato pancakes, and they are not the Ashkenazi latke. The batter is the usual egg, milk, and flour crepe. Jam alone is an honest simpler serving and is listed as a variation.

Cultural background

They are made in ordinary kitchens, rolled, and sometimes given a minute in butter so the outside browns. Sweet cheese, jam, and fruit are the fillings people expect. There is no single feast day that owns them.

Why it tastes this way

The crepe is tender and a little sweet. Farmer cheese with sugar and vanilla tastes clean and slightly tart. A light fry browns the roll without drying the cheese. They should not be thick. A heavy pancake will not roll around this filling.

How it is made

Thin crepes are filled with sweetened farmer cheese, rolled, and warmed in butter.

Authentic touch. Cook the crepes thin and fill them while they are still flexible. Vanilla and lemon zest in the cheese are enough. Do not turn this into a potato placki.

How it is used

As dessert or a sweet breakfast, with a spoon of sour cream if you want it.

Field notes

  • Naleśniki are thin crepes. Placki are the potato pancakes.
  • Sweet cheese is a standard home filling.
  • They are everyday food, not a single feast-day cake.

Ingredients

  • 130 g plain wheat flour
  • 2 large eggs
  • 300 ml whole milk
  • 1 tbsp sugar, plus 2 tbsp for the filling
  • 1 pinch fine salt
  • 30 g unsalted butter, for the pan
  • 300 g farmer cheese, drained
  • 1 tsp vanilla sugar or 1/2 tsp vanilla extract
  • 1 tsp lemon zest

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, eggs, milk, 1 tbsp sugar, and salt. Rest 10 minutes and cook thin crepes in butter.
  2. Mix the farmer cheese with 2 tbsp sugar, the vanilla, and the lemon zest.
  3. Fill and roll the crepes.
  4. Warm the rolls in a little butter until the outsides color. Serve with sour cream or extra sugar.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Jam instead of cheese
  • A savory mushroom or spinach filling

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Breakfast
  • Sour cream
  • A dusting of sugar
  • Jam, if you skip the cheese
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Iceland · Northern Europe

Icelandic pönnukökur

Very thin Icelandic pancakes, rolled with sugar or rhubarb jam.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 10 thin pancakes

History

Pönnukökur are Iceland's very thin pancakes, rolled with sugar, jam, or whipped cream. Rhubarb jam is a common home filling because rhubarb grows well in Iceland. They are everyday and party food. This page does not assign them to Bolludagur, which is the day for cream buns, or to Sprengidagur, which is the day for salted meat and peas. Lummur, the thicker small cakes, are the next page.

Cultural background

A wide pan and a thin batter are the method. The cakes are stacked and rolled just before serving so they stay soft. They belong at birthdays and at ordinary tables.

Why it tastes this way

They are thin enough that butter and sugar dominate. A little vanilla or cardamom in some homes perfumes them. This recipe keeps a pinch of sugar and a neutral batter so the jam shows. Rhubarb is sharp. The edge should be spotted, not stiff.

How it is made

A thin milk and egg batter is cooked like crepes and rolled with sugar or jam.

Authentic touch. Roll them thin enough to see the pan color through the batter as it sets. Sugar alone is an honest filling. Rhubarb jam is the other one this page recommends.

How it is used

Rolled, as a snack or dessert, with coffee. They are not a thick breakfast stack.

Field notes

  • Pönnukökur are very thin and usually rolled.
  • Rhubarb jam is a common Icelandic filling.
  • They are not the same cake as lummur, and they are not the cream buns of Bolludagur.

Ingredients

  • 150 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tbsp sugar, plus more for serving
  • 1 pinch fine salt
  • 2 large eggs
  • 400 ml whole milk
  • 30 g unsalted butter, melted, plus more for the pan
  • Rhubarb jam or another jam, for serving

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, sugar, salt, eggs, milk, and melted butter into a thin batter. Rest 10 minutes.
  2. Cook very thin pancakes in a buttered pan, turning once.
  3. Sprinkle with sugar or spread with jam.
  4. Roll and serve.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A pinch of cardamom in the batter
  • Lummur, the thicker rye-tinged cakes on the next page

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Snack
  • Sugar
  • Rhubarb jam
  • Whipped cream
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Danish pandekager

Thin Danish pancakes, rolled with sugar or jam.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 8 pancakes

History

Danish pandekager are thin wheat pancakes, rolled with sugar, jam, or ice cream. They are everyday food and also appear around Fastelavn, the Danish carnival before Lent. Fastelavn is not only a pancake day. The cream-filled fastelavnsboller are the buns of that festival. This page does not treat the pancake as the whole of Fastelavn. Aebleskiver, the molded round cakes, are a different page.

Cultural background

A Danish home pancake is closer to a crepe than to a thick American stack. Sugar on the hot cake, then a roll, is the simplest serving. Jam is just as common.

Why it tastes this way

Egg and milk make a soft, lightly sweet sheet. Browning in butter adds the toasted note. Sugar crystals melt on contact. A tart jam keeps the roll from being only sweet. They are thin, so the surface area is the flavor.

How it is made

A thin batter of flour, egg, milk, sugar, and salt is cooked in butter and rolled with sugar or jam.

Authentic touch. Roll them while hot so the sugar sticks. A little sugar in the batter is enough. Do not confuse these with aebleskiver, which need a molded pan.

How it is used

Rolled, as dessert, breakfast, or a Fastelavn table among other foods.

Field notes

  • Pandekager are thin and rolled. Aebleskiver are the round molded cakes.
  • Fastelavn includes these pancakes and, famously, the filled buns. The buns are not this recipe.
  • Sugar or jam is the usual filling.

Ingredients

  • 150 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tbsp sugar, plus more for serving
  • 1 pinch fine salt
  • 2 large eggs
  • 350 ml whole milk
  • 30 g unsalted butter, for the pan
  • Jam, for serving

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, sugar, salt, eggs, and milk into a thin batter. Rest 10 minutes.
  2. Cook thin pancakes in a buttered pan until spotted on both sides.
  3. Sprinkle with sugar or spread with jam.
  4. Roll and serve warm.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A squeeze of lemon
  • Aebleskiver, the molded cake on its own page

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Breakfast
  • Feast or fast day
  • Sugar
  • Jam
  • Vanilla ice cream, for a dessert plate
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Swedish pannkakor

Thin Swedish pancakes, pan-cooked, served with lingonberry jam.

15 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 10 pancakes

History

Swedish pannkakor are thin pancakes, cooked in a pan or, in another method, baked in the oven. This page is the pan cake. Thursday yellow pea soup followed by pancakes is a living Swedish habit. A common explanation ties that Thursday plate to a Friday fast. Treat the explanation as common, not settled. The thin pancake also traveled with Swedish immigrants to the American Upper Midwest, where it has a separate journal page. This recipe stays in Sweden. Whipped cream and lingonberry jam are the usual finish.

Cultural background

Pannkakor are home food and school-lunch food. They are dessert after soup more often than they are a tall breakfast stack. Plättar, the tiny pan cakes, are the next page.

Why it tastes this way

A thin egg batter browns in butter and tastes of milk and a little salt. Lingonberry jam is tart, which keeps the butter from feeling heavy. Sugar in the batter is modest. The cake should bend. A thick, dry pancake is a different recipe.

How it is made

Flour, egg, milk, salt, and a little sugar are whisked thin and cooked as crepes in butter.

Authentic touch. Serve lingonberry jam, or another tart jam if you cannot find lingonberries. Do not turn this into the Upper Midwest page. If you bake the batter in a dish, you have started the oven variation, not the pan cake written here.

How it is used

Rolled or folded, with jam and sometimes whipped cream, often after pea soup on Thursdays.

Field notes

  • Thursday pea soup and pancakes are a Swedish habit. The fast-day explanation is not settled.
  • The Upper Midwest Swedish pancake is a different page. This one stays in Sweden.
  • Plättar are the tiny ones, cooked in a special pan.

Ingredients

  • 150 g plain wheat flour
  • 1 tbsp sugar
  • 1/2 tsp fine salt
  • 3 large eggs
  • 500 ml whole milk
  • 30 g unsalted butter, for the pan
  • Lingonberry jam, for serving

Steps

  1. Whisk the flour, sugar, and salt. Beat in the eggs and milk until the batter is thin and smooth. Rest 15 minutes.
  2. Heat a pan and grease it with butter.
  3. Cook thin pancakes until spotted brown on both sides.
  4. Serve with lingonberry jam, rolled or folded.

Refrigerate leftovers and reheat on a dry skillet. Egg or dairy batter should not sit at room temperature. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Ugnspannkaka, the oven pancake, often with pork, which is a different method and a meat dish
  • A savory fold of cheese

Pairings and uses

  • Dessert
  • Feast or fast day
  • Lingonberry jam
  • Whipped cream
  • Yellow pea soup, if this is the Thursday plate
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free
